Chapter Twelve. The Pattern Revealed.

One hundred nine hertz. Kansas City.


The night before Prague, Nadia cut Blake's hair.

It wasn't planned. They were in his apartment. The first time she'd been back since the early days. She'd come to go over the Strahov Library floor plans. Professional reasons. Operational necessity.

But Blake's hair had gotten long. Weeks of neglect while he chased patterns through caves and warehouses. It fell across his forehead, and when Nadia reached to push it back while pointing at a floor plan, her fingers caught in it and they both went still.

"You need a haircut," she said.

"I know."

"Sit down."

He sat in the kitchen chair. She found scissors in a drawer. Stood behind him. Her fingers in his hair, gentle, methodical, the same precision she brought to manuscript pages. The scissors were kitchen shears. Too large for the job. She used them with the adaptation of someone accustomed to working with imperfect tools. The Keeper life. Making do.

Neither of them spoke for a while.

Outside, October was doing what October does in Kansas City. The radiance going early, the air carrying sudden cold. Blake's sensitivity wanted to parse the streetlights into angular relationships. He let the information go. The world. For the space of that act. Was the right size.

"I'm scared," she said.

Blake didn't move. Let her say it to the back of his head, where it was easier.

"Not of Prague. I know Prague." Her fingers kept working. "I'm scared of what happens when you see the complete system. You're already seeing things I can't see. In Prague, with the concentrated patterns. You'll go further. You'll become more." Her voice dropped. "More of what you are. Less of what we are."

Blake closed his eyes. She was right. The expanding perceptual horizon was taking him further from the people who couldn't see. From Andrew, who compensated with technology. From Nadia, who compensated with scholarship. From every human connection that depended on shared experience.

"I don't know how to stop it," he said.

"I'm not asking you to stop it." The scissors resumed. "I'm asking you to come back from it. Every time. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Don't promise easy. Promise hard. Promise knowing it will cost you something. Promise knowing that coming back means choosing the limited world over the unlimited one, choosing -" She stopped. "Choosing me. Over the patterns. Even when the patterns are more beautiful."

"You're more beautiful," Blake said.

Nadia's hand was still on his head. She laughed. Small, surprised, something more vulnerable than the kitchen-floor laugh. "That's a terrible line."

"It's not a line."

"I know." Her hand moved to the side of his face. Fingertips on his temple. His jaw. "That's what makes it scary."

She finished the haircut. Brushed the clippings from his shoulders. He turned in the chair.

They were close. Kitchen chair close. Neither of them in danger. The closeness was chosen.

"I can't," she said. "Not tonight. Not before Prague. If we, and then something happens, I need to be able to function." She paused. "And the vows. They're not nothing, Blake. They're a choice I made when I was twenty-one, and that choice means something to me even when -"

"I know."

"Even when I want -"

She didn't finish. Blake put his hand over hers on his jaw. The same gesture from the warehouse. His hand covering hers. But this time on his skin. This time with her fingers on his face and the distance between them measured in millimeters.

"Prague first," he said. "Then we figure this out."

"Okay. Prague first."

She removed her hand. Stepped back. At the door she turned back.

"Blake -" She stood in the doorway, bag over her shoulder, scissors still in her hand because she'd forgotten to put them down. The domestic absurdity of it. Standing in a doorway holding scissors after cutting the hair of a man who could see the mathematical structure of the universe. Made something in Blake's chest ache.

"Thank you for the haircut," he said.

She looked down at the scissors. Laughed. Set them on the hall table. "You're welcome."

Then she was gone.



Blake opened William's envelope.

The letter was dated March two thousand three, one month before his grandfather's death.

Blake -

If you are reading this, you have chosen to finish what I could not. I am both proud and terrified.

The chamber beneath Strahov contains what Rudolf the Second spent his life gathering. I saw it in 1965. I documented it. And then I sealed it again and came home. I chose silence.

I told myself I was protecting you, protecting your father. And perhaps I was. But I was also protecting myself, from the responsibility of the choice I would have to make if I pursued the truth completely.

That silence killed me. Every day for forty years.

Do not make my mistake. If you have chosen this path, walk it fully. Use the key. Open the chamber. And whatever you find, share it. Make it impossible to suppress.

The Custodians will try to stop you. They killed your father. They will kill you too, if you give them the chance.

But you have something I never had: the ability itself. You can see the patterns, not just theorize about them.

And Blake, do not die with regret. Whatever you choose, choose it completely.

The chamber is waiting. I was not brave enough to open it. But you are.

With love and hope,

Your grandfather

Blake read the letter three times. Then he set it down on the kitchen table where Nadia had spread floor plans, where his hair clippings still dusted the floor, where three Morning Coffees had tracked the arc of something that hadn't existed a month ago.

He pulled out the three keys. Laid them on the table.

William's Oscar-01 key, the beginning. James's key. The middle. The Strahov key. The destination.

Three generations. Three keys. Each one opened by the failure of the last.

Except Blake had already decided. Not silence. Not reckless solitary pursuit. The third option: collaborative revelation. Truth shared, documented, distributed, impossible to suppress.

And coming back. Every time. No matter how far the motifs took him. Coming back to the limited world, the world where a woman could cut your hair in a kitchen and leave with scissors she'd forgotten to put down.

Blake texted Nadia: Read the letter. He says be brave. You already are.

A long pause. Then: So are you. Get some sleep. Early flight.

Another pause. Then: The haircut looks good.

Blake smiled. Set down the phone. Turned off the lights.

In the dark, the sequences sang. But Blake found a different resonance. The one he'd calibrated to in the SubTropolis vault, when a thousand years of encoded knowledge tried to pull him under and he'd found her hand first.

He sustained that vibration.

And slept.
