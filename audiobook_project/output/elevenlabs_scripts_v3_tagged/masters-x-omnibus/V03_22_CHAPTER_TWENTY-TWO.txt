Chapter Twenty-Two. Blake's Sabbath.

One oh seven point three hertz. Kansas City.

[short pause]

He stopped.

The decision was not dramatic. It was not announced. He did not tell Nadia or Marcus or the twelve or the Foundation board. He simply, on a Tuesday morning in October, did not go to UMKC. Did not enter Room 204. Did not sit in the thirteenth chair. Did not teach.

He sat on the Quality Hill balcony.

The balcony was old, the original balcony, the balcony his grandfather built, the wood softened by eighty years of Kansas City weather, the railings worn smooth by the hands of three generations of Masters men who had leaned on them and looked at the street and thought about whatever Masters men think about when they lean on balconies and look at streets. The wood had a frequency. Everything had a frequency. Blake could hear it, a low creak, approximately thirty hertz, the sound of old lumber flexing under gravity and temperature change.

He did not identify the frequency. He heard it. He let it be wood.

[pause]

Day one. He walked to Price Chopper at the end of the block. The automatic doors opened with a pneumatic sigh, two hundred hertz, the hiss of compressed air, the sound that every American hears a thousand times a year and never once considers remarkable. The fluorescent lights buzzed the same one hundred twenty hertz as every institutional fluorescent light he had ever stood under. The refrigeration units hummed at sixty hertz. The Muzak, a saxophone arrangement of something that had once been a song, played at a volume designed to be unnoticed.

He noticed everything. He had been noticing everything since the demonstration, but noticing in a cave or a laboratory or even a classroom was different from noticing in a grocery store. In a cave, noticing was the work. In a grocery store, noticing was an interruption. The frequency of the building was not the point. The point was eggs.

He bought eggs. Bread. The Norton wine, the same Missouri Norton he had served Nadia on their first night in this apartment, the grape that nobody respected and that Blake had always preferred because preference was its own kind of frequency. Coffee. Whole milk, which Nadia did not drink but which Blake's body had been craving since the tremor settled, the body asking for calcium the way a tuning fork asks for a solid surface to amplify through.

He paid in cash. The register beeped. two thousand two hundred hertz, the checkout frequency, identical in every grocery store in America, the sound of a transaction, the sound of ordinary economic exchange, the sound that every person alive in the modern world has heard so many times that it has become silence.

He carried the bags home. The paper bags, he had asked for paper, the way his grandfather had asked for paper, the way every Masters man had asked for paper because paper made a sound when you carried it and plastic did not, and the sound of paper bags against a man's chest is approximately five hundred hertz, the rustle of cellulose fiber against cotton shirt, the sound of a body carrying what it needs.

The walk was six blocks. He heard all six.

[pause]

Day four. He fixed the balcony railing. The wood had separated from the post, eighty years of freeze-thaw cycles, Kansas City's particular talent for seasonal violence. He drove to the hardware store. Bought wood screws, a tube of construction adhesive, a new piece of white oak that the clerk cut to 36 inches.

He worked with his hands. His trembling hands. The screwdriver shook in his grip, the tremor making the Phillips head chatter against the screw slot with a faint metallic clicking, one hundred eleven hertz tapped out on steel, the frequency conducting through the tool into the wood into the building. He was shaking the building. Very slightly. Not enough to feel unless you were the building, unless you had limestone foundations that resonated at one hundred nine hertz and were now receiving an additional input at 111 and producing a composite wave that nobody would ever measure because nobody measures the frequency of a man fixing a balcony rail.

The screw went in. The railing held. He tested it, leaned, pressed, the weight of a man who had lost twenty-nine pounds during the demonstration and gained back fourteen. The railing creaked but held.

He stood there, his hand on the new wood, feeling the adhesive cure beneath his palm, the particular warmth of construction adhesive as it cross-links and hardens, an exothermic reaction, chemistry producing heat, the same principle as Blake's body producing frequency: molecules finding their arrangement and releasing energy in the process.

Day seven. Nadia came home early. He heard her car, the particular engine note of her Volvo XC60, the turbo-four producing approximately eighty-five hertz at idle, a sound he had been hearing for eight years and that he now recognized the way dogs recognize a particular car in a line of traffic, by the specific acoustic signature that no two engines share.

She found him on the balcony. Norton wine. The afternoon light through the elms.

"You're not teaching," she said.

"No."

"Marcus covered your class."

"I know."

She sat down. Not in the other chair, on the balcony rail, the rail he had fixed three days ago, her weight testing the new screw and the construction adhesive and the white oak, the rail holding, the way things hold when they have been repaired with attention.

"How long?"

"I don't know."

She looked at him for a long time. The particular quality of Nadia's looking, clinical and warm simultaneously, the gaze of a woman who had spent a decade measuring things and who had learned that measurement was not control, that knowing the number did not mean you could change it, that the frequency of a man sitting on a balcony drinking wine was not a problem to be solved but a system in the process of finding its rest state.

"Okay," she said. And went inside.

[pause]

Day fifteen. The balcony. Blake thought of Site Echo.

The sphere at the bottom of the lake in the Ozarks, ninety feet below the surface his grandfather had photographed and never returned to. The chamber that no map recorded. The carvings that no living person had read. He had told Nadia, the morning he climbed out of the water with the regulator still in his teeth, that he would go back. He had taken thirty-seven photographs and four minutes of video. He had measured the sphere with a tape measure he had brought specifically for the purpose. He had been twenty-eight years old and had believed that what was down there was waiting for him.

Fourteen years later, on a balcony in October with a glass of Norton in his trembling hand, Blake understood that the sphere had not been waiting for him. The sphere had been there before him. It would be there after him. The chamber's silence was not an invitation; it was a fact. Some chambers are not yours to open. Some carvings are not yours to read. The frequency the sphere was holding had survived the millennia between its making and its discovery, and it would survive the millennia after his death without asking for his attention. It did not require his hands. It did not require his eyes. It did not require completion.

Brother Aldric had scattered seven satchels across continents to delay this exact appetite. Six of them were still in the world: Paris, Rome, Constantinople, Toledo, Uppsala, and the one the Watcher had taken to the place humans cannot go. Blake suspected the seventh was at the bottom of an Ozark lake, but he was no longer the man who needed to confirm it. The frequency was not the satchels. The frequency had never been the satchels. The frequency was what Mara Reeves had heard in a folding chair in Room 204 the moment she had stopped trying.

The sphere would stay where it was. He would not go back.

He took a sip of the wine. He did not write this down.

[pause]

The tremor slowed.

He noticed it on the third day. Not dramatically, not the cessation that had happened in the eighth chamber. This was subtler. A softening. The one eleven point two hertz baseline, which his body had maintained since the restructuring, began to drift. Downward. Slowly.

Day three: 111. Day five: 110.5. Day eight: 110. Day twelve: 109, the frequency of the building's own limestone foundation, as if his body and the building were synchronizing, two resonance chambers sharing a geological substrate. Day eighteen: 108, the frequency Marcus's students had been producing in Room 204, the classroom frequency, the frequency of twelve people sitting in chairs and breathing together. Day twenty-four: 107.5.

By day thirty: one oh seven point three hertz.

one oh seven point three hertz. Below the Strahov specification. Below the preparation protocol's target frequency. Below the measurement that he had chased across continents and that the caves had produced and that his body had internalized at the cost of twenty-nine pounds and a permanent bilateral tremor.

His body was choosing its own frequency.

Not the specification. Not the measurement. Not the number that the manuscripts prescribed or the caves produced or the instruments confirmed. A frequency that belonged to no text, no tradition, no grimoire. A frequency that belonged to Blake Masters' body, this particular body, at this particular age, at this particular weight, sitting on this particular balcony drinking this particular wine.

[pause]

Day twenty-two. Marcus came for dinner. The kitchen table, the same table where every significant moment in the trilogy's domestic register had occurred: the Norton wine, the Monday calls, the morning coffee, the Nadia/Blake geography of sugar and salt and the east window's moving light.

Nadia made pasta. Not institution food, not Foundation food, not the takeout that had sustained them through the NIH study and the congressional letters and the Vance lawsuit. Pasta. Garlic, olive oil, the kind of cooking that requires attention but not intelligence, the kitchen task that lets three people talk about things that matter while their hands do things that don't.

Marcus brought a bottle of wine. Not Norton, a California Pinot that he'd been saving for an occasion he hadn't defined.

"Your students miss you," he said.

"How are they?"

"Perceiving. Three of them now report continuous low-frequency awareness, the building hum, the traffic patterns, the overtone series in conversation. They don't need you. They need each other."

Blake poured the wine. His hand trembled at one hundred eight hertz, down from this morning's 108.3, the fractional drift that he had stopped measuring because measuring was the thing he was learning to stop doing.

"That's the answer, isn't it?" he said.

"To what?"

"To all of it. The cave. The manuscripts. The seven-century preparation protocol. The answer is: you don't need any of it. You just need someone to sit next to."

Marcus held the wine. He had his way of holding things, precise, final. The synesthesia was gone. The colors were gone. But the precision remained, the particular attention of a man who had once seen colors attached to every sound and who now saw only sound and who understood that seeing only sound was not a loss but a simplification, the way a painting stripped of its frame reveals its actual edges.

"I know," Marcus said. "My grandmother knew. Every grandmother knows." He wrote in the tenth Moleskine, the last one, the one he knew would be the last because the writing was slowing the way the tremor was slowing, the man and the vibration and the record all coming to rest simultaneously.

The frequency isn't one eleven point two hertz. The frequency is whatever your body makes when you stop trying.

He closed the Moleskine. Drank the wine. Watched the elms. The tremor, one oh seven point three hertz, his frequency, the frequency that no one had prescribed and no one would replicate because it belonged to one body in one city in one October, hummed in his hands, and the wine trembled in the glass, and the light moved across the balcony the way light moves across everything: without urgency, without schedule, without the institutional pressure to arrive on time.

For thirty days, Blake Masters did nothing. And in doing nothing, his body found the frequency it had been looking for since the Strahov crypt, which was the frequency of not looking.
