Chapter One. The Library Boy.

One hundred nine hertz. Kansas City.


The corner table at Miller Nichols Library had been Blake Masters' territory for three years.

Back to the wall. Clear sightline to both exits. Close enough to the radiator to stay warm in October, far enough to avoid the clanking when the old pipes kicked on. His grandfather had taught him to choose seats like this. Always know your exits. William Masters had flown reconnaissance aircraft over hostile airspace for twelve years, and the habits he'd brought home from the Cold War had filtered down through two generations like table manners: automatic, inherited, instinct rather than training.

Blake adjusted the Breitling Navitimer on his wrist and turned another page of Foucault's Pendulum. Fourth time through. Some obsessions weren't chosen. Some found you the way water finds cracks. Patiently, inevitably, following the path of least resistance until something gives.

He didn't notice the woman at the table three rows over. Not yet.

She was watching him.



The watch had been his grandfather's. A 1967 Breitling Navitimer, the kind pilots wore when pilots still navigated by the stars. William Masters had bought it the year he left Northrop, the year he stopped flying experimental aircraft and moved his family from California to Missouri for reasons nobody discussed.

Blake's father had worn it next. James Masters, electrical engineer, Missouri infrastructure division. He'd worn it until September twenty ten, when he'd stopped wearing anything at all.

Now Blake wore it. Too big for his wrist. Heavy with history.

What had his grandfather been running from?

Blake had spent five years of graduate school and one failed security career trying to answer that. All he had were fragments: the watch, some photographs, his father's memories of William taking him to tour McDonnell Douglas, and a growing certainty that somewhere between his grandfather's test flights and his father's nuclear security clearance, the Masters family had stumbled across something that didn't want to be found.

The woman three tables away shifted. Her eyes found his again, held for one beat too long, then slid away.

Blake felt the hair rise on the back of his neck.



She was good. Professional.

Peripheral glances every thirty seconds, never holding eye contact long enough to register as staring. Most people wouldn't have noticed.

Auburn hair, cut practical and short. Green eyes that held intelligence and something harder underneath. Caution, maybe. Or ancient grief. She wore a black turtleneck and no jewelry except a silver ring on her right hand. Her leather bag looked expensive but worn, the kind that had crossed borders and survived searches.

He cataloged all of this without appearing to look up from his book. What do you see? Tell me everything about them without looking directly. William's game.

What Blake saw now was something he hadn't expected. A tension in her shoulders that wasn't tactical. The way her fingers kept returning to the silver ring, turning it. And her eyes, when they found his again across thirty feet of library carpet, not just watching. Searching.

Blake felt the prickling sensation at the base of his skull. The same feeling he'd had at SubTropolis, right before they'd fired him. The same one his father had described in his journal, the week before the accident that wasn't an accident.

Something was about to change. A cardinal landed on the windowsill.

Male, brilliant red against the gray October afternoon. Blake stared at it, and for a moment he was nine years old again, watching California roll past beneath him at three thousand feet, his grandfather's hands steady on the yoke.

The summer of 1999. Blake's parents had wanted him to really know his grandfather. They'd sent him to California for three months, and William had picked him up at LAX with a hand-lettered sign that said BLAKE and a grin that promised adventure.

Their first flight had been over the coast. William had leveled off at twenty-five hundred feet, the Pacific stretching to the horizon.

"What do you see?"

Blake, nine years old and pressed against the window, had squinted at the white lines forming and reforming below. "Waves?"

"What about the waves?"

He'd looked harder. "They come in sets? Like one, two, three big ones, then smaller ones."

"Good. Now watch where they break. See how the wave height changes with the bottom contour? Shallow water, the wave stands up. Deep water, it rolls past. The ocean floor creates the pattern you see on the surface."

"Most people see the ocean and think it's chaos," William had said. "But there's no chaos in nature, Blake. Only patterns we don't understand yet."

The second flight had been different. Twenty minutes in, William had said the words Blake had been hoping to hear:

"Your airplane."

Blake's hands found the yoke. Suddenly he could feel the aircraft. Every shift in the air transmitted directly into his palms.

"Gentle pressure. You're not wrestling it. You're asking it to dance."

Blake nudged the yoke right and the world tilted. Mountains sliding beneath them, the horizon canting, his stomach floating.

"Now level it out. Feel when it wants to stop turning."

And Blake had felt it. That moment when the forces balanced and the world returned to horizontal. Not something William told him. Something his hands knew.

"You're a natural," William had said, and Blake had heard something beyond pride. Recognition.

They'd flown over the Mojave their third week. High desert, forty-five hundred feet. William pointed down at the dry riverbeds branching across the valley floor.

"What do you see?"

By now, Blake knew better than the obvious. "Arteries and veins. All branching out."

"The path of least resistance. But look closer. See how the angles repeat? The same branching pattern at different scales?"

Blake stared. The same angles, the same ratios, in channels a hundred feet wide and in tiny erosion patterns barely visible from altitude.

"A river does it," William continued. "Lightning does it. Blood vessels in the hand achieve it. Trees do it. That's not just least resistance. That's mathematics expressing itself through nature."

"Why does nature keep using the same pattern?"

"That is the question I've been asking my whole life."

Something in William's voice had made Blake look over. His grandfather was staring at the desert, but seeing something else entirely.

"When I flew test planes," William said quietly, "I saw things. Patterns in cloud formations that didn't match weather reports. Geometric shapes in rock formations too precise to be natural. Once, at fifty thousand feet, I watched a thunderstorm from above and saw..." He paused. "Something I couldn't explain."

"What was it?"

"I don't know. I took photographs, filed reports. The engineers said ice crystals. The meteorologists said lenticular clouds. But I knew what I saw."

Silence. Then: "Most people see the land and move on. But you. You see the pattern and want to understand. That curiosity. That's the beginning of wisdom."

"Will I ever understand? All of it?"

"No. Nobody understands all of it. But you'll see more than most. You'll ask better questions." Quiet. "That's not always a blessing, Blake. Sometimes the questions are heavier than the answers."

Blake hadn't understood. Nine years old, flying over the desert with his grandfather. He'd thought William meant academic questions.

He hadn't known his grandfather was preparing him for something else entirely.



The cardinal flew away. Blake blinked, surfacing from twenty-six years of memory.

The woman with auburn hair was gone.

She'd disappeared while he was three thousand feet above the Mojave. Which meant she was better than good.

She was very, very good.



The apartment was small. A studio in Westport, third floor walkup.

Three photographs hung on the wall. William at Northrop, young and confident, standing next to an aircraft blacked out with government markers. James at Oscar-01, 2009, shaking hands with an Air Force colonel. Blake himself in his G four S uniform, SubTropolis badge visible, a thick black X drawn through it with permanent marker.

Three generations of men who'd worked in places with secrets. Two of them dead.

Blake looked at the middle photograph the longest. His father's face in that picture was the public face, the handshake, the uniform collar, the professional smile aimed at someone holding a camera. But Blake remembered a different face. The one James wore on the back deck of the Jefferson City house on Thursday evenings, when he'd set up the soldering station on the picnic table and build shortwave radios from surplus parts.

Blake was thirteen. He'd come outside with his geometry homework and a glass of the terrible lemonade his mother made from powder because she refused to pay four dollars for lemons. James had a circuit board clamped in a vise, a thin curl of solder smoke rising between them.

"You're making that look harder than it is," James had said without looking up.

"It's proofs. They want me to prove a triangle is a triangle."

"That's not what they want. They want you to show your reasoning. Proofs aren't about the answer. They're about the path."

James had set down the iron. He smelled the way he always smelled on Thursday evenings, solder flux, coffee, the cedar soap he used because Margaret said the regular soap gave him a rash. The specific combination of a specific man on a specific evening, unreproducible, the kind of detail that disappeared from the world when the person did and could never be recovered from photographs or letters or the careful language of obituaries.

"Here." James had taken the pencil. Not to solve it, to draw a radio schematic beside the triangle. "You know what a circuit is?"

"Electricity going in a loop."

"Electricity following a path. The path is the proof. You don't prove the triangle exists. You prove why it has to exist. The angles don't just add up to 180 degrees. They can't not." He'd tapped the schematic. "Same with a circuit. Current doesn't choose to flow. The path makes it inevitable."

He'd grinned, the private grin, the one that wasn't for cameras. The one that made the skin around his eyes crease in a way that said he was delighted by the elegance of something, and that the delight was a gift he wanted to hand to his son the way you hand someone a glass of water. Here. This is good. Drink.

Blake had finished the proofs in twenty minutes. James had finished the radio. They'd sat on the deck after dark, tuning through shortwave frequencies, hearing weather reports from ships in the Gulf of Mexico and ham operators in Manitoba and, once, a burst of numbers in Russian that made James go quiet for a long time.

"Dad?"

"Just listening." James's hand on the dial, steady. "Some signals, you pick up by accident. Some are aimed at you." He'd looked at Blake with an expression that wasn't the grin anymore. Something older and sadder. "The trick is knowing the difference."

Blake hadn't understood. Thirteen years old, lemonade from powder, the cicadas starting in the hackberry tree. He'd thought his father was talking about radio.

The photograph on the wall showed none of this. It showed the handshake. The uniform. The professional distance that official photography required. It didn't show the solder smoke or the crease around his eyes or the particular way he'd handed Blake the pencil, handle first, the way you hand someone a knife, which was the way James did everything: considering the other person's grip before his own.

A wooden box sat on the shelf below. Oak, brass clasp oxidized to green. Inside: a copy of Don Quixote his grandfather had given him at twelve, the margins dense with handwritten notes Blake had never read carefully.

The phone rang.

Blake let it ring. He was thinking about the SD card hidden behind his medicine cabinet mirror. Thirty-seven photographs of geometric carvings in a section of SubTropolis that didn't appear on any official map. The section he'd been fired for entering.

The carvings shouldn't have existed. SubTropolis was a limestone mine converted to underground storage. The main facility dated to the 1960s. But the tunnel Blake had found went deeper. Into bedrock that predated the limestone. Into rock carved with patterns that made his eyes water when he looked at them too long. The same branching angles from William's Cessna. The same proportions, a hundred feet underground.

The same patterns. Different altitude.

The phone stopped ringing. Then started again.

Blake picked up.



"Mr. Blake Masters?" A woman's voice, professional. "Patricia Thornton, attorney with Pemberton and Associates in Jefferson City."

Jefferson City. His father's hometown.

"This concerns your grandfather's estate. There's been a development."

"My grandfather died in 2010. His estate was settled years ago."

"First National Bank recently underwent renovations. They discovered a safety deposit box not in their main system. Established in 1968. Rental paid through 2025. Fifty-seven years in advance."

Blake's hand tightened on the phone.

"The box lists only one authorized heir. Not your father. Not your mother. You."

1968. The year William stopped flying test planes and moved his family to Missouri and never explained why. He'd paid for a box fifty-seven years in advance, timed to arrive at just this moment.

Before Blake was delivered. Before his father was born. William had set this in motion, aimed at a future grandson he would never meet.

Which meant William had known. About the patterns. About everything that would happen to James, and everything that would happen to Blake.

"Mr. Masters? Are you still there?"

"Tomorrow," Blake said. "I'll be there tomorrow."



He didn't sleep.

Blake sat in the dark, looking at the photographs. William. James. Himself. Three men with the same eyes, the same jaw, the same curse of seeing things others walked past.

The third was about to open a box that had been waiting since before he was born.

Geometric motifs swam behind his closed eyes. The SubTropolis photographs. Circles within circles, triangles at impossible angles. Branching patterns like rivers, like lightning, like the Mojave washes from three thousand feet.

He'd spent four years telling himself they were erosion, water damage, the mind finding signal in noise. The same way his father had probably written off the anomalies at Oscar-01, the same way William had probably told himself what he saw from the cockpit were optical illusions.

But they weren't noise. They weren't illusions.

They were beautiful. And terrible. And deliberately carved by hands that understood something about geometry that modern mathematics had forgotten.

The question isn't whether they're there. The question is whether you're ready to see them.

Tomorrow. He would drive to Jefferson City. Open his grandfather's safety deposit box. Start finding answers to questions that had killed his father and driven his grandfather from the sky.

The Breitling caught the streetlight. A pilot's watch on a pilot's grandson, still ticking forward toward whatever William had planned for this moment.

Blake stood and walked to the window.

Whatever altitude they appeared at.
