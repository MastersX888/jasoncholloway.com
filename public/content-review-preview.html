The Frequency That Was Already There

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 1 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

Every page of the Distribution File carries the same footer: f = 111.2 Hz. It runs under the preparation protocols, under the chamber specifications, under the facilitator's manual — two hundred forty-seven pages, one stamp. Readers ask about it more than they ask about anything else in the trilogy. Where did the number come from? Is it real? Should I be listening to it?

The honest answer takes a whole essay, because the honest answer is: the number is fiction, but only by one decimal place. The stone underneath it was measured before I ever started writing.

What was actually measured

Archaeoacoustics is a real academic field, and a modest one. Its researchers record and analyze the acoustic behavior of ancient sites — the resonant frequencies, the reverberation, the way sound moves through a built space — to ask whether those properties were designed or incidental. The field's careful practitioners make careful claims, which is exactly why it is worth taking seriously.

The site everyone reaches for is the Ħal-Saflieni Hypogeum in Malta: an underground temple complex carved from limestone somewhere around 3600 to 2500 BCE, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site so fragile that only eighty visitors a day are admitted. In its Oracle Chamber — a roughly oval room under a curved limestone ceiling — researchers have documented a pronounced resonance around 110 to 111 Hz. A male voice chanting in that range produces an intense reverberant effect; the room answers back. The frequently cited 2008 study by Cook, Pajot, and Leuchter reported a shift in prefrontal activity in subjects exposed to sound at that resonance. It was one study, with a small sample. That caveat belongs in every sentence that cites it, and it appears in mine.

Malta is not alone. Paul Devereux and colleagues systematically measured resonances at Neolithic chambers across Britain and Ireland — chambered tombs, stone circles — and found peak frequencies consistently in the 95-to-120 Hz band. Aaron Watson and David Keating did the same at Camster Round in Scotland. Further back still, Iegor Reznikoff and Michel Dauvois observed that the Palaeolithic paintings at Lascaux and other French caves cluster disproportionately in the zones of highest acoustic resonance, and published the finding in 1988. Mainstream archaeology has received that interpretation with interest and caution in roughly equal measure.

I keep the full record — sites, studies, citations, and an honest FAQ — in the 111 Hz Field Note, which is the most complete public account of this research I could assemble. The short version: a recurring resonance near 110 Hz in ancient stone enclosures is documented. Whether anyone intended it is not.

Why the fiction adds a decimal

So why does the trilogy say 111.2 instead of 110?

Partly because the extra digit is honest in its own way. If I had written 110 Hz, I would have been borrowing the authority of the measured literature for claims the literature does not make. The archaeoacousticians measured a resonance; they did not measure Blake Masters' bilateral tremor, which the novels lock at exactly 111.2 Hz, or a preparation protocol, or a perception chamber. Moving one decimal past the real number is the fiction signing its own work. The number is close enough to honor its source and distinct enough that nobody can mistake my invention for their measurement.

And partly because precision is a character trait. William Masters is an engineer. A man who leaves seven leather notebooks for his grandson does not write "about 110." He writes 111.2, with the instrumentation to defend the second decimal. The number carries his personality the way handwriting does.

The whole ladder, graded

The trilogy's frequency system is a ladder, and every rung has a real referent — which is precisely why each one needs a grade.

At the bottom: 7.83 Hz, the Schumann resonance, the fundamental frequency of the cavity between the Earth's surface and the ionosphere. This is textbook geophysics — measured, no asterisk needed.

Next: 110 Hz, the archaeoacoustic band described above. Measured, with the honest caveat that the literature is thinner than a thriller needs it to be.

Then the trilogy's carrier: 111.2 Hz. Invented. Mine. One decimal past the stone.

And at the top: 136.6 Hz, which the novels connect to transcendence states. This rung comes from alternative tuning traditions and from the Monroe Institute's focus-level framework — a body of practice the U.S. Army once formally assessed in the Gateway Process document, which is its own strange story and its own essay. The grade here is contested at best: the traditions are real, documented practices; the claims made within them are not scientific findings.

The chapter headers in the trilogy map this ladder onto real places — the Kansas City limestone under SubTropolis, the Strahov crypt in Prague, Icelandic basalt, the Schumann fundamental. Every referent is documented. The claim that they form one coherent system is the novel. That sentence is the whole method of these essays, so let me set it off plainly: the rungs are real; the ladder is fiction.

Sound has shapes

There is one more piece of real science holding the frequency system up, and it is the most photogenic physics of the eighteenth century. In 1787, Ernst Chladni drew a violin bow along the edge of a sand-covered metal plate and showed that the sand migrates to the nodal lines — the places where the plate is still — forming geometric figures that change with the frequency. Hans Jenny extended the work in the 1960s with oscillators, liquids, and powders, and documented how narrowly the geometry depends on the tone: shift from 800 to 865 cycles per second and the pattern doesn't merely adjust, it transforms into a categorically different figure.

That sensitivity is what makes a precise frequency narratively load-bearing. In a universe where sound produces form, 111.2 is not interchangeable with 111.3. The Cymatics Field Note covers Chladni and Jenny in full, including where popular cymatics claims outrun the research — and the third essay in this series walks that seam at length.

Why label any of this

A reasonable reader might ask why a novelist bothers grading his own numbers. The fiction would work fine without the disclosure; thrillers borrow scientific authority all the time and nobody sues.

The answer is that the trilogy is about the danger of ungraded patterns. Umberto Eco's warning — that pattern-hunters end up manufacturing the pattern they seek — is quoted, structurally, in everything Blake Masters does. A book built on that warning cannot then hand its readers an unmarked mixture of measurement and invention and hope they sort it out. The two-register rule that governs the encyclopedia, the Field Notes, and these essays — in the world versus in the novels — is not a legal disclaimer. It is the ethic the story argues for, applied to the story itself.

So, one last time, the grades. The Schumann resonance: in the world. The 110 Hz chamber literature: in the world, thin but real. The 111.2 Hz carrier, the tremor, the protocol, the chamber: in the novels. The full apparatus — tables, sources, and every grade — arrives in the Masters X Universe Encyclopedia. Until then, the research layer is live in the Field Notes, and the books are at jasoncholloway.com.

The facts are in the files. The fiction is in the books.

Blog 02: The Grimoire That Was a Study Aid READY

The Grimoire That Was Actually a Study Aid

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 2 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

There is a moment in The Inheritance of Frequency when Nadia, sitting on a kitchen floor with a facsimile page between her hands, says the line the whole trilogy pivots on: "The Ars Notoria isn't magic. It's technology. Cognitive technology." Readers tend to assume I invented the manuscript she is holding. I did not. The Ars Notoria is real, it is catalogued, and you can request it at the British Library.

What I invented is narrower than that — and stranger for its narrowness. Let me walk the seam.

The manuscript on the record

The Ars Notoria — the Notory Art — belongs to the Solomonic tradition, the family of medieval texts attributed, at least nominally, to the biblical King Solomon. The earliest surviving manuscripts date to the mid-thirteenth century. Institutional copies survive across Europe, including British Library MS Sloane 1712 and holdings at the Bibliothèque nationale de France. Robert Turner translated it into English in 1657; modern scholarly editions and translations exist, and serious scholars — Claire Fanger and Julien Véronèse among them — have spent careers on it.

What the text promises is remarkable enough that no fiction writer would dare invent it from scratch: the rapid acquisition of the seven liberal arts — grammar, rhetoric, logic, arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy — through the structured contemplation of geometric figures called notae, combined with specific prayers, in a prescribed schedule of practice. Not wealth, not love, not power over demons. Knowledge, memory, and eloquence, on an accelerated timetable. It is, on its face, a medieval manual for learning faster.

The notae themselves are the heart of it. They are dense, elaborate diagrams — geometric structures carrying prayers in Latin, Hebrew, Greek, and occasionally scripts nobody has identified — meant to be gazed at in sequence while the accompanying orations are recited. As objects, they are unlike anything else in medieval book culture: not illustrations of a text but apparently instruments of it. The full account, with sources, is in the Ars Notoria Field Note.

Condemned as cheating

Here is the detail that tells you how seriously the medieval world took this book: it was condemned — by the Inquisition, and by no less an authority than Albertus Magnus. And the grounds of the condemnation are the interesting part. The core objection was not devil-worship. It was that the Ars Notoria promised an illicit shortcut — knowledge acquired without the sanctioned labor of study. It was condemned, in effect, as cheating.

Institutions rarely mobilize against texts they consider trivial. Nobody convenes a tribunal over a book that doesn't work and that nobody reads. The vehemence of the response suggests the manuscript was circulating widely and being taken seriously by exactly the people who could afford to commission copies — which is to say, by the educated class the universities were built to produce. Whether the readers were right to take it seriously is a separate question. That they did is documented.

How a banned book survives

There is a bibliographic subplot here worth a paragraph, because it shapes how the trilogy uses the text. Condemned books in the manuscript era did not disappear; they went expensive. Copies of the Ars Notoria continued to be produced for centuries after the condemnations — on good vellum, with the notae executed carefully, which means patrons kept paying scribes serious money for a text the authorities had ruled against. By 1657 it had crossed into print in English through Robert Turner's translation, and from there into the long tail of early-modern occult publishing that eventually delivered it to modern editors. A text survives seven hundred years of official disapproval only if a chain of individual readers, generation after generation, privately concluded it was worth the risk and the price. The trilogy's Keepers are fiction; the behavior they are modeled on — quiet, well-funded, multi-generational custodianship of a condemned text — is just the Ars Notoria's actual transmission history, read as a plot.

The pattern a modern reader can't unsee

Now the middle register — not measurement, not invention, but the observation that sits between them.

Strip away the angelic language and describe the Ars Notoria's method operationally: extended visual contemplation of complex structured figures; recitation on a fixed schedule; prescribed postures; repetition across weeks and months; a curriculum that sequences material from foundational to advanced. A cognitive scientist reading that description will recognize elaborative encoding, spaced repetition, and structured retrieval practice — the load-bearing findings of modern memory research — seven centuries before anyone had the vocabulary.

Does that mean medieval practitioners empirically discovered spaced repetition and encoded it as prayer? It might. It might equally be a coincidence of forms — ritual happens to be repetitive, and repetition happens to aid memory. The scholarly literature treats the resemblance with appropriate care, and so do I. But the resemblance is real, it is striking, and it is the documented foundation the fiction stands on. A grimoire that maps onto genuine learning science is a far better inheritance for a novel than a grimoire that maps onto nothing.

Where the fiction begins: efficacy

So here is the seam, stated as plainly as I can state it.

Real: the manuscript, the notae, the promise of accelerated learning, the condemnation, the surviving copies, the scholarly reappraisal of the text as something more interesting than superstition.

Invented: that it works — and how it works. Nadia's argument, developed across two volumes, is that the notae are interfaces: calibrated visual objects designed to produce specific neurological states in a sufficiently prepared observer, with the manuscript's fasting rules, postures, and prayer sequences functioning as a protocol for reaching the required state of cortical coherence before engaging the figures. That reading is the novel's invention. No historian makes it. No cognitive scientist makes it. It is the trilogy's one experiment the lab has never run — and in The Grimoire, the lab runs it.

I will flag one further invention, because it connects this essay to the next one: the trilogy's claim that the notae are frozen sound — cymatics patterns copied from standing waves by monks who watched geometry form in water and sand. The figures are real. The mechanism is mine. Essay three, on Chladni and Hans Jenny, walks that particular seam in full, and the Cymatics Field Note holds the physics.

Why this manuscript, of all manuscripts

The occult corpus is enormous. The trilogy could have run on the Lesser Key, on Agrippa, on Dee's Enochian tables — all of which sit in my research library and none of which carries the story. The Ars Notoria does, for one reason: it is the only major text in that tradition whose stated purpose is cognition. It doesn't summon anything. It doesn't bind anything. It claims to change what a mind can hold and how fast. A trilogy about perception — about what a prepared human being might register that an unprepared one cannot — needed an operational manual, and the thirteenth century had already written one and been condemned for it.

The manuscript is real. Nadia's reading of it is the novel. The full apparatus — the entry on the Ars Notoria, the notae plates, the sources, and the honest grades — arrives in the Masters X Universe Encyclopedia. Until then, the research is live in the Field Notes, and the books are at jasoncholloway.com.

Blog 03: Sound Into Form READY

Sound Into Form: What Hans Jenny Actually Proved

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 3 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

The oldest special effect in my trilogy requires no computer. A metal plate, a violin bow, a spoonful of fine sand. Draw the bow along the plate's edge and the sand jumps, migrates, and settles into a symmetrical geometric figure — as if the tone had drawn it. The demonstration is from 1787. You can reproduce it on your kitchen table tonight, and I mean that literally: level the plate, sift the sand, keep the bow pressure light.

Everything strange in the Masters X universe stands, finally, on that plate. So this essay does what the trilogy's apparatus always does: it separates what the plate actually proves from what the novels build on top of it.

Chladni: frequency becomes figure

Ernst Chladni (1756–1827) was the first to document the phenomenon systematically, in his 1787 Entdeckungen über die Theorie des Klanges. The physics is elegant. A bowed plate vibrates in modes; at a resonant frequency, some regions of the plate move violently while the nodal lines stay still. Sand bounces off the moving regions and collects on the still ones. The figure you see is a map of the silence — the geometry of where the plate is not moving. Change the frequency and the nodal map changes, so the figure changes with it: simple crossings at low tones, elaborate mandalas higher up.

The grade on all of this is the cleanest in the whole trilogy: measured, reproducible by anyone, disputed by no one.

Jenny: one phenomenon, three registers

A century and a half later, Hans Jenny (1904–1972), a Swiss physician, took Chladni's parlor demonstration and turned it into a research program. Crystal oscillators replaced violin bows, so the driving frequency could be set precisely. Liquids, pastes, and fine powders replaced sand, so the behavior of different media could be compared. He published the results in the two-volume Kymatik (1967, 1972) — translated as Cymatics, the term he coined.

Two of Jenny's findings matter most for the fiction. The first is sensitivity: a small change in frequency can produce a categorically different pattern — his documented shift from 800 to 865 cycles per second transforms the figure rather than adjusting it. In a world where sound makes form, the decimal places matter, which is why a novel that runs on 111.2 Hz cannot treat 111 and 112 as neighbors.

The second is conditionality. Turn the tone off and the pattern collapses into disorder. Turn it back on and the same pattern returns. Order, in a cymatics rig, is not a thing that gets built and then persists; it is a state continuously maintained by the frequency. Jenny's own summary — "one can hear what one sees" — names his triadic model: the wave you hear, the pattern you see, the periodicity you feel are one phenomenon apprehended three ways.

That is what Jenny actually proved, and it is plenty. What he did not prove — and here the record demands honesty, because the internet is thick with claims wearing his name — is anything about healing frequencies, water memory, or consciousness. The Cymatics Field Note draws that boundary explicitly: the physics is real; a good deal of what circulates under the word "cymatics" outruns it.

The gap the novel lives in

Now the seam, and I want to cut it precisely, because this essay's honesty is only worth something if it names the exact sentence where the record ends.

There is no evidence that viewing a cymatics pattern alters consciousness. None. The documented phenomenon is sound organizing matter; the trilogy's central conceit is that the resulting geometry, contemplated by a prepared observer, does something to the observer. That claim belongs entirely to the novels. It is the bridge between this essay and the last one — between Jenny's plates and the Ars Notoria's notae — and the bridge is fiction. The trilogy proposes that medieval practitioners, chanting daily for decades in resonant stone, over water basins, saw standing-wave patterns form and copied them; that the notae are cymatics figures, frozen sound; and that a scribe in Bohemia in 1267 fixed seven hundred twelve of them onto vellum without knowing what he was drawing. Every clause of that is mine. No historian makes the claim. It is, as I've said elsewhere in this series, the novel's one experiment the lab hasn't run.

Honest fiction names its gap, and this is the trilogy's: the leap from sound makes form (measured) to form remakes the observer (invented).

Water, and why the trilogy keeps drowning things

Jenny filmed liquids as well as powders, and liquids are where his footage is most arresting — standing patterns holding on a vibrating water surface like geometry with surface tension. The research note in my files that grew into a structural principle is one sentence long: water amplifies cymatics effects.

Readers notice that the trilogy keeps returning to water: Brother Aldric's basin in the 1267 prologue, Father Moreau's river, the Ozark lake with its ninety documented feet of dark. That recurrence is the fiction using Jenny's liquids as physics. In the novels, water is the medium in which the frequency becomes visible enough to copy — which is why the story's pivotal images keep forming on its surface, and why its losses keep happening under it.

There is also a fictional payoff built directly on the physics of pattern specificity. In The Grimoire, Nadia has spent months mapping the Strahov chamber's resonance, which produces — in the novel — a stable hexagonal figure. Then she watches footage of Kofi Asante's drumming in Ghana producing a five-sided standing wave in red laterite clay, and recognizes a different expression of the same underlying system. Hexagonal Chladni figures are common enough in nature's close-packing geometry; stable pentagonal symmetry is rarer. Both specific patterns, and Nadia's inference that they share a structural origin, are invented. The dependence of pattern on material, geometry, and frequency — the thing that makes her inference feel like science — is real.

Yuki Tanaka, and fictionalized methodology

One more register deserves labeling. The trilogy's researchers do not merely cite cymatics; they practice it, and their methodology is written to be recognizable. Yuki Tanaka's disc experiments in the novels — controlled frequency, documented media, repeated trials, the discipline of changing one variable at a time — are fictional experiments conducted correctly. I wrote them that way on purpose. If the trilogy's science had been sloppy inside the fiction, the reader's trust in the labeled seams outside it would have been unearned. The characters' method is real method; only their results are invented.

That, in the end, is what Hans Jenny gives the Masters X universe: not proof of anything mystical, but a demonstration — bench-top, repeatable, gorgeous — that frequency and form are already married in the physical world. The novels only propose one further wedding, and they say so out loud.

Blog 04: Why Kansas City? READY

Why Kansas City? The Ground Itself Is Significant

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 4 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

When people learn that the Masters X Trilogy is a conspiracy novel, they assume Rome. When they learn it involves medieval manuscripts, they assume Prague — and the story does get to Prague. But the gravity well of the whole trilogy is Kansas City, Missouri, and readers regularly ask whether that was hometown convenience. I live here; I wrote what I could see.

The honest answer is that the convenience ran the other direction. I did not choose Kansas City because I live here. I came to understand, over twenty years of research, that I live somewhere genuinely strange — that five independent traditions across two thousand years looked at the same thirty miles of Missouri river bluff and concluded, in five unrelated vocabularies, that this ground matters. The novel didn't invent that convergence. It just needed a mechanism to explain it.

This essay lays out four of the five. The fifth gets an essay of his own.

The people who were already building underground

Start two thousand years back. The Hopewell tradition — the mound-building culture that spread along the river systems of eastern North America between roughly 100 BCE and 700 CE — reached its westernmost frontier at the Missouri River bluffs near present-day Kansas City. About thirty sites are documented in the area. And the detail that stopped me when I first read the site reports: some of these mounds contained stone-vault tombs — constructed chambers, entered by doorways, built inside the earthworks.

Which means the impulse the trilogy is built on — cutting significant chambers into this specific ground — is not a modern invention and not a fictional one. People were building rooms in this ground before Rome fell. Missouri's prehistoric record runs deeper still: the state's cave art, documented by Carol Diaz-Granados and James Duncan, is among the richest in the interior of the continent. The Kansas City reader's map holds the geography.

The name for the thing

The second tradition supplied the trilogy with its central abstraction, and I want to be careful to credit the source rather than absorb it. The Osage, whose historic corridor runs through western Missouri, carry the concept of Wah-kon-tah — often rendered as the sacred mystery that connects everything. It is an attunement tradition: a relationship to the connecting mystery, practiced rather than notated.

The trilogy is, at bottom, a story about people trying to build instruments for something that one of this continent's older traditions simply practiced, without needing the machinery. When the novels gesture at what the Foundation is chasing, they are gesturing at something the Osage already had a name for. The name is not mine, and the books say so.

Zion, sixty-three acres of it

The third tradition arrives with a date and a survey plat. In July 1831, Joseph Smith declared Independence, Missouri — twenty minutes from my desk — to be the "center place" of Zion, recorded in Doctrine and Covenants 57. The theology that grew around the declaration is more specific than most people outside the Latter Day Saint movement realize: the Garden of Eden located in Jackson County; Adam-ondi-Ahman seventy miles north; a city plat drawn with sites for twenty-four temples. On August 3, 1831, a sixty-three-acre parcel — the Temple Lot — was dedicated for the first of them.

The temple was never built. The lot was never abandoned. It is maintained today, kept by three denominations of the movement, a mown green rectangle holding a hundred-ninety-year-old appointment. Whatever a reader makes of the theology, the fact pattern is extraordinary: an American prairie town, formally designated the center of a cosmology, with the designation still honored by its inheritors.

And here is the encyclopedia's honesty rule in action, because this is the claim readers most expect me to have exploited: there is no Temple Lot scene in the trilogy. We checked, during the research audit. The site exerts gravity on the story without ever appearing in it — which is, I'd argue, how significance actually behaves.

One more building belongs in this section, because it is the tradition's rhyme with the trilogy's other obsession. In 1994 the Community of Christ completed its temple in Independence — architect Gyo Obata, a sanctuary that spirals a hundred ninety-five feet upward on a nautilus form. The Fibonacci sequence, as literal architectural DNA, standing in the prophesied Zion. Readers assume I invented that one. I did not.

The constant is the stone

Under all of these — under the mounds, the corridor, the platted Zion — runs the fourth tradition, which is not a belief system but a rock formation. The Bethany Falls limestone was deposited roughly 270 million years ago, when the middle of North America was a shallow sea. Kansas City's builders quarried it through the boom of 1880 to 1920, building the city above while hollowing the ground beneath. By mid-century, someone noticed that the worked-out chambers held a constant 65 degrees year-round, and the quarries became warehouses, and the warehouses became SubTropolis: fifty-five million square feet of excavated limestone, the largest underground business complex in the world, with roughly seventeen hundred people commuting into the ground every working day. The trilogy's bedrock tunnels run 160 feet down; the real facility's depth is in the same conversation. Some settings you don't have to invent.

The stone also supplied the trilogy's most quoted line with its documentary anchor. In 2011, Westport Presbyterian Church burned — roof, interior, everything — except the 1904 limestone walls, which stood. "The stone remembers" was in the manuscript before I found the fire that proved it. That story gets its own essay later in this series.

And one honest gap, stated plainly because the pattern of this series demands it: there is no published acoustics study of the Kansas City underground. The chambers of Malta and Britain have been measured; the chambers under my own city have not. The fiction is sited here, not sourced here. Whatever the Bethany Falls limestone does to a standing wave at 160 feet, nobody has put it in a journal — which is exactly the kind of silence a novel is entitled to fill, provided it says it is filling one.

The fifth seeker

Four traditions, then: the mound builders who cut chambers into this ground; the Osage, who named the connecting mystery; the Latter Day Saint movement, which surveyed Zion here to the acre; and the limestone, which made all the burrowing possible and remembers everything built on it.

The fifth is a living man — an engineer with a prophetic calling and a hundred eighty underground acres of the same limestone, documented in land records and public reporting, whose real biography out-strange-ed anything I had drafted. He is the subject of the next essay, because compressing him into a paragraph would waste him. What the novel adds to all five is a fictional sixth: William Masters, whose Foundation inverts the pattern the real fifth seeker set.

At some point, as Nadia says in the trilogy, coincidence becomes evidence. The apparatus around the novels exists to tell you which side of that line every sentence stands on. The full five-tradition essay, with sources, arrives in the Masters X Universe Encyclopedia; the research layer is live now in the Field Notes, and the books are at jasoncholloway.com.

The facts are in the files. The fiction is in the books.

The Man Who Built a City Under Zion

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 5 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

Every novelist who works from research eventually meets the fact that embarrasses the fiction — the documented thing so improbable that putting it in a novel unaltered would read as laziness. Mine lives twenty minutes from my desk, in Independence, Missouri, a hundred and eighty acres down.

A sourcing note before anything else, because this essay concerns a living person: everything factual below is drawn from public records, published reporting, and his son's public statements. Where the record is silent, this essay is silent. The parallels to my fiction are conceded openly at the end, and the divergences matter more than the resemblances.

The public record

Roger Billings entered the public record as a prodigy. As a teenager — seventeen, in the standard telling — he converted a car to run on hydrogen, decades before hydrogen energy had a lobby or a market. The work was real engineering, and it made him, in the small world of alternative energy, a genuine pioneer. He went on to found research ventures and an educational institution, the Academy, and to accumulate the kind of résumé — inventor, entrepreneur, institution-builder — that profiles get written about.

In 1980, he relocated to Independence, Missouri. And here the story acquires its second axis, because by his own account the relocation was not about markets or family or weather. It was prophetic. Independence is the ground that Joseph Smith designated, in 1831, as the center place of Zion — the fact pattern the previous essay in this series laid out. Billings came to Independence, founded his own church within the broader Restoration tradition — the Church of Jesus Christ in Zion — and holds within it the office his tradition describes as patriarch and prophet.

The record beneath the surface

Then there is the land. Billings acquired approximately one hundred eighty acres of underground space — worked limestone, the Bethany Falls formation, the same Pennsylvanian-age stone that hosts SubTropolis on the other side of the metro. And he developed it: laboratories, work space, living quarters, an operating campus inside the rock. An engineer-prophet, running research facilities in the ground his own tradition identifies as the New Jerusalem.

Read that sentence again as a fiction editor would. A hydrogen pioneer. Who founded his own church. Who calls himself its patriarch and prophet. Who bought a hundred eighty acres of underground limestone inside the prophesied Zion. And built laboratories in it. If I had submitted that character in a first draft, any competent editor would have flagged him as overwritten — too many axes of strangeness in one man. He is not a draft. He is a matter of county land records and published interviews, and my research file on him (`ROGER_BILLINGS_MASTERS_X_ANALYSIS.md`, for those keeping score against the encyclopedia's bibliography) is one of the thickest in the archive.

The parallel, conceded

Readers of The Inheritance of Frequency will already have made the mapping, so let me concede it rather than be caught at it. William Masters, the trilogy's founding figure, is an engineer of unusual gifts who becomes convinced that something immense sleeps under the Kansas City corridor; who acquires underground limestone; who builds a private research campus in it; who documents everything in notebooks; and who chooses silence over publication for most of his life. The resemblance to the documented figure is not accidental, and the encyclopedia's entry on William Masters says so with a straight face: the strangest thing in the Masters X universe is that its most implausible character has a documented counterpart.

But the divergences are the actual content of the fiction, and they are deliberate. Billings' compound is, on the record, private — fenced, surveilled, access-controlled, as any private research facility would be. His tradition's claims run through his church. William Masters' arc bends the opposite way at every joint the story controls. The Masters Foundation for Acoustic Research is written consent-first: nobody in the trilogy is brought into the chambers unknowing. Its findings are hoarded for two books and then, in the trilogy's ending, given away entirely — the Distribution File, released to the public domain at midnight, 247 pages that cannot be un-released. Where the real pattern is a prophet holding ground privately against a foretold future, the fictional pattern is an engineer's estate finally refusing to hold anything at all.

That inversion is the trilogy's ethical argument, and it only lands because the documented case exists. Fiction can't meaningfully disagree with a straw man. It can disagree with a land record.

The editorial care note, expanded

Three Factions, One Declassified Document

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 6 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

Thriller architecture usually requires three factions. One to guard the secret, one to exploit it, one to destroy it — the triangle generates plot the way a three-phase motor generates torque, and readers of the genre can feel the machinery even when they enjoy it. So when readers assume the Masters X Trilogy's triangle — the Keepers, the Custodians, the Completion Sect — was engineered for torque, the assumption is fair. It also happens to be wrong in the way this whole series exists to document.

I didn't invent the three factions. I found them in a declassified CIA file, already arguing.

The document

The file carries the designation CIA-RDP96-00792R, and it is a translation, dated to 1984: Langley rendering a Chinese government journal on paranormal research into English for internal circulation. Sit with what that sentence contains. On one side, a state Human Science Research Commission — a peer-reviewed journal, national conferences, funded experiments, the full institutional apparatus of a government studying claims of extraordinary human capacities openly. On the other side, an intelligence agency translating that open literature and filing it under classification. The line I kept returning to in my research notes is the one the trilogy eventually absorbed whole: both sides were studying the same thing. One government published. The other classified.

That asymmetry — openness versus secrecy as policies toward the identical subject — is the political physics of the entire trilogy, and I found it in the file, not the other way around.

Three camps, on the record

Inside the translated material, the debate has three recognizable positions, and I want to describe them as the document presents them rather than as the fiction stylizes them.

The first camp is the skeptics — those who condemned the research as "feudal sorcery," a phrase carrying the full ideological weight of a modernizing state embarrassed by its own countryside. Their program was suppression: stop the work, discredit the workers, protect the institutions from contamination.

The second camp is the preservers — researchers and officials who argued that if the phenomena were real, the only defensible response was open verification: publish the methods, replicate the results, let the work survive or die in daylight.

The third camp does not argue in the document so much as attend: the military interest, present wherever a state studies capacities that might be applied.

Suppress. Verify. Weaponize. In the trilogy those camps become the Custodians, the Keepers, and the Completion Sect — institutionalized, mythologized, given eight centuries of backstory. But when Nadia briefs Blake on the factions' logic, she is doing historiography, not invention. The novel says as much through her line: every government that discovered this made the same choice — meaning, faced the same three-way fork the 1984 file preserves.

Why a translation is stranger than a report

It is worth pausing on the document's genre, because the genre is what made it novelistically irresistible. CIA-RDP96-00792R is not an intelligence assessment of paranormal claims; the Agency produced those too, and they read as you'd expect — findings, confidence levels, recommendations. This is a translation: the raw open literature of one government, carried across a language barrier and filed by another. Which means the file preserves the original debate in its own voices — the skeptics' polemics, the preservers' methodological appeals, the institutional throat-clearing — rather than an analyst's summary of them. For a novelist, that difference is everything. A summary gives you conclusions; a translation gives you people arguing, and factions are made of people arguing. The three camps in my trilogy have the texture they have because their source was never a finding. It was a transcript of a fight.

The Army's own file

The 1984 translation is not the only declassified document in the trilogy's load-bearing wall. The other is American: the Gateway Process assessment — formally, the Analysis and Assessment of Gateway Process — a real U.S. Army evaluation of consciousness-alteration methods, examining the Monroe Institute's audio-driven techniques and attempting to construct a theoretical account of how such methods might work. Whatever a reader concludes about its physics, the document's existence is the point: the American security establishment did not merely spy on other governments' consciousness research; it commissioned an internal assessment of its own.

Readers of the first essay in this series will recognize where the Gateway lineage surfaces in the trilogy: the 136.6 Hz rung of the frequency ladder, which the novels map to the Monroe focus-level framework. The rung's referent is real — the framework exists, the assessment exists — and the rung's grade is the ladder's weakest: a documented body of practice, not a documented body of findings. The distinction is the whole reason the ladder is graded at all.

The fourth answer

Here is where the fiction stops transcribing the record and starts arguing with it.

The historical debate offers three answers to the discovery of a dangerous capacity: suppress it, verify it, weaponize it. The Stone Remembers: A Fire in Westport

Novelists are supposed to distrust their own best lines. "The stone remembers" had been in the Masters X manuscript for years — Father Moreau's line, the kind of sentence a writer keeps rereading with the queasy suspicion that it is too good, too round, the metaphor doing work the world never authorized. The correction that improved the book

The fire comes later. The correction comes first, because it is the less flattering story and this series has rules.

Early drafts described the Cathedral of the Immaculate Conception — the landmark on the Quality Hill bluff, the highest ground in downtown Kansas City — as limestone. It seemed safe. This is a limestone city; the trilogy is practically about the limestone; the cathedral crowns the bluff two hundred feet above the West Bottoms. Of course it was limestone.

It is red brick. Built 1882 to 1912, brick throughout, with the gold-leaf cupola added in 1960. Our research pass — the same church-by-church audit that produced the `KC_CHURCHES_RESEARCH_ANALYSIS` file in the encyclopedia's bibliography — caught the error before print.

A reader could reasonably ask why one wrong wall material matters in a work of fiction. It matters because of what the trilogy claims about ground truth. A novel arguing that the physical city is stranger and more significant than fiction has noticed forfeits that argument the moment it gets the physical city wrong. Every real place in the books — the corner table at Miller Nichols Library, the grade of the walk from Quality Hill down into the West Bottoms, the Hotel Phillips lobby — is rendered as exactly as I could manage, and the reader's map of the real Kansas City exists so anyone can check. The cathedral correction is the audit working as designed: the fiction gets to invent events, never geography.

And the corrected fact turned out to be better than the assumed one. A red brick cathedral wearing a gold crown on the city's highest bluff, above a floodplain, is a more precise image of Kansas City than another limestone facade would have been — the city's aspirations built in one material, its foundations in another.

The fire

Westport Presbyterian Church was built in 1904, in the neighborhood that was once the last outfitting stop for wagon trains heading west — stone walls, timber roof, a century of congregational life.

In 2011, it burned. The fire took the roof. It took the interior — the sanctuary, the woodwork, everything combustible that a hundred and seven years had accumulated. Photographs from the morning after show what fire photographs from stone buildings always show, and what I had somehow never registered until I was looking for it: the walls, standing. The 1904 limestone, smoke-darkened and roofless, holding its full height around a burned-out interior — the building's memory of itself outlasting everything the building contained.

The congregation rebuilt within the standing walls, which is its own kind of sentence about what stone makes possible: the fire could erase the contents but not the fact of the church, because the fact was mineral.

I found the fire in the research files years after Moreau's line was written. The novel did not predict anything — a 2011 fire preceded my finding it, and church fires are not rare. What the discovery changed was the line's status. "The stone remembers" had been a metaphor awaiting a warrant, and the warrant existed all along, four miles from my desk, on the public record. The encyclopedia's entry on Moreau cites the fire as the line's documentary anchor, and grades the pairing honestly: the fire is fact; the line's placement in a priest's mouth in a novel is fiction; the resonance between them is the reader's to keep.

What a church audit looks like

Since this essay leans on it twice, a note on the instrument itself. The church research file behind both stories is a building-by-building audit of Kansas City's historic religious architecture: construction dates, wall materials, fires, rebuilds, and siting — which congregations built on the bluffs and which in the bottoms, and what the difference cost them over a century of floods and fires. It began as fact-checking for a handful of scenes and became something closer to a materials census of the city's sacred buildings, because every checked fact raised two more. St. Francis Xavier's original church, for instance, burned in September 1925 and was answered with an exterior sculpture carved from twenty-one thousand pounds of Bedford limestone — a congregation replying to fire in the one vocabulary fire had respected. The audit exists so that when the novels say stone, brick, 1904, or bluff, a reader with a library card can catch me if I'm wrong. Once, as above, the audit caught me first.

The boom that built and hollowed the city

Both stories — the brick cathedral, the standing walls — sit on the same half-century of geology-as-economics, and it is worth laying the substrate bare because the whole trilogy stands on it.

Between roughly 1880 and 1920, Kansas City quarried its own foundation. The Bethany Falls limestone — deposited some 270 million years ago under a shallow inland sea — came out of the bluffs and went up into the city: churches, warehouses, retaining walls, the bones of every neighborhood old enough to have bones. The boom built the city above and hollowed the ground beneath, and the hollowing never really stopped; it just changed vocabulary, from quarry to warehouse to SubTropolis, the fifty-five-million-square-foot underground complex where seventeen hundred people now work inside the mined-out seam.

So when the trilogy treats Kansas City as a city with a mineral unconscious — a place whose surface and depths are the same substance in different states — that is not mysticism. It is the municipal history. The 1904 walls that survived the 2011 fire and the chambers 160 feet under the bluffs are the same formation, the same boom, the same stone remembering in two different postures.

That proposal is fiction and is labeled everywhere it appears. But it is fiction that got its best line audited against the record and kept it.

The artifact

Chen's brief: make the work boring

The intuitive opposite of classification is leaking. Chen's brief rejects the intuition. A leak, the argument runs, preserves everything that gives a secret its power — the scarcity, the drama, the sorting of the world into those who know and those who don't — and merely relocates that power to whoever did the leaking. The mystery survives its own exposure; often it grows.

The actual opposite of classification is to make the work boring: documented to the point of tedium, reproducible by strangers, free to the point of worthlessness as property. Nothing left to guard; no one left to guard it from; no initiation left to sell. The midnight release under CC0 is the mechanism — public domain as a one-way door. Ending the mystery by ending the scarcity is not a compromise between the trilogy's factions. It is the move that dissolves the board they were playing on.

Why a file format can carry an ending

A craft note, because "the trilogy ends with a document" is the kind of choice a writer should be able to defend. Endings resolve pressure, and for two and a half books the trilogy's pressure is scarcity: one protocol, guarded by three factions, each defined entirely by its answer to the question of who may know. A confrontation ending — chamber stormed, sect defeated — would have resolved a battle while leaving the scarcity intact; whoever won would simply become the new custodian, and the eight-hundred-year cycle would be one victor older. The only ending that actually discharges the pressure is one that destroys the scarcity itself, and scarcity of information is destroyed by exactly one instrument: unrestricted copying. Hence a license. The quietest legal object in the world, doing what no gunfight in the genre has ever managed — ending the story by making its secret unownable. The midnight timestamp is drama; the CC0 dedication is the plot.

The fiction's argument, graded honestly: the choice the file represents — daylight as policy, radical enough to be boring — is a documented tradition with living practitioners.
