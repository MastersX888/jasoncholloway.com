Chapter Ten. SubTropolis.

One hundred nine hertz. Kansas City.


The service shaft dropped into darkness.

They stood at the edge. Rusted metal grate, weathered wooden housing, surrounded by overgrown grass on property the Jesuit shell company had owned for sixty years. October night, cold and clear. Stars thick as spilled salt. No moon. The only light came from Kansas City's ambient glow on the horizon, an orange haze that stopped at the treeline.

Andrew pulled the grate aside. Metal on stone. The sound carried farther than it should have.

Blake went first. His hands found the rungs. Cold, slick with condensation. The commercial mining level, SubTropolis proper, fifty-five feet below the surface, ended here. Everything below this point was older, deeper, and no longer part of anyone's inventory. The shaft dropped a hundred and sixty feet into limestone that had been forming since before anything human existed. The first ten feet were lit by Andrew's flashlight. Rust-streaked concrete, bolts driven into bedrock. Then the light ended. Below that threshold, darkness. Not the absence of light but the presence of stone, the geological weight of 270 million years pressing inward from every direction.

Nadia came last. She'd changed into practical clothes. Dark pants, boots, a jacket Blake hadn't seen before. No silver ring. She'd taken it off and put it in her pocket before they left Genessee Street. Blake had noticed. She'd seen him notice. Neither mentioned it.

At the bottom, the shaft opened into a natural chamber. The darkness was total. Before the flashlight swept the space, the darkness resolved into geometry, not empty but structured, saturated with information encoded in limestone that hadn't seen light in years.

The experience was this: standing inside a pattern.

Not observing one. Standing inside a space where the walls, the ceiling, the floor had been shaped by forces, geological and human, that operated on the same mathematical principles his sight had been learning to read. The carvings covered every surface. A knowledge system encoded in stone by hands that understood symmetry as architecture, as the load-bearing structure of reality itself.

"They're everywhere," Andrew breathed, his light finding the carvings.

This wasn't the commercial SubTropolis Blake had patrolled for four years. The climate-controlled warehouses and office spaces. This was older. The natural cave system carved by underground rivers over a quarter billion years. And carved by humans at some point after. Pre-Columbian. A thousand years old. Maybe more.

"This way." Blake moved toward a passage William's map indicated. His sensitivity guided him now. The patterns forming directional indicators, a map written in proportion for those who could read it.

Blake could read it.



They moved through the caves for what felt like hours. Natural limestone chambers connected through passages that varied from wide corridors to narrow squeezes where they turned sideways, jackets scraping stone. Temperature holding at sixty-eight degrees, had held steady for millennia. Perfect for preservation.

Blake led, following patterns his grandfather had documented sixty years ago. Andrew checked their position on his tablet. Nadia moved between them, her hand occasionally touching the cave walls. Touching the stone as she touched manuscript pages. Fingertips. Reverent.

Blake stopped at an intersection. Three passages branching from a chamber whose ceiling rose beyond the reach of their flashlights. His flashlight caught something near the ceiling. A geometric figure matching one from the notebooks. Below it, barely visible: a smaller mark. Modern tools.

W.C.M., 1963.

William Carroll Masters. His grandfather had stood here sixty-two years ago.

"He was here," Blake said quietly.

"He told you he was." Nadia's voice behind him in the dark. "Through the notebooks. Through the safety deposit box. Through sixty years of patient documentation."

Blake touched the carved initials. Felt the grooves William's hands had made. Careful, precise. William would have been young then. Blake's age. Standing in this same darkness. Just beginning to understand.

He turned and continued down the passage William had marked.



The hum told them they were close. Not sound exactly. Vibration. The low-frequency tremor of industrial ventilation. Climate control, air circulation, the mechanical heartbeat of the commercial SubTropolis above and around them.

Andrew checked his tablet. "Vault 7-B should be through there." He pointed at what looked like solid rock.

But the limestone told him the truth. A door. Ancient, cleverly disguised, its seams hidden within the natural fracture patterns. The proportional figures around it formed a frame. An invitation. A warning. Both.

"There's a mechanism," Blake said, running his hands along the stone. His perception guided his fingers to depressions matching the proportional figures. "It's a lock. The pattern is the key."

He pressed the depressions in sequence. The order determined by the geometric logic. One. Three. Five. Seven. The Fibonacci positions. A grinding sound deep in the stone. Gears or counterweights, mechanisms that had been waiting for sixty years.

The wall swung inward. Cool air. The smell of old paper and oilcloth.

Beyond: shelves. Boxes. The accumulated secrets of a century.

"Vault 7-B," Nadia whispered. "It's real."



The vault was larger than expected. Shelves lining three walls, floor to ceiling. Boxes labeled in Latin, dates ranging from 1890 to 1924.

"Ars Notoria fragments," Blake said. "Thirteenth century. Thought lost in a monastery fire in 1342."

Nadia was already photographing, her hands shaking. "How did Moreau get them?"

"He was a Keeper."

They found more. Box after box. Hebrew texts alongside angular figures. Arabic calligraphy flowing around the same seven-fold symmetry. Chinese characters describing the same relationships. Moreau hadn't just collected Western grimoires. He'd assembled evidence of a universal system, structure recognition technology that transcended cultures, languages, continents.

Blake and Nadia worked side by side, photographing everything. Their movements synchronized. He'd open a box, she'd document, he'd move to the next. Working together without speaking.

Andrew monitored his tablet. "Motion sensors. Security patrol approaching. Maybe three minutes."

They killed their flashlights. Darkness. Absolute, total, the kind that only exists deep underground.

Blake could hear the guards through the wall. Footsteps. Voices. Complaining about the late shift. Unaware that sixty years of secrets waited beyond the limestone they patrolled.

And in the darkness, Blake felt it start.

The cascade began. Patterns exploding through his mind. From the stone itself, vibrating at frequencies his enhanced neurology could detect through his hands, through his feet. Information flooding in from every direction. His cognition expanding past its limits, trying to process a thousand years of encoded knowledge at once.

His muscles began to lock. Vision fragmenting even in the dark.

Andrew felt it. Blake's body going rigid. "Blake." Desperate whisper. "Circuit breaker."

Blake couldn't find it. The patterns were too dense, too many, feeding on his panic -

Then Nadia's hand found his in the darkness.

Not his shoulder. Not his arm. His hand. Her fingers interlacing with his, grip tight, the calluses on her palm pressing against his skin.

"I'm here." Her voice in his ear, the same voice that had guided him through three days of training. "Find me first. Then find the figure."

Blake found her. In the cascade of a thousand years of geometric information, he found the pattern of Nadia's hand in his. The pressure points, the warmth, as her fingers fit between his. Human geometry. The simplest pattern. The oldest.

From there, he found the seventh figura. The aperture slammed shut. The cascade cut off.

He gasped. Shaking. Sweat on his face. Her hand clasped in his.

The guards' footsteps grew louder. Six inches of limestone between them and discovery.

Then the footsteps faded. Moving on.

They waited ten minutes in the darkness. Nadia's hand didn't leave his. At some point her thumb had begun moving across his knuckles. Slow, rhythmic, the same cadence she used when guiding the notae meditation.

When the silence had held long enough, Blake whispered: "We need to move. Take what we can carry."

Andrew photographed in the dark. Nadia gathered the most critical manuscripts into her waterproof bag, working one-handed because her other hand was still holding Blake's.

She let go when they needed to move. The release was deliberate. She opened her fingers slowly, and the loss of contact was a physical thing, like stepping from a warm room into cold air.

Blake found the photograph last. William Carroll Masters, 1963, standing in this vault, holding a manuscript fragment. Young. Blake's age. Gazing at the camera with wonder and terror.

He pocketed the photograph. Led them back into the darkness.



The return journey was slower. Halfway to the shaft, Blake sensed something. A presence. A disturbance in the geometric field.

A light appeared ahead. Not a flashlight, a lantern, old-fashioned, casting flickering shadows on limestone.

Behind it, a figure. Elderly. Black robes. Jesuit.

"Mr. Masters." The voice echoed, calm, measured. "We've been expecting you."

Blake's awareness read the old man instantly. Not a threat. The mathematical figures on the walls seemed to respond to his presence.

"You're a Keeper," Blake said.

"I was a Keeper." The old priest stepped closer. "Before the factions split. My name is Father Elias Dryden. I've been guarding this place for forty years, since your grandfather asked me to."

"William knew you."

"We met in 1963. I was a young priest, assigned to maintain the property above. I caught him coming up the shaft." A thin smile. "He could have killed me. Instead, he showed me what he'd found. And he asked me to help protect it."

Nadia stepped forward. "Forty years. Guarding this alone."

"Someone had to." Father Dryden's eyes found Blake's. "William believed the right person would come. Someone who could see what he saw." He studied Blake's face. "You have his eyes."

The old priest reached into his robe. A small leather case. "William left this for you. He said you'd need it when you reached Prague."

"Prague?"

"Rudolf the Second's court. Sixteenth century. All three manuscripts came together there for the first time. Something happened. Something that changed everything." Dryden pressed the case into Blake's hands. "Your grandfather went in 1965. Found something. Came home and never spoke of it again."

Blake opened the case. A brass key. Ornate, antique. And a photograph: a library interior with vaulted ceilings and painted frescoes.

"The Strahov Library," Dryden said. "One of the oldest in Europe. Your grandfather believed there was a chamber beneath it, hidden since the sixteenth century. He found the entrance. Never opened it." The old priest's eyes held Blake's. "He was waiting for you."

Dryden led them to the shaft. At the base of the ladder, he stopped.

"Father Crane arrived in Kansas City three months ago. The Completion Sect has agents here too." His voice dropped. "Be careful who you trust."

He looked at Nadia. At their hands, still close from the vault. His face opened, recognition, perhaps. Or memory.

"Pray you make better choices than the rest of us," he said. Then he disappeared into the dark.



They emerged into dawn. October sky pink and gold, the air sharp after hours of underground stillness.

Andrew pulled the grate back. Nadia held the manuscript bag against her chest. Blake stood in the early light, William's photograph in one pocket, Dryden's brass key in the other, the Breitling counting time on his wrist.

Three keys now. William's Oscar-01 key. James's key from Margaret's box. And the Strahov Library key, waiting since 1965.

"Prague," Andrew said.

Blake looked at Nadia. She was standing in the dawn light with manuscripts pressed against her chest and her ring finger bare. In the early light, she looked different. Not less committed. Less divided.

"Prague," Blake confirmed. "But first we analyze what we found. We back up everything. Make it impossible to suppress. Then Prague."

"I know Prague," she said quietly. "I know the Strahov. I know the archives, the churches, the underground. I can get us in."

"I know you can."

They walked toward the car. Dawn breaking around them. Behind them, the sealed shaft, the sixty-year vigil, the old priest returning to his post in the dark.

And between Blake and Nadia, in the space where their hands had been linked in the vault, something had shifted. Not closed. Not resolved. But acknowledged.

She'd held his hand in the dark. He'd found her first, before the circuit breaker, before the emergency figure.

Neither said it. Not yet. There was Prague first. The Strahov Library. Whatever William had found in 1965.

But the hand. In the dark. Her fingers between his.
