### BEAT 1 — The record (0:45–2:45)

> Documented: the monastery was founded in 1143 by Vladislaus II of Bohemia for the Premonstratensian order. The Theological Hall was built 1671 to 1679 — barrel vault, frescoes painted by Siard Nosecký in the 1720s, wooden reading desks, astronomical globes. Chained libraries were standard practice across medieval Europe: valuable manuscripts secured to the desks so knowledge could be used but not removed.
>
> And one institutional miracle: the library survived the Communist period substantially intact — rare among Czech religious collections.

[OST: `1671–1679 · ~200,000 volumes`]

---

### BEAT 2 — The reading culture (2:45–4:30)

> The detail that matters isn't the beauty. It's the discipline. The Premonstratensian rule required systematic study; their libraries were working research collections, not decoration. Manuscripts flowing through their scriptoria in the twelve and thirteen hundreds included theology, philosophy, and what we would now call science.
>
> And the architecture itself concentrates: a barrel vault focuses both light and sound toward the center of the room. That is documented physics, not my invention.

[OST: `A working collection, 900 years deep`]

[VISUAL: To-camera.]

> Now the label. In *The Grimoire*, the Strahov Library is not a repository but a preparation chamber: twenty-three texts, read at twenty-three desks, in sequence, before the monks descended to an acoustic chamber below the Theological Hall. The twenty-three-desk inventory, the preparation function, the sealed crypt, and everything that happens inside it — fiction, all of it.
>
> What's true is narrower and stranger: the crypt levels beneath Strahov are not publicly documented the way the halls are. The monastery has floors tourists never reach. The fiction lives in exactly that gap.
