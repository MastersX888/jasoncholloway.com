# The Frequency That Was Already There

*The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 1 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press*

Every page of the Distribution File carries the same footer: f = 111.2 Hz. It runs under the preparation protocols, under the chamber specifications, under the facilitator's manual — two hundred forty-seven pages, one stamp. Readers ask about it more than they ask about anything else in the trilogy. Where did the number come from? Is it real? Should I be listening to it?

The honest answer takes a whole essay, because the honest answer is: the number is fiction, but only by one decimal place. The stone underneath it was measured before I ever started writing.

## What was actually measured

Archaeoacoustics is a real academic field, and a modest one. Its researchers record and analyze the acoustic behavior of ancient sites — the resonant frequencies, the reverberation, the way sound moves through a built space — to ask whether those properties were designed or incidental. The field's careful practitioners make careful claims, which is exactly why it is worth taking seriously.

The site everyone reaches for is the Ħal-Saflieni Hypogeum in Malta: an underground temple complex carved from limestone somewhere around 3600 to 2500 BCE, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site so fragile that only eighty visitors a day are admitted. In its Oracle Chamber — a roughly oval room under a curved limestone ceiling — researchers have documented a pronounced resonance around 110 to 111 Hz. A male voice chanting in that range produces an intense reverberant effect; the room answers back. The frequently cited 2008 study by Cook, Pajot, and Leuchter reported a shift in prefrontal activity in subjects exposed to sound at that resonance. It was one study, with a small sample. That caveat belongs in every sentence that cites it, and it appears in mine.

Malta is not alone. Paul Devereux and colleagues systematically measured resonances at Neolithic chambers across Britain and Ireland — chambered tombs, stone circles — and found peak frequencies consistently in the 95-to-120 Hz band. Aaron Watson and David Keating did the same at Camster Round in Scotland. Further back still, Iegor Reznikoff and Michel Dauvois observed that the Palaeolithic paintings at Lascaux and other French caves cluster disproportionately in the zones of highest acoustic resonance, and published the finding in 1988. Mainstream archaeology has received that interpretation with interest and caution in roughly equal measure.

I keep the full record — sites, studies, citations, and an honest FAQ — in the [111 Hz Field Note](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/111-hz/), which is the most complete public account of this research I could assemble. The short version: a recurring resonance near 110 Hz in ancient stone enclosures is documented. Whether anyone intended it is not.

## Why the fiction adds a decimal

So why does the trilogy say 111.2 instead of 110?

Partly because the extra digit is honest in its own way. If I had written 110 Hz, I would have been borrowing the authority of the measured literature for claims the literature does not make. The archaeoacousticians measured a resonance; they did not measure Blake Masters' bilateral tremor, which the novels lock at exactly 111.2 Hz, or a preparation protocol, or a perception chamber. Moving one decimal past the real number is the fiction signing its own work. The number is close enough to honor its source and distinct enough that nobody can mistake my invention for their measurement.

And partly because precision is a character trait. William Masters is an engineer. A man who leaves seven leather notebooks for his grandson does not write "about 110." He writes 111.2, with the instrumentation to defend the second decimal. The number carries his personality the way handwriting does.

## The whole ladder, graded

The trilogy's frequency system is a ladder, and every rung has a real referent — which is precisely why each one needs a grade.

At the bottom: 7.83 Hz, the Schumann resonance, the fundamental frequency of the cavity between the Earth's surface and the ionosphere. This is textbook geophysics — *measured*, no asterisk needed.

Next: 110 Hz, the archaeoacoustic band described above. *Measured*, with the honest caveat that the literature is thinner than a thriller needs it to be.

Then the trilogy's carrier: 111.2 Hz. *Invented.* Mine. One decimal past the stone.

And at the top: 136.6 Hz, which the novels connect to transcendence states. This rung comes from alternative tuning traditions and from the Monroe Institute's focus-level framework — a body of practice the U.S. Army once formally assessed in the Gateway Process document, which is its own strange story and its own essay. The grade here is *contested at best*: the traditions are real, documented practices; the claims made within them are not scientific findings.

The chapter headers in the trilogy map this ladder onto real places — the Kansas City limestone under [SubTropolis](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/subtropolis/), the Strahov crypt in Prague, Icelandic basalt, the Schumann fundamental. Every referent is documented. The claim that they form one coherent system is the novel. That sentence is the whole method of these essays, so let me set it off plainly: *the rungs are real; the ladder is fiction.*

## Sound has shapes

There is one more piece of real science holding the frequency system up, and it is the most photogenic physics of the eighteenth century. In 1787, Ernst Chladni drew a violin bow along the edge of a sand-covered metal plate and showed that the sand migrates to the nodal lines — the places where the plate is still — forming geometric figures that change with the frequency. Hans Jenny extended the work in the 1960s with oscillators, liquids, and powders, and documented how narrowly the geometry depends on the tone: shift from 800 to 865 cycles per second and the pattern doesn't merely adjust, it transforms into a categorically different figure.

That sensitivity is what makes a precise frequency narratively load-bearing. In a universe where sound produces form, 111.2 is not interchangeable with 111.3. The [Cymatics Field Note](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/cymatics/) covers Chladni and Jenny in full, including where popular cymatics claims outrun the research — and the third essay in this series walks that seam at length.

## Why label any of this

A reasonable reader might ask why a novelist bothers grading his own numbers. The fiction would work fine without the disclosure; thrillers borrow scientific authority all the time and nobody sues.

The answer is that the trilogy is *about* the danger of ungraded patterns. Umberto Eco's warning — that pattern-hunters end up manufacturing the pattern they seek — is quoted, structurally, in everything Blake Masters does. A book built on that warning cannot then hand its readers an unmarked mixture of measurement and invention and hope they sort it out. The two-register rule that governs the encyclopedia, the Field Notes, and these essays — *in the world* versus *in the novels* — is not a legal disclaimer. It is the ethic the story argues for, applied to the story itself.

So, one last time, the grades. The Schumann resonance: in the world. The 110 Hz chamber literature: in the world, thin but real. The 111.2 Hz carrier, the tremor, the protocol, the chamber: in the novels. The full apparatus — tables, sources, and every grade — arrives in the Masters X Universe Encyclopedia. Until then, the research layer is live in the [Field Notes](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/), and the books are at [jasoncholloway.com](https://jasoncholloway.com/books/masters-x/).

The facts are in the files. The fiction is in the books.
