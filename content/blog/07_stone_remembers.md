# The Stone Remembers: A Fire in Westport

*The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 7 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press*

"The stone remembers" sat in the Masters X manuscript for most of a decade on probation. It is Father Moreau's line, and it is exactly the kind of sentence a novelist should distrust: too round, too quotable, the metaphor claiming work the world never authorized.

Then a research pass turned up a fire four miles from my desk, and the line came off probation.

## The correction that improved the book

The fire comes second. The correction comes first, because it is the less flattering story.

Early drafts described the Cathedral of the Immaculate Conception, the landmark on the Quality Hill bluff and the highest ground in downtown Kansas City, as limestone. It seemed safe. This is a limestone city, the trilogy is practically *about* the limestone, and the cathedral crowns a bluff two hundred feet above the West Bottoms. Of course it was limestone.

It is red brick. Built 1882 to 1912, brick throughout, with the gold-leaf dome added in 1960. Our church-by-church research pass caught the error before it reached print.

One wrong wall material in a novel might not seem worth the paragraph. It matters because of what this trilogy claims about ground truth. A book arguing that the physical city is stranger than fiction has noticed forfeits the argument the moment it gets the physical city wrong. Every real place in the books is rendered as exactly as I could manage, from the corner table at Miller Nichols Library to the grade of the walk from Quality Hill down into the West Bottoms to the Hotel Phillips lobby, and the [reader's map of the real Kansas City](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/kansas-city-locations/) exists so anyone can check me.

The corrected fact turned out better than the assumed one. A red brick cathedral wearing a gold crown on the city's highest bluff, above a floodplain, is a more precise image of Kansas City than another limestone facade: the city's aspirations built in one material, its foundations in another.

## The fire

Westport Presbyterian Church was built in 1904, in the neighborhood that was once the last outfitting stop for wagon trains heading west. Stone walls, timber roof, a century of congregational life inside.

In 2011 it burned. The fire took the roof. It took the interior, meaning sanctuary and woodwork and everything combustible that a hundred and seven years had accumulated. Photographs from the morning after show what fire photographs from stone buildings always show, and what I had somehow never registered until I was looking for it: the walls, standing. The 1904 limestone, smoke-darkened and roofless, holding its full height around a burned-out interior.

The congregation rebuilt inside the standing walls, which is its own sentence about what stone makes possible. The fire could erase the contents but not the fact of the church, because the fact was mineral.

I found the fire years after Moreau's line was written. Nothing was predicted; church fires are not rare, and a 2011 fire preceded my finding it by the better part of a decade. What the discovery changed was the line's status. "The stone remembers" had been a metaphor waiting on a warrant, and the warrant had been sitting on the public record the whole time. The fire is documented. Putting the line in a priest's mouth is invention. The resonance between them is the reader's to keep.

## What a church audit looks like

Both stories came out of the same instrument, which deserves a note of its own.

The research behind them is a building-by-building audit of Kansas City's historic religious architecture: construction dates, wall materials, fires, rebuilds, and siting, tracking which congregations built on the bluffs and which in the bottoms, and what the difference cost them across a century of floods and fires. It started as fact-checking for a handful of scenes and turned into something closer to a materials census, because every checked fact raised two more.

St. Francis Xavier's original church, for instance, burned in September 1925 and was answered with an exterior sculpture carved from twenty-one thousand pounds of Bedford limestone: a congregation replying to fire in the one vocabulary fire had respected.

The audit exists so that when the novels say *stone*, *brick*, *1904*, or *bluff*, a reader with a library card can catch me if I am wrong. Once, as above, the audit caught me first.

## The boom that built and hollowed the city

Both stories sit on the same half-century of geology-as-economics.

Between roughly 1880 and 1920, Kansas City quarried its own foundation. The Bethany Falls limestone came out of the bluffs and went up into the city: churches, warehouses, retaining walls, the bones of every neighborhood old enough to have bones. The boom built the city above and hollowed the ground beneath, and the hollowing never really stopped. It changed vocabulary, going from quarry to warehouse to [SubTropolis](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/subtropolis/), where roughly seventeen hundred people now work inside the mined-out seam.

So when the trilogy treats Kansas City as a place whose surface and depths are the same substance in different states, that is municipal history, not mysticism. The 1904 walls that survived the 2011 fire and the chambers 160 feet under the bluffs are the same formation, the same boom, the same stone in two postures.

## What the fiction adds

Not much, in this one. The cathedral's brick and its 1960 dome, the Quality Hill bluff, the Westport fire and the standing walls, the rebuilding, the quarrying boom, the hollowed ground: all documented. Father Moreau, his river, his line, and every event the novels stage inside the real geography: invented.

The trilogy's only addition here is the proposal it always makes, that the stone's persistence is not merely structural but significant, that what survives fires and holds temperature and carries standing waves might be holding something else as well. That belongs to the novels, and it is labeled everywhere it appears. But it is fiction whose best line got audited against the record and kept.
