Chapter Six. The Investigation.

One hundred nine hertz. Kansas City.

[short pause]

Genessee Street smelled like river water and old brick.

October morning. Sharp. Bright. The kind of Kansas City day where the light hit the limestone bluffs and turned them the color of old bone. Blake could see further than he'd ever seen. The skyline downtown, the grain elevators on the far bank, the mathematical spacing of the railroad bridges.

Nadia led him through the West Bottoms at 9 AM, their breath visible. She walked a half-step ahead, confident, familiar, and Blake tracked her rhythm without deciding to.

1647 Genessee Street looked abandoned. Graffiti on the loading dock. Windows covered with plywood. A faded sign: RIVERWARDS COLD STORAGE CO. 1923.

But Blake could see the security now. Cameras disguised as broken light fixtures. Motion sensors in the graffiti patterns. A door that looked rusted shut but had fresh oil on its hinges. The geometric placement creating overlapping coverage zones.

"Hidden in plain sight," he said.

Nadia glanced back. Almost smiled. "Your grandfather taught you well."

The interior was nothing like the exterior. Clean. Modern. Banks of computers along one wall, bookshelves along another filled with volumes that predated mass production. A conference table covered with maps and documents in Latin, Greek, Arabic.

And standing at the table, a man Blake hadn't expected to see.

"Andrew?"

Andrew Chen looked up from his laptop. Same angular face Blake had known since eighth grade, same analytical intensity. But there was something new, a wariness Blake hadn't seen before.

"Hey." Andrew closed the laptop. "So. This is real."

Blake looked at Nadia. "You contacted him."

"Two months ago. When the safety deposit box timeline started accelerating. We needed a technical analyst. Andrew was already in your orbit, already had the skills." She met Blake's stare evenly. "He's been read in on the basics. He didn't believe any of it until this morning."

Andrew ran a hand through his hair. "She showed up at my apartment at 6 AM with seven leather notebooks. I thought she was insane." He paused. "Then I started reading Notebook Three."

"The cross-references," Blake said.

"The cross-references." Andrew's voice had gone quiet. "Between cave carvings and classified aerospace research. Blake, your grandfather was reverse-engineering antenna designs from petroglyphs. Those aren't diagrams, they're technical specifications."

"He found something," Nadia said simply.

Andrew studied Blake's face. The bruise, the butterfly bandages, the eyes that were probably still too bright.

"You look different," Andrew said.

"I am different."

"Show me."

Blake pointed at the wall behind him. "The brickwork. You see a wall. I see a tessellation. Hexagonal stress patterns from the original construction, each brick positioned according to load-bearing mathematics. The mortar lines create a grid that deviates because the building settled unevenly, probably due to the river table." He paused. "Your heartbeat just accelerated."

Andrew was silent for a long moment. Then: "Jesus Christ, Blake."

"I know."

Andrew looked at Nadia. "Can you do this?"

"No." The word came out flat. Final. Blake heard something underneath. Years of trying. "I understand them intellectually. But I can't perceive them the way Blake does. The Masters bloodline carries a neural framework that's uniquely responsive."

Blake watched the way she kept her voice clinical, her posture professional. But her eyes had gone somewhere private and painful before coming back.

She wanted to see what he saw. Had probably wanted it for years. And couldn't.

[pause]

They worked all morning.

Andrew scanned the notebooks page by page. Blake walked him through William's discoveries. Nadia translated Latin marginalia, cross-referencing citations against the Keepers' archive. She sat close enough that their elbows occasionally touched, and each time, neither moved away.

Andrew noticed. Blake caught him noticing.

"Father Moreau," Nadia said, pulling a photocopied newspaper clipping from the archive. "This is where it starts."

Kansas City Star, June 6, 1924. Page six. A French-Canadian priest at Sacred Heart Church had drowned in the Missouri River. Accidental. Case closed.

"Page six," Blake said. "For a priest who'd served at Sacred Heart for two years."

"Just like your father," Andrew said quietly. "Eighty-six years apart. Both investigating motifs. Both dead suddenly."

Blake was looking at Nadia's hands. The way she touched the century-old newsprint as if it were sacred text. Because to her, it was. Father Moreau had been one of hers.

"Every time someone starts seeing what you see," Andrew said, pulling up a timeline on his laptop, "someone else shows up to make them stop."

"The Custodians," Blake said.

"Your grandfather was smart enough to hide. Your father wasn't."

The room went quiet. Blake felt Nadia's hand brush his arm, not a grab. A touch. I'm here.

[pause]

Late afternoon. Andrew gone. Nadia reorganizing documents at the conference table.

"You wanted to see them," Blake said.

Her hands stopped. "What?"

"The patterns. When you said you can't perceive them, that wasn't just information. That was grief."

She didn't answer immediately. "I've spent my entire adult life studying this. Since Sister Katarina showed me a fragment of the Ars Notoria when I was sixteen. I felt something. A pull. I thought if I studied hard enough, I could teach myself to see."

"It didn't work."

"The awareness is neurological, not intellectual. You can't study your way into it any more than you can study your way into perfect pitch." The saddest smile Blake had seen from her. "So I became the world's foremost expert on a thing I can never experience."

Blake walked around the table. Sat next to her.

"Show me what you see when you look at the notae," he said.

"I just told you -"

"Not the patterns. Show me the scholarship. The history. The thing that made you take vows and cross an ocean and sit in a Kansas City warehouse waiting for someone like me."

Nadia's composure broke into surprise. No one had asked her that before.

She pulled the untitled book toward them. "Here. This is a figura notarum. But look at the construction lines." Her finger traced faint marks. "The artist used a compass and straightedge. Euclidean construction. In a book that carbon-dates to the eleventh century, in a tradition your grandfather traced to pre-Columbian North America."

"Which means -"

"Which means either the timeline of knowledge transmission is completely wrong, or there was contact between traditions that history doesn't account for." Her eyes were bright now, alive with the fierce intensity of someone speaking about the thing they loved. "That's what I see, Blake. Not the patterns. The history. The human hands that made these. The people who carried this knowledge across centuries and oceans, who encoded it in proportional figures because geometry is the one language that survives every empire."

She was leaning toward him. He was leaning toward her.

"That's what I see," she said quietly. "Is it enough?"

"It's beautiful," he said. And meant the history. And meant her.

The moment held. Somewhere in the future, the distance the gift would create between them was already forming. But not yet. Not today.

Today, Nadia smiled at him. The real smile. And Blake smiled back, and for a few minutes in a warehouse on Genessee Street, they were just two people sitting close together over an old book.

[pause]

They closed the warehouse at sunset. Walking back through the West Bottoms, they passed a wine bar. A chalkboard listed Missouri wines by the glass.

"Missouri was the third-largest wine-producing state before Prohibition," Nadia said. "The Jesuits planted the first vineyards in the 1820s. They mapped the terroir using angular measurements from the Ars Notoria. The vine rows followed the same proportional ratios as their manuscript illuminations."

"Wine and manuscripts," Blake said.

"Both ways of storing something alive in a container that outlasts the maker."

"Dinner?" he said.

"I should go back to -"

"Dinner. Not a briefing. Just. I haven't had dinner with another person in six months."

"Thai again?" she said.

Blake blinked. "What?"

"Thai food. Or something different?"

"Something different."

"There's an Ethiopian place on 39th Street. If you like injera."

"I have no idea what injera is."

"Then you'll learn something." She started walking again. "I should warn you. I eat with my hands. The nuns thought it was barbaric. I thought it was honest."

They walked east, toward the lights of the city, the distance between them closing with each block in a way apart from geometry and everything to do with the ordinary mathematics of two people choosing to be near each other.

The gray sedan followed at a respectful distance.

Neither of them looked back.
