# Why Kansas City? The Ground Itself Is Significant

*The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 4 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press*

Somewhere around the second century, people on the Missouri River bluffs built rooms inside their burial mounds — stone-vault tombs, constructed chambers with doorways, sealed inside the earthworks. Roughly thirty Hopewell-tradition sites are documented in the Kansas City area, the westernmost reach of a mound-building culture that spread along the eastern river systems between about 100 BCE and 700 CE.

I read that in a site report years into the research and set the page down. The impulse the whole trilogy runs on — cutting significant chambers into this specific ground — was not something I invented and not something modern. People were doing it here before Rome fell.

Readers assume Kansas City is hometown convenience. I live here; I wrote what I could see. The convenience ran the other direction. Twenty years of research taught me I live somewhere strange: four traditions and one living man have looked at the same thirty miles of river bluff and concluded, in vocabularies that never borrowed from each other, that this ground matters.

## The people who were already building underground

The Hopewell record is the oldest layer, and Missouri's prehistory runs deeper still. The state's rock art — documented over decades by Carol Diaz-Granados and James Duncan — is among the richest in the interior of the continent. What the mounds establish for my purposes is narrow and sufficient: chamber-building in this ground has a two-thousand-year precedent, on the public archaeological record, with no help from me.

The trilogy does not stage a scene in a Hopewell mound. It inherits a habit.

## The name for the thing

The second tradition gave the trilogy its central abstraction, and the credit belongs upstream. The Osage, whose historic corridor runs through western Missouri, carry the concept of *Wah-kon-tah* — renderings vary across sources and periods — often described as the sacred mystery that connects everything. It is not a doctrine so much as a relationship: practiced, not notated.

That distinction is the quiet joke underneath eight hundred pages. Masters X is a story about people building instruments, protocols, and chambers to reach something one of this continent's older traditions simply *practiced* without the machinery. When the novels gesture at what the Foundation is chasing, they are gesturing at something the Osage already had a name for. The name is not mine, and the books say so.

## Zion, sixty-three acres of it

The third tradition arrives with a date and a survey plat. In July 1831, Joseph Smith declared Independence, Missouri — twenty minutes from my desk — the "center place" of Zion, recorded in Doctrine and Covenants 57. The theology that grew around the declaration is more specific than most people outside the Latter Day Saint movement realize: the Garden of Eden placed in Jackson County, Adam-ondi-Ahman seventy miles north, a city plat drawn with sites for twenty-four temples. On August 3, 1831, a sixty-three-acre parcel — the Temple Lot — was dedicated for the first of them.

The temple was never built. The lot was never abandoned. It is maintained today, held by denominations of the movement, a mown green rectangle keeping a hundred-ninety-year-old appointment.

Whatever a reader makes of the theology, the fact pattern is extraordinary: an American prairie town formally designated the center of a cosmology, with the designation still honored by its inheritors.

And this is the claim readers most expect me to have exploited, so: there is no Temple Lot scene in the trilogy. We checked during the research audit. The site exerts gravity on the story without ever appearing in it, which is how significance actually behaves.

One more building belongs here. In 1994 the Community of Christ completed its temple in Independence — architect Gyo Obata — a sanctuary that spirals a hundred ninety-five feet upward on a nautilus form. Readers who know the trilogy's fixation on growth ratios assume I invented a Fibonacci spire standing in the prophesied Zion. I did not. The building is there, and it is open to visitors.

## The constant is the stone

Under the mounds, the corridor, and the platted Zion runs the fourth tradition, which is not a belief system but a rock formation.

The Bethany Falls limestone was laid down when the middle of North America was a shallow sea. Kansas City's builders quarried it hard through the boom of 1880 to 1920, raising the city above while hollowing the ground beneath. By mid-century someone noticed the worked-out chambers held a constant sixty-five degrees year-round, and the quarries became warehouses, and the warehouses became [SubTropolis](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/subtropolis/): roughly fifty-five million square feet of excavated footprint, the largest underground business complex in the world, with about seventeen hundred people commuting into the ground every working day.

The trilogy's bedrock tunnels run 160 feet down. The real facility's depth is in the same conversation. Some settings you do not have to invent.

The stone also handed the trilogy's most quoted line its documentary anchor. In 2011 Westport Presbyterian Church burned — roof, interior, everything — except the 1904 limestone walls, which stood. "The stone remembers" was in the manuscript before I found the fire that proved it. That one gets [its own essay](https://jasoncholloway.com/blog/the-stone-remembers/).

## What nobody has measured

Here is the gap. There is no published acoustics study of the Kansas City underground. The chambers of Malta and Britain have been recorded and analyzed; the chambers under my own city have not. The fiction is *sited* here, not *sourced* here. Whatever Bethany Falls limestone does to a standing wave at 160 feet, nobody has put it in a journal — which is a silence a novel may fill, provided it admits it is filling one.

## The fifth

Four traditions, then: the mound builders who cut rooms into this ground, the Osage who named the connecting mystery, the Latter Day Saint movement that surveyed Zion here to the acre, and the limestone that made all the burrowing possible.

The fifth is a living man — an engineer with a prophetic calling and a large underground holding in the same formation, documented in land records and public reporting. His biography out-strange-ed anything I had drafted, and he is getting an essay of his own rather than a paragraph here. What the novel adds to all five is a fictional sixth: William Masters, whose Foundation inverts the pattern the fifth seeker set.

At some point, as Nadia says in the trilogy, coincidence becomes evidence. My job is to keep telling you which side of that line each sentence is standing on. The mounds, the corridor, the plat, and the limestone are in the world. The convergence that explains them is in the novels.
