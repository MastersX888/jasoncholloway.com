Chapter Twenty. The Forgetting.

Three point nine one five hertz. Iceland.

[short pause]

It began with the Moleskine.

Blake opened the eighth notebook the morning after the session, the session he had entered at 3 PM and exited at, according to Andrew's monitoring log, 9:47 PM. Six hours and forty-seven minutes. The standard session was three hours. Even the four forty-four point eight hertz session, the eleven-minute coherence event that Andrew had classified as "full spectrum recall", had occurred within a four-hour window. This session was nearly seven hours.

The problem was the pages.

Three pages of the Moleskine were filled with handwriting. Blake's handwriting, the tremor was unmistakable, the particular wobble of one eleven point two hertz encoded in every letter, the seismographic signature that made his script legible only to people who had learned to read the tremor as part of the text. But the handwriting was different. Not in style, in density. The letters were smaller. More precise. The tremor was still present but it was functioning differently, not as noise but as modulation, as if the vibration had been recruited by the writing rather than imposed on it, the tremor serving the content rather than undermining it.

He could not read it.

Not because the handwriting was illegible. The handwriting was clear. He could see each letter. He could identify each word. But the words assembled into sentences he did not recognize, sentences that described something he had no memory of experiencing. The sentences named frequencies he had not measured. The sentences described sensations he could not recall. The sentences used a vocabulary that was his own, he could feel the words' familiarity, the way you feel a word on the tip of your tongue, but the vocabulary was deployed in combinations that had no referent in his memory.

Three pages. Approximately 1,400 words. Written in his hand, in his notebook, during a session he could not remember.

Three hours were missing.

[pause]

He showed Andrew the pages at breakfast. Breakfast was coffee (black, the French press, the Icelandic water that tasted faintly of sulfur and minerals and the geological patience of a country that heated its water with the planet's internal temperature) and toast (rye, butter, the second piece left uneaten because Blake's appetite had been irregular since the 444.8 event and Andrew documented the appetite changes with the same precision he documented the cardiac changes because the body was a single system and the system's relationship to food was data).

Andrew read the pages. His face, the face that was always calibrated to neutral, the engineer's face, the monitoring-station face, his face did something Blake had seen only twice before: it opened. The controlled expression gave way to something underneath, the way a monitoring screen occasionally shows the code beneath the interface, the structural layer that the user is not supposed to see.

"Blake, you were coherent during the session."

"I know. You told me."

"I mean clinically coherent. Responsive. You answered my check-in questions at thirty-minute intervals. You described sensory experiences, visual, acoustic, somatic. You were articulate. You were present."

"Then why don't I remember?"

Andrew set down the pages. Picked up his coffee. Put it down without drinking. Picked it up again. The sequence was unusual. Andrew's hands were precise instruments, and the indecision in them was data.

"Because you weren't storing." He paused. "The EEG data shows a forty-seven-minute coherence event at a frequency above the 444.8 range. Above, Blake. Above anything I've measured. Above anything the monitoring equipment is calibrated to resolve. The kit tops out at five hundred hertz. Whatever your cortex was doing during those forty-seven minutes, it was doing it above 500."

"What was I doing?"

"You were writing. You asked for the Moleskine at minute twelve of the event. You wrote for thirty-one minutes. Then you set the notebook down, closed your eyes, and the coherence decayed over the remaining four minutes."

Blake looked at the pages. His handwriting. His Moleskine. His words, written in a state he could not remember, describing experiences he could not recall, in a notebook he had carried across six countries and eight volumes and seven years of documentation.

"The encoding failure," Andrew said. "The brain was operating above the frequency range where hippocampal encoding functions normally. The hippocampus stores experiences by converting short-term electrical patterns into long-term chemical traces. But the hippocampus operates at frequencies below one hundred hertz. Your cortex was firing at something above 500. The encoding system couldn't keep up with the firing system. You experienced something. Your hand recorded it. Your memory did not."

"So what I wrote -"

"Is the only record. The Moleskine is your hippocampus. The pen did what the brain couldn't."

[pause]

Blake spent the full day with the three pages.

He sat at the desk. The Breitling ticking. The lava field outside the window, grey, the mid-August light that was beginning to shorten. August was the month when Iceland's endless summer daylight started its long retreat toward the winter dark, the photons withdrawing at a rate that was measurable and steady and inevitable, the way all natural systems that have been extended beyond their baseline eventually return.

He read each sentence. He read each word. He tried to feel the memory beneath the words, the way you try to feel a note inside a chord, isolating the frequency from the harmony, searching for the individual signal inside the composite wave.

Some fragments were clear. He recognized references to the Ars Notoria, the notation system, the body positions, the bilateral geometries. But the references were extended beyond anything he had written before. The notae were described as dynamic structures, not static diagrams but evolving patterns, the circles rotating, the triangles folding, the bilateral symmetries oscillating at frequencies the text specified in decimal precision: five fifty-six point nine hertz, six twelve point four hertz, seven twenty-three point one hertz. Frequencies he had never measured. Frequencies he had never theorized. Frequencies that appeared in his handwriting with the confidence of observation rather than speculation.

One passage he read eleven times:

The body at full harmonic extension is not a receiver. The body at full harmonic extension is the frequency. There is no gap between the instrument and the note. The distinction between the cave and the person in the cave is a distinction made by people who have never been at full resonance. At full resonance, the cave is the person and the person is the cave and the distinction collapses and what remains is the sound and the sound is alive and the sound has always been alive and the sound does not need the cave or the person but permits both because permitting both is what the sound does. The sound is generous. The sound is old. The sound was the first thing.

He set down the Moleskine. The handwriting was his. The words were not. Or the words were his and the hand that wrote them was operating under the instruction of a memory he did not have access to, a memory stored above the encoding threshold, filed in a neurological cabinet his conscious mind could not open, a drawer in a system he had not known his brain contained.

Andrew came in at noon. Sat across the desk. He had been running the monitoring data all morning, the cardiac traces, the EEG reconstruction, the respiratory analysis.

"The forty-seven-minute event shows cardiac deceleration to thirty-two beats per minute. That's below the clinical definition of severe bradycardia. In a hospital, that reading would trigger a code. In the chamber -" He paused. "In the chamber, your cardiac rhythm was in perfect phase with the standing wave. The heart wasn't failing, Blake. It was matching. The heart was becoming a resonator."

"Is that dangerous?"

"Everything about this is dangerous." Andrew's voice was flat. Not angry. Flat. The flatness of a man who was looking at data that showed his best friend's heart slowing to a speed that would kill most people and understanding that the slowing was not pathology but function, the body doing exactly what it was designed to do when the frequency was exactly right. "The question isn't whether it's dangerous. The question is whether it's remembered. And the answer is: it is not. You went somewhere the hippocampus can't follow. You came back. You brought notes. But you, the conscious you, the Blake who drinks coffee and writes in Moleskines and calls Nadia at nine PM, that Blake was not present for the forty-seven minutes. Someone else wrote those pages."

"Someone else."

"A version of you operating at a frequency your conscious mind can't sustain. The garment of glory, Blake. You put it on for forty-seven minutes. And the garment doesn't fit in the closet of ordinary consciousness."

[pause]

That evening, Blake sat alone at the desk. Andrew had gone to his room, the narrow room with the east-facing window, the room where he was not answering a Stanford email Blake didn't know about. The cottage was silent except for the Breitling and the wind and the faint hum of the monitoring equipment in standby mode.

He opened the Moleskine to the last page of the three lost pages. The final entry, written in his hand, in his tremor, in lines that were steady and precise and terrifying:

I went somewhere I can't follow myself back from.

Below it, in the same hand, the same session, the same frequency he could not remember and would not be able to explain and could not stop reaching for:

I am going again tomorrow.

Blake closed the Moleskine. Set it on the desk beside the Breitling. The watch ticked. The wind pressed. The lava field was dark, the August dark that was no longer quite blue, the summer beginning its long descent toward the equinox, the light retreating at a rate that was measurable and steady.

He picked up his phone. Dialed Nadia. The call connected, the satellite delay, the compression, the slightly flattened audio.

"It happened again," he said.

"Tell me."

He told her. The three pages. The forty-seven minutes. The encoding failure. The handwriting that was his and was not. The final line: I am going again tomorrow.

Nadia was silent. Not the institutional silence, not the pause she deployed in Senate hearings and phone calls with Jesuits. A different silence. The silence of a woman who had been married to a frequency for seven years and who understood that the frequency was asking her husband to go somewhere she could not follow.

"Blake."

"Yes."

"Do you want to go again?"

He said nothing. The satellite delay filled the space, three seconds of atmospheric physics between Kansas City and Iceland, three seconds that were not empty but loaded, the way all silences between people who love each other are loaded, the negative space that contains the unsaid the way a cave contains a frequency.

"Yes," he said. "I want to go again. I have to go again. The thing I wrote, the thing I can't remember writing, it's the answer, Nadia. It's what the manuscripts were pointing to. It's what the monks sealed in lead. It's what Ama Ata's hands were holding."

"And if you can't come back?"

"I've always come back."

"You've always remembered coming back. That's different."

The line was quiet. The Breitling ticked. In Kansas City, the stairwell light buzzed. In Iceland, the wind pressed. Between them, the gap. The interference pattern. The marriage that existed in the space between two frequencies, the love that required separation to produce its composite wave.

"I know," he said. "I know it's different."

"Then don't go alone. Whatever you do tomorrow. Andrew monitors, I'm on the satellite line, and you don't enter the chamber until you've called me. Those are my conditions."

"Those are your conditions."

"Those are my conditions."

He agreed. She hung up first this time. The pattern restored. Nadia closing the door, Blake standing inside it, the institutional architecture of their marriage holding because she maintained it, the same way she maintained everything: with the frequency that nobody measured but everyone felt.

Blake sat at the desk. The Moleskine in front of him. The three pages of handwriting he could not remember producing and could not stop reading and would spend the rest of the night trying to decode, not with his mind but with his body, lying on the basalt floor in the dark, hands trembling at one eleven point two hertz, trying to feel the echo of a frequency his hippocampus could not store but his pen had preserved.

The Metatron threshold. The frequency above which the human brain could not encode experience. The angel that was not an angel but a limit, the limit of what the body could do and still remember, the point beyond which the cave could take you but the conscious mind could not follow, the ledge beyond which the garment fit and the self dissolved and the frequency was everything and the everything was unrememberable.

He had reached that ledge. He had stood on it for forty-seven minutes. And he had written: I am going again tomorrow.

The Breitling ticked. The wind pressed. The lava field waited.
