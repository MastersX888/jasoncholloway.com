Chapter Twenty. Nadia's Departure.

Sixty-seven hertz. Kansas City.


Three weeks later. A Thursday. 5:12 AM.

She had chosen Thursday because she had checked the calendar and Thursday was the day Marcus taught the advanced cohort and Blake would be at UMKC by seven and the apartment would be empty by the time the difficult part began. She had organized her own departure with the same institutional precision she applied to everything. She did not feel guilty about this. Organization was how she survived. It was how she had always survived.

The suitcase was packed already, had been packed since Tuesday, zipped and standing in the guest room closet behind the winter coats where Blake would not perceive it, though she understood now that "where Blake would not perceive it" was a phrase without operational meaning. He perceived everything. He probably knew the suitcase was packed. He probably knew it had been packed since Tuesday. He probably knew what was in it and what she had chosen to leave.

He hadn't said anything. That was either mercy or cowardice and she couldn't tell the difference anymore and after ten years the inability to tell the difference was itself a kind of answer.



She moved through the apartment in the dark.

The bedroom first. Blake asleep, the tremor going, always going, one oh seven point three hertz against the sheets, the small perpetual motion that had been the background of her sleep for a decade. She did not look at him for long. Looking was a kind of attending and attending was a kind of hearing and she could not afford to hear anything this morning. Not from him. Not from herself.

She took her things from the dresser. Underwear, socks, the cashmere sweater from the Copenhagen conference that she wore on Saturdays when nobody was watching. She took the framed photograph from her nightstand, the two of them at the Foundation opening, Blake in a suit he hated, Nadia in the blue dress she'd bought specifically because institutional blue commanded a room without competing with the speaker. They were laughing in the photograph. She could not remember what about.

She left the photograph. Put it back. Left it.

From the top drawer: the silver convent ring. Undecorated. Wrapped in the same square of cloth she had wrapped it in nine years ago, on the Tuesday she had set it on the kitchen table and told Blake there would be no wedding ring. The silver had darkened the way silver darkens when nobody touches it, the way silver remembers oxygen. The ring was smaller than she remembered, half a size smaller, because she had been sixteen when Sister Maria Theresa gave it to her and her hands had not yet become the hands of a woman who held things. She put the ring in her coat pocket. The cloth she folded and left in the drawer.

She did not yet put the convent ring on. Not in this house. She would put it on later, in the car, or at the Foundation, or wherever the moment found her, and the ring would fit because grief shrinks everyone and the girl who had left the convent at eighteen was the same girl who was leaving this house at, she stopped counting. The counting was a habit and the habit was a wall and the wall was cracking.



The kitchen.

She put the coffee on. Not for herself, for Blake. The machine, the grind, the settings she had calibrated years ago when Andrew mentioned that 200 degrees Fahrenheit reduced bitterness. She set it to brew at 6:45. He would come downstairs and the coffee would be ready and for one moment, before the empty chair registered, the morning would feel ordinary.

She took the Foundation mug from the cabinet. Left the blue one, the one from their first dinner, the one with the chipped handle that Blake refused to replace because the chip was from the night she told him about the NIH grant and he'd set it down too hard on the counter while saying "Are you serious?" and she'd said "I am always serious" and the mug had cracked the way hearts crack, invisibly, along a line you can't see until someone fills you with something hot and the pressure finds the fault.

She left the blue mug.

She took the Foundation mug and put it with her things and then she stood in the kitchen and did not move for what might have been a minute or might have been five. The light was coming, the first gray light of October, the light before the light, the light that shows you the room as it is without the kindness of full illumination. The kitchen in that light looked like what it was: a room where two people had drunk coffee and argued and lived, lived the way people live when they are too busy living to notice that the room is changing because one of them is already gone.

She wrote a note. On the back of a Foundation envelope, the same kind of envelope she had practiced her new signature on at the Jackson County courthouse, which felt like a century ago and was nine years.

Blake -

She stopped. Stared at the word. His name. His name was the problem and the problem was unsolvable because you cannot ask a man to stop hearing any more than you can ask a building to stop humming.

She crossed out the note. Turned the envelope over. Wrote nothing on the other side.

Left the blank envelope on the counter.

Picked it up. Put it in her purse. Left no note. The absence was the note. She drove to the Foundation.

October morning. Kansas City traffic sparse at 5:40 AM, the occasional truck, the overnight shift workers, the other women who were awake before the sun because the world required them. She drove down Troost. Past the gas station. Past the church where they'd held the first community protocol session. Past the Quality Hill boundary into the institutional district.

The Foundation parking lot was empty. Her spot, the one nearest the door, the one the board had assigned her because she arrived first and left last and the proximity was not a privilege but a diagnosis. She parked.

She sat in the car for eleven minutes.

She knew it was eleven minutes because the dashboard clock said 5:44 when she parked and 5:55 when she opened the door, and in between those numbers she sat with her hands on the steering wheel and her forehead on her hands and she did not cry.

She had cried last night. In the bathroom. With the water running. The water could not mask the sound from a man who heard everything, but the water was the gesture and the gesture was the point. She had cried standing in the shower with her clothes on, which was the posture of a woman who could not afford the vulnerability of being naked while she wept, which was the posture of a woman who had learned in a convent that composure was a garment you wore even when all your other garments were soaked.

But she had cried. And now she was finished.

She opened the car door. The October air was cold. Her coat, the good one, the black one, the coat that made her look like a woman who ran things because she was a woman who ran things, was buttoned to the throat.

She walked into the Foundation. Turned on the lights. The fluorescent hum, one hundred twenty hertz, the institutional heartbeat. The server room LEDs reflecting green off the hallway wall. The building receiving her the way it always received her: without judgment, without perception, without the invasive tenderness of a man who loved her enough to hear her cry through walls.

She sat at her desk.

She opened her laptop. The screen glowed. The inbox, forty-two messages overnight, because the Foundation was global now and the globe did not sleep and the work did not stop and Nadia Volkov, she was using Volkov again in her mind, had been using it for weeks, the maiden name surfacing the way original architecture surfaces when a renovation is torn away. Nadia Volkov did not stop either.

She began the day.

The ring stayed in her coat pocket through the first email. Through the second. Through the seventh. At the fourteenth email, a logistics update from Kofi about the Volta monitoring schedule, the most mundane correspondence in her inbox, she paused. Looked at her bare hand on the keyboard. The hand that had carried no ring for the nine years of her marriage, the bareness that had been the architecture of a Thursday afternoon and a courthouse clerk and a diner on 12th Street.

She reached into her coat pocket.

The convent ring caught the fluorescent light. Silver, oxidised, the band of a girl who had been held by an institution when no person would hold her, and who had spent her life building institutions because institutions were what she understood, and who was now, in the forty-second year of her life, returning to the original architecture.

She slid the silver convent ring onto the finger that had carried no ring for nine years, and the silver fit her finger the way the silver had fit when she was sixteen, the same band, the same circle, the same girl.

The convent ring fit.

She typed the fifteenth email. Her hands were steady. Her heart rate was 67 BPM, she did not need monitoring equipment to know this, she simply knew it, the way every woman knows her own body when her body has decided something and the decision is final.

Outside, the sun was coming up. Institutional light replacing institutional dark. The Foundation opening for another day, and Nadia Volkov opening with it, because the holder holds and the architecture survives and the curriculum works and the work continues and the coffee in the blue mug at the Quality Hill apartment was getting cold and nobody was there to drink it.
