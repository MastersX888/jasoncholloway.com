PROLOGUE
THE WATCHER'S GIFT. BOHEMIA, 1267
109 Hz · Kansas City
I wasn't there. Nobody alive was there. But the manuscripts survived, and the monastery records survived, and the margin notes in Brother Aldric's own hand survived seven centuries of weather and war and institutional forgetting. What follows is my reconstruction, part scholarship, part imagination, part a knowing that happens when you've held the pages a dead man copied and felt the weight of what he carried. I've done my best to honor the evidence. Where the evidence runs out, I've followed the patterns. They have never lied to me yet.
 - Blake Masters, Mýrdalsjökull, Iceland
✦  ⊕  ✦
Restless candlelight guttered.
Brother Aldric steadied his hand. Seven years. Two thousand, five hundred and fifty-six days of transcription. And now, in the small hours before Matins, each final figure.
He dipped the quill in ink that had cost the monastery three months' tithes. Lamp black mixed with iron gall, thickened with dark arabic until it flowed like blood from a fresh wound. The parchment was calf vellum, scraped so thin that candlelight passed through it like stained glass. Forty calves had died for this manuscript. Aldric had blessed each one.
The proportional figure before him was the last of seven hundred and twelve. A circle inscribed within a circle, bisected by lines that met at angles his mathematics could not name. The original had been written by hands not entirely human, and Aldric had long since stopped trying to understand how such precision was possible.
He set the quill to vellum one last time.
The ink flowed. The circle closed. And for a moment the angular pattern seemed to rotate through dimensions the candlelight couldn't explain.
Aldric blinked. The figure was still. Ink on vellum. Nothing more.
And his hands would not stop shaking.
✦  ⊕  ✦
"You came." Aldric didn't turn from the manuscript. "I wondered if you would."
The hooded figure moved into the scriptorium without sound, without disturbing the candle flame. It had entered through a door Aldric had bolted from the inside three hours ago.
When the figure spoke, its words bypassed his ears entirely. They arrived fully formed, like memories of conversations he had never had.
Is it finished?
"The transcription is complete." Aldric turned. The hood revealed nothing, not darkness, not light, simply an absence where a face should be. "But you knew that."
We observe. We do not know. There is a difference.
"Is there?" Aldric gestured at the manuscript. "You gave us this knowledge. You must have known what we would do with it."
We gave you potential. What you do with potential is what makes you interesting.
The figure moved closer. In seven years, Aldric had learned many things about his visitor's kind. They did not harm. They did not help. They watched with patience that made mountains seem impetuous.
But he had also learned they could be surprised.
"I divided it," he said.
The figure went still. Stiller than stone, stiller than death.
Explain.
Aldric pointed to seven leather satchels arranged along the scriptorium wall.
"Seven parts. Seven locations. Prague, where the emperor collects wonders he does not understand. Paris, where the university burns books it cannot control. Rome, where the Holy Father keeps secrets older than his faith. Constantinople, where the libraries still remember Alexandria. Uppsala, where the old gods are not quite forgotten. Toledo, where the translators turn Arabic wisdom into Latin ignorance." He paused. "And one location I will not name. Not even to you."
You scatter what we gave you as one.
"I protect what you gave us." Aldric's voice hardened. "Brother Matthias used the figures last spring. He saw everything. Every pattern, every connection. And it broke him. He clawed out his own eyes trying to make the visions stop." His hands clenched. "I held him while he died."
Some minds are stronger.
"And some are not. And you do not care which is which." Aldric stepped closer than he had ever dared. "You watch. You find us interesting. But you do not protect. That burden falls to us."
They nearly destroyed themselves once. With fragments. What makes you believe scattered pieces are safer than the whole?
"Because the whole is too beautiful." His voice cracked on the word. "The patterns, the geometries. They are the most beautiful things I have ever seen. More beautiful than sunrise over the mountains. More beautiful than my mother's face, which I can barely remember. More beautiful than the face of God, which I have sought my entire life."
He took a breath.
"And humans cannot resist the beautiful. We will seek it out, piece it together, no matter the cost. So I scattered the pieces far enough apart that finding them all will take lifetimes. Centuries. And in that time, perhaps we will become ready."
Or perhaps you will simply delay the inevitable.
"Perhaps." Aldric almost smiled. "But delay is all we have ever had. Delay between birth and death. Between ignorance and wisdom. Between the question and the answer." He turned back to the original. "This is my gift to the future. Not knowledge. Time."
✦  ⊕  ✦
The fire took the manuscript slowly.
The flames licked at its edges, darkened its binding, curled its pages, but the geometric figures at its heart seemed to repel the fire itself.
Then the figures began to move.
Rotating through angles that had no names. The circles spun. Here, the triangles folded through themselves. The manuscript was dying, and in its death it was revealing what it had always contained. Information encoded in proportion, meaning stored in mathematical relationships that transcended the medium that carried them.
The whispers came then. Thousands of voices speaking in mathematics, in patterns that burned themselves into Aldric's vision even as he squeezed his eyes shut.
They were beautiful. They were terrible. They were true.
And then they were ash.
Aldric stood trembling in the smoke. His copies. Seven imperfect echoes. Were packed and sealed. Seven years of work. Each fragment carrying a fraction of the whole. The system demanded unity. His response had been to deny it that unity, to scatter the invitation across the known world and trust that centuries of distance would serve as quarantine until the species could survive the cure.
The figure had not moved.
Someone will reassemble it. Someone always does.
"Then we must pray they are ready." Aldric held out the seventh satchel. "Take this one. Hide it where humans cannot go. Not yet."
We do not interfere.
"You gave us the original. That was interference enough."
For a long moment, nothing moved. The candles burned. The embers cooled. Somewhere in the monastery, a bell began to toll for Matins.
The figure reached out. Fingers that were not quite fingers closed around the satchel. And then the figure was gone, and Aldric was alone in the scriptorium with six remaining packages and a lifetime of questions he would never answer.
✦  ⊕  ✦
He stood at the window until dawn.
Snow had begun, fat flakes that caught the torchlight and turned the world to shifting white. Each one a hexagonal geometry, a crystalline proof that the patterns existed at every scale.
Beyond the gates, wolves circled the treeline. Their eyes caught the light. Amber points moving through the darkness with the patience of creatures that understood time differently than monks did. They sensed the geometry. Not as knowledge. As vibration.
Seven pieces. Seven locations. Perhaps seven centuries before someone found them all. The figures were too beautiful to remain hidden forever. Beauty had a way of surfacing, no matter how deep you buried it.
The question was whether that call would be answered by someone who could survive it.
"May they be worthy," he whispered. Not a prayer. A hope cast forward through time, aimed at strangers who would not be born for generations.
The bell for Matins rang again. Bronze and old.
Aldric gathered his cloak. Checked the first satchel. Prague. Hoisted it onto his shoulder. The weight was less than he expected. Seven years of transcription, and the physical result weighed less than a sack of grain.
The knowledge had always been lighter than its consequences.
He walked out into the snow and the dark and the howling of wolves.
Behind him, in the hollow scriptorium, the last candle guttered and died.
✦  ⊕  ✦
758 years later.

CHAPTER ONE
THE LIBRARY BOY
109 Hz · Kansas City
The corner table at Miller Nichols Library had been Blake Masters' territory for three years.
Back to the wall. Clear sightline to both exits. Close enough to the radiator to stay warm in October, far enough to avoid the clanking when the old pipes kicked on. His grandfather had taught him to choose seats like this. Always know your exits. William Masters had flown reconnaissance aircraft over hostile airspace for twelve years, and the habits he'd brought home from the Cold War had filtered down through two generations like table manners: automatic, inherited, instinct rather than training.
Blake adjusted the Breitling Navitimer on his wrist and turned another page of Foucault's Pendulum. Fourth time through. Some obsessions weren't chosen. Some found you the way water finds cracks. Patiently, inevitably, following the path of least resistance until something gives.
He didn't notice the woman at the table three rows over. Not yet.
She was watching him.
✦  ⊕  ✦
The watch had been his grandfather's. A 1967 Breitling Navitimer, the kind pilots wore when pilots still navigated by the stars. William Masters had bought it the year he left Northrop, the year he stopped flying experimental aircraft and moved his family from California to Missouri for reasons nobody discussed.
Blake's father had worn it next. James Masters, electrical engineer, Missouri infrastructure division. He'd worn it until September 2010, when he'd stopped wearing anything at all.
Now Blake wore it. Too big for his wrist. Heavy with history.
What had his grandfather been running from?
Blake had spent five years of graduate school and one failed security career trying to answer that. All he had were fragments: the watch, some photographs, his father's memories of William taking him to tour McDonnell Douglas, and a growing certainty that somewhere between his grandfather's test flights and his father's nuclear security clearance, the Masters family had stumbled across something that didn't want to be found.
The woman three tables away shifted. Her eyes found his again, held for one beat too long, then slid away.
Blake felt the hair rise on the back of his neck.
✦  ⊕  ✦
She was good. Professional.
Peripheral glances every thirty seconds, never holding eye contact long enough to register as staring. Most people wouldn't have noticed.
Auburn hair, cut practical and short. Green eyes that held intelligence and something harder underneath. Caution, maybe. Or ancient grief. She wore a black turtleneck and no jewelry except a silver ring on her right hand. Her leather bag looked expensive but worn, the kind that had crossed borders and survived searches.
He cataloged all of this without appearing to look up from his book. What do you see? Tell me everything about them without looking directly. William's game.
What Blake saw now was something he hadn't expected. A tension in her shoulders that wasn't tactical. The way her fingers kept returning to the silver ring, turning it. And her eyes, when they found his again across thirty feet of library carpet, not just watching. Searching.
Blake felt the prickling sensation at the base of his skull. The same feeling he'd had at SubTropolis, right before they'd fired him. The same one his father had described in his journal, the week before the accident that wasn't an accident.
Something was about to change.
✦  ⊕  ✦
A cardinal landed on the windowsill.
Male, brilliant red against the gray October afternoon. Blake stared at it, and for a moment he was nine years old again, watching California roll past beneath him at three thousand feet, his grandfather's hands steady on the yoke.
The summer of 1999. Blake's parents had wanted him to really know his grandfather. They'd sent him to California for three months, and William had picked him up at LAX with a hand-lettered sign that said BLAKE and a grin that promised adventure.
Their first flight had been over the coast. William had leveled off at twenty-five hundred feet, the Pacific stretching to the horizon.
"What do you see?"
Blake, nine years old and pressed against the window, had squinted at the white lines forming and reforming below. "Waves?"
"What about the waves?"
He'd looked harder. "They come in sets? Like one, two, three big ones, then smaller ones."
"Good. Now watch where they break. See how the wave height changes with the bottom contour? Shallow water, the wave stands up. Deep water, it rolls past. The ocean floor creates the pattern you see on the surface."
"Most people see the ocean and think it's chaos," William had said. "But there's no chaos in nature, Blake. Only patterns we don't understand yet."
The second flight had been different. Twenty minutes in, William had said the words Blake had been hoping to hear:
"Your airplane."
Blake's hands found the yoke. Suddenly he could feel the aircraft. Every shift in the air transmitted directly into his palms.
"Gentle pressure. You're not wrestling it. You're asking it to dance."
Blake nudged the yoke right and the world tilted. Mountains sliding beneath them, the horizon canting, his stomach floating.
"Now level it out. Feel when it wants to stop turning."
And Blake had felt it. That moment when the forces balanced and the world returned to horizontal. Not something William told him. Something his hands knew.
"You're a natural," William had said, and Blake had heard something beyond pride. Recognition.
They'd flown over the Mojave their third week. High desert, forty-five hundred feet. William pointed down at the dry riverbeds branching across the valley floor.
"What do you see?"
By now, Blake knew better than the obvious. "Arteries and veins. All branching out."
"The path of least resistance. But look closer. See how the angles repeat? The same branching pattern at different scales?"
Blake stared. The same angles, the same ratios, in channels a hundred feet wide and in tiny erosion patterns barely visible from altitude.
"A river does it," William continued. "Lightning does it. Blood vessels in the hand achieve it. Trees do it. That's not just least resistance. That's mathematics expressing itself through nature."
"Why does nature keep using the same pattern?"
"That is the question I've been asking my whole life."
Something in William's voice had made Blake look over. His grandfather was staring at the desert, but seeing something else entirely.
"When I flew test planes," William said quietly, "I saw things. Patterns in cloud formations that didn't match weather reports. Geometric shapes in rock formations too precise to be natural. Once, at fifty thousand feet, I watched a thunderstorm from above and saw..." He paused. "Something I couldn't explain."
"What was it?"
"I don't know. I took photographs, filed reports. The engineers said ice crystals. The meteorologists said lenticular clouds. But I knew what I saw."
Silence. Then: "Most people see the land and move on. But you. You see the pattern and want to understand. That curiosity. That's the beginning of wisdom."
"Will I ever understand? All of it?"
"No. Nobody understands all of it. But you'll see more than most. You'll ask better questions." Quiet. "That's not always a blessing, Blake. Sometimes the questions are heavier than the answers."
Blake hadn't understood. Nine years old, flying over the desert with his grandfather. He'd thought William meant academic questions.
He hadn't known his grandfather was preparing him for something else entirely.
✦  ⊕  ✦
The cardinal flew away. Blake blinked, surfacing from twenty-six years of memory.
The woman with auburn hair was gone.
She'd disappeared while he was three thousand feet above the Mojave. Which meant she was better than good.
She was very, very good.
✦  ⊕  ✦
The apartment was small. A studio in Westport, third floor walkup.
Three photographs hung on the wall. William at Northrop, young and confident, standing next to an aircraft blacked out with government markers. James at Oscar-01, 2009, shaking hands with an Air Force colonel. Blake himself in his G4S uniform, SubTropolis badge visible, a thick black X drawn through it with permanent marker.
Three generations of men who'd worked in places with secrets. Two of them dead.
Blake looked at the middle photograph the longest. His father's face in that picture was the public face, the handshake, the uniform collar, the professional smile aimed at someone holding a camera. But Blake remembered a different face. The one James wore on the back deck of the Jefferson City house on Thursday evenings, when he'd set up the soldering station on the picnic table and build shortwave radios from surplus parts.
Blake was thirteen. He'd come outside with his geometry homework and a glass of the terrible lemonade his mother made from powder because she refused to pay four dollars for lemons. James had a circuit board clamped in a vise, a thin curl of solder smoke rising between them.
"You're making that look harder than it is," James had said without looking up.
"It's proofs. They want me to prove a triangle is a triangle."
"That's not what they want. They want you to show your reasoning. Proofs aren't about the answer. They're about the path."
James had set down the iron. He smelled the way he always smelled on Thursday evenings, solder flux, coffee, the cedar soap he used because Lorraine said the regular soap gave him a rash. The specific combination of a specific man on a specific evening, unreproducible, the kind of detail that disappeared from the world when the person did and could never be recovered from photographs or letters or the careful language of obituaries.
"Here." James had taken the pencil. Not to solve it, to draw a radio schematic beside the triangle. "You know what a circuit is?"
"Electricity going in a loop."
"Electricity following a path. The path is the proof. You don't prove the triangle exists. You prove why it has to exist. The angles don't just add up to 180 degrees. They can't not." He'd tapped the schematic. "Same with a circuit. Current doesn't choose to flow. The path makes it inevitable."
He'd grinned, the private grin, the one that wasn't for cameras. The one that made the skin around his eyes crease in a way that said he was delighted by the elegance of something, and that the delight was a gift he wanted to hand to his son the way you hand someone a glass of water. Here. This is good. Drink.
Blake had finished the proofs in twenty minutes. James had finished the radio. They'd sat on the deck after dark, tuning through shortwave frequencies, hearing weather reports from ships in the Gulf of Mexico and ham operators in Manitoba and, once, a burst of numbers in Russian that made James go quiet for a long time.
"Dad?"
"Just listening." James's hand on the dial, steady. "Some signals, you pick up by accident. Some are aimed at you." He'd looked at Blake with an expression that wasn't the grin anymore. Something older and sadder. "The trick is knowing the difference."
Blake hadn't understood. Thirteen years old, lemonade from powder, the cicadas starting in the hackberry tree. He'd thought his father was talking about radio.
The photograph on the wall showed none of this. It showed the handshake. The uniform. The professional distance that official photography required. It didn't show the solder smoke or the crease around his eyes or the particular way he'd handed Blake the pencil, handle first, the way you hand someone a knife, which was the way James did everything: considering the other person's grip before his own.
A wooden box sat on the shelf below. Oak, brass clasp oxidized to green. Inside: a copy of Don Quixote his grandfather had given him at twelve, the margins dense with handwritten notes Blake had never read carefully.
The phone rang.
Blake let it ring. He was thinking about the SD card hidden behind his medicine cabinet mirror. Thirty-seven photographs of geometric carvings in a section of SubTropolis that didn't appear on any official map. The section he'd been fired for entering.
The carvings shouldn't have existed. SubTropolis was a limestone mine converted to underground storage. The main facility dated to the 1960s. But the tunnel Blake had found went deeper. Into bedrock that predated the limestone. Into rock carved with patterns that made his eyes water when he looked at them too long. The same branching angles from William's Cessna. The same proportions, a hundred feet underground.
The same patterns. Different altitude.
The phone stopped ringing. Then started again.
Blake picked up.
✦  ⊕  ✦
"Mr. Blake Masters?" A woman's voice, professional. "Patricia Thornton, attorney with Pemberton and Associates in Jefferson City."
Jefferson City. His father's hometown.
"This concerns your grandfather's estate. There's been a development."
"My grandfather died in 2003. His estate was settled years ago."
"First National Bank recently underwent renovations. They discovered a safety deposit box not in their main system. Established in 1968. Rental paid through 2025. Fifty-seven years in advance."
Blake's hand tightened on the phone.
"The box lists only one authorized heir. Not your father. Not your mother. You."
1968. The year William stopped flying test planes and moved his family to Missouri and never explained why. He'd paid for a box fifty-seven years in advance, timed to arrive at just this moment.
Before Blake was delivered. Before his father was born. William had set this in motion, aimed at a grandson who did not yet exist.
Which meant William had known. About the patterns. About everything that would happen to James, and everything that would happen to Blake.
"Mr. Masters? Are you still there?"
"Tomorrow," Blake said. "I'll be there tomorrow."
✦  ⊕  ✦
He didn't sleep.
Blake sat in the dark, looking at the photographs. William. James. Himself. Three men with the same eyes, the same jaw, the same curse of seeing things others walked past.
The third was about to open a box that had been waiting since before he was born.
Geometric motifs swam behind his closed eyes. The SubTropolis photographs. Circles within circles, triangles at impossible angles. Branching patterns like rivers, like lightning, like the Mojave washes from three thousand feet.
He'd spent four years telling himself they were erosion, water damage, the mind finding signal in noise. The same way his father had probably written off the anomalies at Oscar-01, the same way William had probably told himself what he saw from the cockpit were optical illusions.
But they weren't noise. They weren't illusions.
They were beautiful. And terrible. And deliberately carved by hands that understood something about geometry that modern mathematics had forgotten.
The question isn't whether they're there. The question is whether you're ready to see them.
Tomorrow. He would drive to Jefferson City. Open his grandfather's safety deposit box. Start finding answers to questions that had killed his father and driven his grandfather from the sky.
The Breitling caught the streetlight. A pilot's watch on a pilot's grandson, still ticking forward toward whatever William had planned for this moment.
Blake stood and walked to the window.
Whatever altitude they appeared at.

CHAPTER TWO
SISTER NADIA
109 Hz · Kansas City
The campus coffee shop occupied a converted Victorian house at the edge of the UMKC quad.
He'd chosen his usual booth. Wall behind him, clear sightline to the door. Old habits. The same positioning that had made Sarah call him exhausting before she'd left six months ago with a speech about how she couldn't live with someone who treated every coffee shop like a potential ambush site.
She'd been wrong about the paranoia being irrational.
She'd just been right that it was exhausting.
She moved too quietly. The floorboards should have announced her. But the creaking came a half-second late, as if the wood itself had been surprised to find her already inside.
Auburn hair. Green eyes. The same woman from the library yesterday, the one who'd watched him with professional patience and disappeared without him seeing her leave.
Closer now, she looked different. Younger. The intelligence was still there, the caution, the old grief. But there was something else. A restlessness, a coiled energy that wasn't quite anxiety. The energy of someone who'd been waiting for something a long time and could feel it arriving.
"You're very careful about where you sit," she said, and sat down across from him without waiting for an invitation.
Blake didn't reach for his coffee. "Most people knock."
"I'm not most people." She set her leather bag on the seat beside her. Up close, the stitching had been repaired at one corner with thread that didn't quite match. A bag that had been loved, not just used. "Sister Nadia Volkov. I'm researching medieval manuscripts at the university."
She flagged the barista without looking. Raised two fingers. Tea arrived two minutes later, loose leaf, something herbal that smelled like chamomile and something sharper underneath. Not coffee. Tea said patience. Ritual. The kind of person who'd learned to wait for things to steep.
"Sister?" Blake glanced at her clothes. Black turtleneck, dark jeans, silver ring on her right hand. No habit. No cross.
"The order is informal. I took vows, Mr. Masters." A slight smile, sad around the edges. "I just don't advertise them."
"You know my name."
"I know many things." She studied him with eyes that held too much knowledge for someone who looked barely thirty. "Your academic work on medieval grimoires. Your time at SubTropolis. What happened to your security clearance." She tilted her head. "I know your grandfather was more than a pilot. And I know you already suspect that."
Blake's hand found his coffee cup. Not to drink. His throat had gone too tight. But to hold something steady. She'd compressed what she knew into implications rather than recitation, and that was more unsettling than a full dossier. It meant she understood which details mattered.
"Whatever's in that safety deposit box," Nadia said, leaning forward, "it's not family mementos. And the people who killed your father would very much like to know what's inside."
The words hit Blake like a physical blow. The people who killed your father.
"My father died in a car accident."
"Your father died on a stretch of road he'd driven a thousand times, in perfect weather, at two in the afternoon. Three days after he started asking questions about anomalies at Oscar-01. Three days after he filed a FOIA request for construction documents classified above his clearance level."
She pulled a slim folder from her bag, slid it across the table.
"No skid marks. No mechanical failure. No witnesses. The official report called it 'driver error.' A man with twenty years of perfect driving losing control on a straight road in broad daylight." She leaned back. "Do you believe that, Mr. Masters?"
Blake looked at the folder. Looked at Nadia. Her green eyes were steady but not cold. There was something underneath. Pain, the kind you learn to navigate around but never quite through. Her hands on the table weren't entirely still.
She was afraid of something too.
"No," Blake said quietly. "I don't believe that."
Nadia nodded. Not triumph. Relief. As if she'd been carrying this knowledge alone for a long time and was genuinely grateful to share the weight.
"Good," she said. "Then we have something to talk about."
✦  ⊕  ✦
The model aircraft hung from William's garage ceiling on fishing line so fine they seemed to float.
SR-71 Blackbird. U-2 Dragon Lady. P-38 Lightning. A-12 Oxcart. Blake had helped build each one over multiple summers, following William's instructions about angles and proportions that mattered more than they should for model airplanes in a garage.
But William had been precise about everything. The angle of each aircraft relative to the others. Here, the direction they faced. The height at which they hung. He'd used a protractor and a level and strings to ensure every measurement was exact.
"Why does it matter where they point?" Blake had asked. Eleven or twelve, that last summer before everything changed.
William had stood on a stepladder, adjusting the P-38's angle. "Hidden in plain sight. The best secrets are the ones people see every day without understanding."
"Is there a treasure map on your ceiling?"
William had laughed. The sound stuck in Blake's memory like a splinter, sharp and precious. "Maybe. Would you know how to read it?"
Blake had spent the rest of that summer studying the aircraft. The SR-71 pointed northwest. The U-2 northeast. The P-38 southwest. The A-12 southeast. Perfect cardinal directions, or close enough that the deviation seemed intentional.
He'd given up after three weeks. William had watched him quit with sadness mixed with patience. The look of a man who knew his grandson wasn't ready yet.
Now Blake wondered if he was finally ready to read the map.
✦  ⊕  ✦
"The safety deposit box." Blake's voice was steadier than he felt. "How do you know about that?"
"I know you're planning to drive to Jefferson City tomorrow. And I know you won't be the only one interested in what your grandfather left behind."
She reached into her bag and produced a plain white card. No logo. Just a phone number in black type, and below it, two words: WHEN READY.
"Whatever's in that box, don't tell anyone you found it," Nadia said. "And don't go alone."
"Why should I trust you?"
"You shouldn't." Her smile again, not performed. Lived-in. "Trust no one. Question everything. Verify before you believe. It's the only thing that's kept your family alive this long."
She walked away without looking back. Her footsteps made no sound on the floorboards that had announced Blake like a herald and let her pass like a ghost.
Blake sat in the booth, holding the card, trying to understand why he felt seen. For the first time in years, someone had looked at him and recognized what they were looking at.
✦  ⊕  ✦
Her paper trail was perfect. Too perfect.
Blake spent four hours hunting through databases and archives, building a picture of Sister Nadia Volkov that should have reassured him and instead made his skin prickle with the awareness that everything he was finding had been designed to be found.
Born 1994, Lviv, Ukraine. Orphaned at six. Raised by the Sisters of the Holy Mother, a convent outside Kyiv. Immigrated at sixteen. Full scholarship to Georgetown. PhD in Medieval History at twenty-five.
Her dissertation: "Cognitive Transformation in Medieval Grimoires: The Notory Arts Reconsidered." The thesis argued that medieval grimoires were cognitive technologies. Systematic attempts to enhance human perception through geometric visualization.
She'd researched exactly what Blake had experienced in the tunnels beneath SubTropolis. The patterns that had made his vision swim and his thoughts accelerate.
Every detail confirmed. Every fact checkable. Perfect the way a legend is perfect. The way a cover identity is perfect when built by people who know what questions investigators will ask.
But he couldn't stop thinking about her smile. The lived-in sadness of it. The calloused fingertips. The repaired bag stitching. Small details that didn't fit a manufactured identity.
Either Sister Nadia Volkov was telling the truth, or she was very, very good at lying.
Blake looked at the card. WHEN READY.
Was he ready?
✦  ⊕  ✦
He left before dawn. The Missouri River Runner. Blake needed to be early, needed to walk the platform and watch who else was traveling east.
The gray sedan was parked outside the station. Same car from yesterday, same tinted windows. They weren't trying to hide.
The train followed the river. October morning. Harvested corn fields stretching to the horizon. Abandoned barns listing toward collapse. Small towns appearing and disappearing. Main Streets with more vacant storefronts than open businesses.
Missouri in autumn. Luminous and dying.
He thought about Nadia's dissertation. About cognitive technologies and geometric enhancement. He thought about her hands. The way she'd said your grandfather with something closer to reverence than clinical interest.
Don't go alone, she'd said.
But Blake was alone. Had always been. Isolated by questions he couldn't stop asking. Sarah had left because it was exhausting. She'd been right.
Except they weren't coincidences.
The Missouri State Capitol dome appeared through the window. Jefferson City. A gray sedan idled in the station lot, different plates, same make, same tinted windows.
They'd been waiting for him.
Blake walked to First National Bank, carrying his grandfather's watch on his wrist and his father's questions in his head.
And Nadia's card in his pocket, warm from being held too long.

CHAPTER THREE
THE BOX
109 Hz · Kansas City
Blake followed Patricia Thornton down a hallway marked PRIVATE. His hands were steady. The rest of him was not.
"Box 1847," Patricia said, unlocking a heavy door marked VAULT with a key and a code. "Established in 1968. Rental paid through December 2025, fifty-seven years in advance." She led him into a room lined with brass doors, the air cool and dry. "It would have lapsed in two months. But your grandfather was very specific in his instructions. Only one authorized heir."
She pulled a card from her pocket. The handwriting was instantly recognizable. William's precise script, the same hand that had filled the margins of the Don Quixote Blake still kept on his nightstand.
For Blake Masters. When he is ready.
His hands started shaking.
Patricia inserted two keys and turned them at once. The door swung open with a whisper of hinges that hadn't moved in decades.
A metal box, approximately twelve by eighteen by six inches deep. Patricia lifted it out, set it on the table.

Then she paused. Looked at the pen. Looked at Blake.
"Done," the clerk said. "Congratulations." She said it the way she said it twenty times a week, kindly, automatically, the warmth genuine but distributed thin, like butter scraped over too much bread.
They took the elevator down. In the lobby, Nadia stopped at a bench. She sat down and took an envelope from her bag, a Foundation envelope, already stamped, addressed to the IRS in the handwriting of a woman who was always three steps ahead of every administrative requirement in her life.
She turned it over. On the blank back, with the county ballpoint she'd pocketed without thinking, she wrote: Nadia Katarína Masters.
She looked at it. Crossed it out. Wrote: N. K. Masters.
Crossed that out. Wrote: Dr. Nadia Masters.
"You're practicing your signature," Blake said.
"I am practicing my signature." She did not look up. She wrote it again, Nadia Masters, and this time she did not cross it out. She looked at the name. Two words. The Slovak first name, the American surname. The combination that sounded like two countries agreeing on a border.
"It's a good name," Blake said.
"It's your name."
"It's yours now."
She put the envelope in her bag. Stood up. Took his hand, the trembling hand, the 111.2 Hz hand, the hand she would hold through every chamber and every hearing and every 3 AM kitchen floor for the rest of their lives.
"I'm hungry," she said.
"There's a diner on 12th."
"I know. I checked."
Of course she had.
They walked out of the courthouse into Thursday afternoon. October light, warm for Kansas City, the kind of weather that made you forget winter was coming. No ceremony. No rings. No witnesses. Just a form, a clerk, a lobby signature, and a diner on 12th Street where they sat in a booth and she ordered a club sandwich and he ordered soup and they were married.
The soup was adequate. The sandwich was good. Nadia ate the pickle.
That was the wedding.
