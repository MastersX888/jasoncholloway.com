Chapter Five. Yuki's World.

One hundred nine hertz. Kansas City.

[short pause]

The Price Chopper on 39th Street smelled the way all Price Choppers smell, the industrial-sweet composite of floor cleaner and produce mist and the particular staleness of refrigeration, the olfactory signature of a building designed to preserve food and present it under conditions that would make you want to buy it even when you weren't hungry.

Yuki Tanaka pushed her cart through the produce section and perceived everything.

The fluorescent lights: one hundred twenty hertz, the standard ballast frequency, the sound that most people stopped hearing after thirty seconds and that Yuki had not been able to stop hearing for two and a half years. The HVAC: forty-eight hertz, cycling every fourteen minutes, the system's thermostat operating on a duty cycle she could track by the changes in the standing wave pattern she could see, actually, visually see, on the reflective surface of the wet produce she was selecting. The produce mist: a zero point five hertz pulse, the automated sprayers activating every two minutes, the droplets catching the fluorescent light and producing brief prismatic arcs that Yuki's visual cortex decoded into frequency bands like a living spectrometer.

A toddler crying in aisle seven. Approximately four hundred forty hertz, A4, the concert tuning frequency, the pitch that orchestras tune to and that human infants accidentally produce when they are hungry or frightened or simply insisting on the continued existence of their needs. The toddler's cry contained harmonics, the second harmonic at 880, the third at 1320, the child's vocal cords producing a frequency series that was mathematically perfect and emotionally devastating and utterly ordinary.

Yuki selected a bunch of broccoli. The broccoli did not have a frequency. The broccoli was a vegetable.

But the hand that held it did.

[pause]

She had been in continuous perception for two and a half years.

It started during a Foundation cohort session, a planned, monitored, clinically supervised exposure to the three thirty-three point six hertz harmonic in the basalt chamber. She was Cohort 4, the musician, the oboist whose ears had been trained to perceive pitch differences of less than one hertz. Andrew had flagged her as a candidate because her auditory discrimination scores were in the 99.8th percentile. She entered the chamber and her perceptual threshold opened and it did not close.

Two and a half years. Every surface showed wave interference patterns, the cymatics were constant, visible on water, on glass, on the surface of her coffee, on the chrome fixtures in her bathroom, on the windshield of her car when it rained. Every voice carried a frequency signature she could read the way literate people read text: automatically, continuously, without the option to not read. She knew her mail carrier's stress level (elevated, three hundred forty hertz dominant, throat constriction indicating chronic tension) the way she knew his name (Carl). She knew the barista's mood (contented, two hundred twenty hertz, warm and open) the way she knew the barista's coffee order (oat latte, extra shot).

She was not suffering. She wanted this to be clear, because the clinical literature on "persistent perceptual changes" used vocabulary, "flashbacks," "hallucinatory," "post-exposure syndrome", that framed the experience as pathological. It was not pathological. It was the opposite of pathological: it was clarity. She was perceiving the world as it was, not as the brain's filtering systems typically presented it. She saw the signal without the noise reduction.

She was saturated.

[pause]

She had started keeping a journal. Not a scientific document, she left the clinical tracking to Andrew, who maintained her quarterly EEG assessments with the meticulous devotion of a man who had developed a fondness for her data that bordered on the aesthetic. Her journal was domestic. Personal. Written in the voice of a woman who was trying to describe what it was like to live inside a miracle while simultaneously needing to do laundry.

Tuesday. Bought groceries. The checkout clerk's voice: strained, three hundred forty hertz dominant, throat constriction indicating chronic stress. I considered telling her. I didn't. What would I say? "Your voice tells me you're exhausted"? She knows she's exhausted. She doesn't need a frequency reader. She needs a day off.

Wednesday. The elm outside my window is producing approximately eight hundred hertz in the wind, turbulence-dependent, rising when gusts exceed 20 mph. I used to find this distracting. Now it's weather. Acoustic weather. It tells me what the wind is doing without my looking outside. I have become a person who checks the tree before the forecast.

Thursday. I can see the kingdom. I live in the kingdom. I still have to do my laundry in the kingdom.

[pause]

Coffee with Marcus. Mud Pie Vegan Bakery on Grand Boulevard, the café where the espresso machine produced a steam wand frequency of four thousand two hundred hertz (too high for most people, perfectly audible to Yuki, and not unpleasant, the way a soprano note is not unpleasant even when it's loud) and the background music was always Radiohead played at a volume low enough to suggest sophistication and loud enough to identify Thom Yorke's fundamental vocal frequency (approximately one hundred eighty hertz, tenor, with a vibrato width of exactly two hertz).

Marcus sat across from her with the posture of a man who was doing fine. And he was, he was doing fine. The synesthesia was gone. The colors that had once attached themselves to every sound he heard, the colors that had made him one of the most gifted audio engineers in the country, the secret gift that his nervous system had metabolized during the 72-hour demonstration, were gone, and in their place was a perception that was, as he described it, "quieter." He heard frequencies without seeing them. He heard the world in monochrome where he used to hear it in color.

He did not miss the colors every day. But he missed them.

"The patterns aren't stopping," Yuki said.

"Will they?"

"I don't know. They were supposed to. Sabrina's model predicted a three-month integration period, the nervous system absorbs the perceptual expansion and normalizes it, the way the eyes adjust to a dark room. Three months. That was twenty-seven months ago."

Marcus drank his coffee. He had a way of drinking coffee that Yuki found comforting, slow, deliberate, the sip of a man who was paying attention to the act of drinking rather than performing it unconsciously. The mindfulness that some people achieve through meditation, Marcus achieved through having lost his colors and learning to pay attention to what remained.

"The patterns will stop when you stop needing them," he said.

"I'm not sure I need them. I think they're just on."

"Then maybe 'on' is your baseline. Maybe you're not expanded. Maybe you're just you."

She considered this. On the surface of her coffee, a Radiohead guitar riff was producing a visible interference pattern, concentric rings, like a stone dropped in still water, except the stone was sound and the water was a four-dollar oat latte.

"Is Blake 'just Blake' with the tremor?"

"Yes. Sabrina said so. The tremor isn't a side effect. It's who he is now."

"Then this is who I am. The woman who sees sound. In grocery stores. In coffee shops. While buying broccoli."

She smiled. The smile was real, unforced, unclinical, the kind of smile that happens when you find the comedy in your own condition, when you see the absurdity of a woman who can perceive the fundamental resonance of a Paleolithic cave who is sitting in a vegan bakery in Kansas City worrying about whether the persistent perception of acoustic wave interference in her oat latte constitutes a medical condition or a lifestyle.

[pause]

The laundry.

Yuki's apartment was on the second floor of a duplex on Holmes, three blocks south of the Foundation. The washing machine was in the basement, shared with her downstairs neighbor, a retired electrician named Dennis who left his socks in the dryer and who Yuki liked because Dennis did not ask questions and did not make small talk and produced a resting vocal frequency of one hundred eighty hertz that was warm and uncomplicated and sounded, to Yuki's perpetually calibrated ears, like someone who was exactly where he wanted to be.

She loaded the washing machine. The agitator engaged. Normal wash, warm cycle, the machine's motor producing a fundamental of sixty hertz, the AC line frequency, grid power, the hum of American electricity that most people never heard because most people's auditory cortex had been filtering it since birth. Yuki could not filter it. She heard the grid everywhere, in walls, in appliances, in the transformers on the power lines outside her window. She heard the American electrical grid the way fish hear water: as the medium itself, as the invisible substance in which everything else happened.

The wash cycle: eighteen minutes. The water filling: two thousand four hundred hertz from the intake valve, dropping to two hundred hertz when the basin was full. The agitator: one point three hertz, a slow pendulum, the clothes moving in the soapy water with a rhythm that was almost biological, almost the rhythm of breathing, and that Yuki knew was actually the rhythm of a 1,725 RPM motor geared down through a transmission ratio that converted industrial rotation into domestic oscillation.

She sat on the basement step and listened to her laundry.

Not because she wanted to. Because tuning it out required more effort than listening, and she was tired, and sometimes being tired meant letting the kingdom in rather than holding it at arm's length. The detergent bubbles: each one a spherical resonance chamber, the soap film vibrating at frequencies determined by the bubble's diameter, the smaller bubbles producing higher tones, the larger bubbles producing lower tones, the wash water a symphony of soap-film resonators that no human being had ever heard before and that Yuki heard now, clearly, the way you hear a radio in the next room, present, identifiable, not selected but not avoidable.

The spin cycle was worse. Or better, she could never decide. 1,200 RPM extracting water through centrifugal force, the drum producing a bass frequency of approximately twenty hertz that she felt in her pelvic floor and her solar plexus simultaneously, the sub-audible vibration of a machine converting her cotton T-shirts into damp cylinders at a rotation speed that, in a clinical setting, would require a consent form.

She moved the clothes to the dryer. The dryer's drum: three point seven hertz, the tumble frequency, the rate at which a household appliance produced a rhythm indistinguishable from the Schumann resonance she had felt in the basalt caves. She was drying her socks in a chamber that produced the earth's fundamental frequency. She had a dryer that did what the cathedrals did.

She wrote in her journal, sitting on the basement step while her clothes dried at three point seven hertz:

Saturday. The dryer produces the Schumann resonance. I am drying my towels at the same frequency that Chartres produces. I should tell Andrew. I will not tell Andrew. Andrew will want to measure my dryer.

The dryer beeped. two thousand eight hundred hertz, the alert frequency, the signal that the cycle was complete. She pulled the towels out. Warm. The lint trap: full, the accumulated fibers carrying a static charge of approximately 800 volts per square centimeter, the kind of voltage that made the hair on her forearms stand up and that she perceived not as static but as a micro-field, a tiny electrical atmosphere clinging to the dryer's exhaust vent.

She folded the towels. Each fold compressed the fibers, changing the resonance properties of the fabric, a folded towel had a different acoustic signature than an unfolded towel, the same way a folded piece of paper had a different vibration mode than a flat sheet. She could perceive the difference. She could perceive the acoustic difference between a towel folded in thirds and a towel folded in halves.

She did not catalog this difference. She did not write it in the journal. She folded the towel and placed it on the stack and reached for the next one.

The towel was warm. She let it be warm.

[pause]

She walked home. January in Kansas City, the air sharp, the elms bare, the particular quality of winter light that made the city look like an architectural drawing, all lines and angles and the shadows that lines and angles produce.

She did not identify the wind frequency in the elm branches. She heard it, approximately eight hundred hertz, she knew this, it was there, the information was available the way the color of the sky is available to anyone who looks up, but she did not process it. She let it remain unanalyzed. She let the wind be wind.

This was the practice. Not the three-minute practice that Blake taught in his classroom. Not the 52-week preparation protocol. Yuki's practice was the opposite: the practice of not perceiving. The practice of letting the kingdom be the kingdom without reading its frequency signature. The practice of walking through a world that was always singing and choosing, for a few minutes at a time, to not transcribe the song.

She walked. She heard the wind. She saw the patterns on every surface, the cymatics on store windows, the interference patterns on puddles, the standing waves in the columns of exhaled breath that Kansas City produced on cold January days when the architecture of a city becomes visible in vapor.

She saw all of it. She let all of it be.

A dog barked. three hundred forty hertz. She smiled at the dog. The dog did not care about its frequency. The dog cared about the squirrel.

Yuki Tanaka walked home through the kingdom, carrying broccoli.
