### BEAT 1 — Malta (0:50–2:45)

> The Ħal-Saflieni Hypogeum: a Neolithic temple complex cut from limestone, roughly 3600 to 2500 BCE, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The Oracle Chamber's geometry — a roughly oval space under a curved limestone ceiling — produces strong standing waves around 110 to 111 hertz. Male voices chanting in that range create an intense reverberant effect. That's the measured core. The 2008 study — Cook, Pajot, Leuchter — reported a prefrontal shift at resonance: one study, small sample. Grade: *measured, thin*.

[OST: `Oracle Chamber · ~110 Hz`]

### BEAT 2 — Britain and France (2:45–4:45)

> Malta is not alone. Aaron Watson and David Keating measured resonances at Camster Round in Scotland. Paul Devereux and colleagues surveyed Neolithic chambers across Britain and Ireland — chambered tombs, stone circles — and found peak resonances consistently in the 95-to-120-hertz band. Published acoustics, real journals.
>
> And older still: Lascaux. Iegor Reznikoff and Michel Dauvois observed that Palaeolithic cave paintings cluster disproportionately in zones of high acoustic resonance — published in 1988. Compelling; not universally accepted. Grade: *measured, contested interpretation*.

[OST: `95–120 Hz · Britain · Ireland · France`]

### BEAT 3 — The question nobody can answer (4:45–6:15)

> Now the honest center of the whole literature: intentionality. Limestone chambers at human scale produce standing waves in this band the way an organ pipe of a given length produces a given pitch — geometry and material. Did Neolithic builders design for it? Discover it and build in response? Or is it a byproduct of building with stone at the scale of human bodies?
>
> The record, in 2026, does not resolve that question. And the claims that travel furthest online — 111 hertz switching off the prefrontal cortex, activating spiritual states — are the least supported part of the story. What's actually documented is narrower, and stranger for it.
