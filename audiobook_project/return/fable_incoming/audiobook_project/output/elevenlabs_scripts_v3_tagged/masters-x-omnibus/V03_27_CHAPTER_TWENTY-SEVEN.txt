Chapter Twenty-Seven. Mýrdalsjökull.

[short pause]

April. The same flight he had taken nine times before. KEF in the early afternoon, the volcanic light coming through the airplane window with a clarity that no other latitude produced, the air refracting the way only Icelandic air refracted because there was so little of it between the eye and the geology.

He took a taxi from the airport. The driver asked where he was going. Blake told him the address. The driver did not pretend to recognize it. Some addresses in Iceland are not recognized; they are simply found, by the people who need to find them, by the patient logic of a country that has decided not to label what it does not need to label.

The cottage was where he had left it. The wind too. The lava field too. The black expanse with its small green textures of moss and lichen, the same moss that had been making its slow argument with the basalt for ten thousand years, an argument the moss had been winning at a rate of approximately one square centimeter per decade.

He paid the driver. Stood in the gravel of the drive with his bag at his feet. The cottage was unlocked, the way it had been unlocked for seven years, the way it would remain unlocked. There was no one to take what was inside. Or rather, there was no one who had not already taken what they had needed.

He went inside.

[pause]

The desk.

The same desk. The wood had not warped. The wind had not moved it. The lamp Andrew had wired into the surge protector was still where Andrew had left it. The small oak table beside the desk, scarred by a thousand French-press condensations, held the envelope with its handwritten note about the cave coordinates and the deactivated monitors, exactly where Andrew had placed it five months ago.

Blake looked at the envelope but did not open it. He had read the note already, two days after Andrew shut the facility down, when Andrew had emailed him a photograph of the table and the note and the envelope, the way Andrew did everything, with the redundancy of a man who understood that important things must be witnessed twice to be real.

The desk was dust. Five months of dust from a cottage in a lava field where the wind brought volcanic particulate through every seam in the windows and where no one had been present to wipe.

Blake wiped the desk with his sleeve. The dust came up in a small cloud, a brief geological cloud, the residue of pulverised basalt and aeolian transport and whatever else the wind had brought to this room while the room was empty.

The Breitling on his wrist ticked at eight hertz. Constant. Reliable. The mechanism his grandfather had wound every morning at the bathroom sink in Kansas City, the watch he had carried from a cottage in Iceland to a kitchen in Quality Hill and back again, the third watch in an inheritance whose first watch had been a pocket-piece William had bought used in 1953 and given to James in 1979 and that had stopped working a decade before James died, in a drawer, where it remained.

Blake unfastened the strap. Took the watch off. Held it against the lamplight.

The Breitling was running. The hands were at 4:18. The present time, Reykjavík time, the time he had set the watch to in November when he wound it again in the Quality Hill kitchen.

He set it on the desk. Face up. Crystal up. Where it had been before, in December, before he had come back for it.

He did not wind it.

He understood, now, that the watch's mainspring would carry it for another twenty-eight hours, give or take, the standard power reserve of the Navitimer 8, before the spring would exhaust itself and the second hand would stop. He would not be here for that. He would be in the cottage tonight; tomorrow morning he would drive into Vík for paper and ink; tomorrow night he would write at this desk. The day after, perhaps the day after that, the watch would run down without him. The cottage would receive the silence. The silence would be the same silence that had received the watch the first time it stopped, on the December night the grimoire was sealed, except this time the silence would not need a witness. The watch did not require him. It never had.

[pause]

He went to the bookshelf. The grimoire was not there. The grimoire was in Kansas City, in the wooden Icelandic-birch box Andrew had commissioned, on the floor of his study, where it would remain. The grimoire was sealed. He had not opened it since closing it.

What he was here to write was not the grimoire.

He had brought a stack of paper from a stationer in Kansas City. Heavy paper, archival, eighty-pound, cream, unlined. The kind of paper a man buys when he understands that what he is about to write is going to outlast him and he wants the paper to honor the writing. He had wrapped the stack in butcher's twine, the way his grandfather wrapped the field journals he never published, the way William had wrapped everything that mattered: tied, knotted twice, set down on the corner of the desk where the dog could not reach.

He set the paper on the desk beside the Breitling. He sat in the chair he had sat in for eleven months while writing what became Volume One, and again for two months last winter while sealing what became Volume Two, and now, today, in the spring of his fifty-second year, while beginning what would become Volume Three.

He took out a pen. Not the Lamy 2000 — that pen was in Kansas City, on the kitchen table, where he had left it beside the closed Moleskine. He had a different pen for this. A Pilot. A working pen. A pen for prose, not for tremor-record.

He untied the butcher's twine. Laid the first sheet of paper on the desk. The lamp light caught the cream surface and turned it, briefly, to the colour of vellum.

He thought of Brother Aldric.

He thought of the figure Aldric had argued with — the hooded one, the one whose fingers were not quite fingers, whose voice arrived in the mind already-formed. Blake looked at the corner of the cottage where the dark gathered between the bookshelf and the wall, and saw nothing, and could not be entirely certain that the nothing was nothing. Some absences, William had written in Notebook 4, in handwriting that Blake had only learned to decipher last year, are not absences. They are presences that have learned not to disturb.

The wind moved against the cottage walls.

Blake held the pen above the page the way Aldric had held the quill above the parchment in the scriptorium in 1267. The same gesture. Across seven hundred and fifty-eight years, the same gesture. Two men with two different inks and two different alphabets and the same essential posture: the human animal at a desk, deciding what to leave.

[pause]

This is the report.

The Word came first. The Body came second. The Listening came after. I have written what I can write of all three.

I have done my best, where I can, to write what happened. Where memory ran out I followed the frequency. Where the frequency ran out I followed the patterns my grandfather circled in red ink and never explained, the figures Brother Aldric copied from a manuscript he saw burned, the cymatic geometries an Ashanti grandmother saw forming in a goatskin under her hand. They have never lied to me yet.

Some things I have not written. The seven satchels Aldric scattered across continents in 1267 — Prague, Paris, Rome, Constantinople, Uppsala, Toledo, and the one location he refused to name even to the figure who took it. I have written of the first and last only. Of the others I will say that the tradition that produced them is not finished, and that the satchels surface, when they surface, in the bodies that need them. Aldric was wiser than the Watcher who came to his scriptorium. Delay, he said, is all we have ever had. He gave us time. We have used some of it. We have, in the matter of grandmothers in folding chairs in nine hundred living rooms in forty-seven countries, used it well.

Some things I cannot write. There is a chamber at the bottom of an Ozark lake; I will not return to it. There is a sphere in that chamber whose carvings no living person has read; that is not my work to read. There is a silver convent ring in a kitchen in Kansas City that may or may not be on my wife's finger when she wakes tomorrow morning. The sphere will keep without me. The lake will keep without me. The ring is hers, and the choice is hers, and the love between us is hers.

Of the figure that came to Aldric in the scriptorium, the one whose words bypassed his ears, the one whose fingers were not quite fingers — I have nothing to add. He took the seventh satchel to the place humans cannot go. Whether the place is underwater, or in a fold of geometry that human language has no name for, or simply behind us, in the absence we mistake for our own thinking, I do not know. I do not need to know. The frequency continues. The frequency was never the satchel.

The Word is still speaking. You have always been listening. You always were.

— Blake Masters, Mýrdalsjökull, Iceland He drew a line under the byline.

He turned the page over. Smoothed it under his palm. The paper accepted the pressure the way good paper does: by giving back nothing, by simply being the surface the writer had decided it would be.

The cottage was warm. The wind was high. The Breitling on the desk ticked at eight hertz and would tick for another twenty-seven hours and then would not.

Blake wrote the first line of the manuscript.

Restless candlelight guttered.
