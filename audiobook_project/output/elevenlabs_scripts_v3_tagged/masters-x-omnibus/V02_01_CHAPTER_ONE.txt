Volume Two: The Grimoire.

Chapter One. The Stone Cottage.

Three point nine one five hertz. Iceland.

[short pause]

Thirteen months. Four hundred and two days since the demonstration ended and the tremor began and Andrew walked him out of the eighth chamber with a blanket around his shoulders and a look on his face that said he'd been ready to walk in after him for the last six hours.

The stone cottage. One window facing south. The desk Blake had used for eleven months to write the account that would become the first volume, every word of it set down in the seventh Moleskine with a pen that shook in his hand at a frequency he no longer tried to control. one eleven point two hertz. Bilateral. Permanent, according to Sabrina Volkov, whose clinical assessments had the compassion of a woman and the precision of an instrument.

He finished the manuscript that day.

He set down the pen and watched the tremor put a wobble into the final period. A dot. The smallest possible mark. The entire account, Prague and Venice and the crypt and the mirrors and the Completion Sect and Marcus's synesthesia and Yuki's dissolution and Andrew's seven years and William's watch and all of it, compressed into a single point of vibrating ink.

"Done," he said aloud. His voice sounded unused, the word coming out rusty and uncertain, the way a door sounds when you open it for the first time in months. The echo came back from the basalt walls at 0.026 seconds, the cottage was nine meters long, and the reflection had a warmth to it that the Iceland chambers didn't, something in the volcanic stone that softened the return instead of sharpening it. He had been hearing this echo for eleven months. He would miss it when he left.

He would not miss the wind. The gale in Mýrdalsjökull had opinions.

[pause]

He called Nadia at 9 PM Kansas City time, 1 AM in Iceland, which was a kindness she would never name and he could never repay. She picked up on the first ring. She had always picked up on the first ring.

"Chapter?"

That was Nadia. Not hello. Not how are you. Chapter. She had been tracking his word count the way she tracked everything, with the institutional precision of a woman who believed that if you could not measure a thing, you could not protect it. She knew he was on Chapter 67. She knew because he had told her three days ago, and Nadia filed information the way archivists filed parchment: permanently, in order, with cross-references.

"Done," he said. "The whole thing. It's done."

Four seconds of silence. He counted. Counting silence was a habit he'd developed since the demonstration, the way other people counted beats in music, Blake counted the spaces between speech. Four seconds was long for Nadia. Four seconds meant she was feeling something she had decided to manage before it reached her voice.

"Read me the last paragraph."

He read it. The paragraph about the Moleskine, the Breitling, the stars over the lava field. She listened without interrupting. Nadia's listening was a physical force, a quality of attention that made you aware of every word you were choosing and why.

When he finished:

"It's good. The watch is good. The stars are too many."

"Too many stars?"

"Three sentences about stars. You only need one. The watch carries the weight. Let the watch do its job. The stars dilute it."

He re-read the paragraph. She was right. She was always right about this, about structure, about proportion, about which details served the architecture and which were ornament pretending to be load-bearing. He had spent six years learning to hear the frequency of caves and manuscripts and medieval machinery. Nadia heard the frequency of sentences. She always had.

"I'll cut two."

"Cut all three. End on the watch."

[pause]

The package arrived the next morning.

FedEx, from Prague. Return address: Eva Černá, Charles University Archives. Blake hadn't heard from Eva in four months, since her Strahov paper was published in the Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory and immediately attacked by three Premonstratensian scholars who took personal offense at the suggestion that their monastic order had been guarding acoustic secrets for seven centuries.

Eva's response to the attacks had been a single sentence in the journal's letters section: "The lead box exists. The manuscripts exist. The frequencies are measurable. I await their counter-measurements." Nobody had responded.

He opened the package at the kitchen table. Inside: a photocopy of a manuscript page, 15th-century hand, the ink faded to the particular brown that iron gall produces after five hundred years of oxidation. It was not from the Strahov collection itself. Eva's cover letter explained that she'd found it in a secondary archive at Charles University, a Premonstratensian loan record from 1491. The page had been catalogued as "administrative correspondence" and filed in a box that hadn't been opened since 1968.

It was not administrative correspondence.

It was a reading list.

Twenty-three titles, written in the monastic Latin that Blake had learned to recognize though not fluently translate. The page header: Lectiones ante introitum. Readings before entry. Some titles he recognized immediately. Ars Notoria, which he'd encountered in fragments during the Prague research. De Occulta Philosophia, Agrippa's three books, which Andrew had flagged as a potential source text for the mirror specifications. Liber Juratus, the Sworn Book of Honorius, which had appeared in the Keeper's own annotations on the fourth Strahov page.

Most of the titles he didn't know. Liber Sapientiae, the Wisdom of Solomon. Liber Esdrae Quartus, the Fourth Book of Esdras. And one that made him set the photocopy down and stare at the cottage wall for a long time: Sefer Yetzirah. The Book of Formation. A Jewish Kabbalistic text, on a Christian monastic reading list, in a Premonstratensian archive in Prague.

The Strahov monks had been reading across traditions. The preparation wasn't just acoustic. It was textual. They weren't just tuning their bodies to the frequency, they were tuning their minds to the knowledge the frequency carried. And the knowledge came from everywhere: Christian, Jewish, Islamic, Hermetic. A 15th-century curriculum that would have gotten them burned if the wrong bishop had seen it.

He photographed the list and sent it to Eva with one question: Can you source all twenty-three?

Then he sent it to Andrew.

[pause]

Andrew's response arrived in eleven minutes. Blake knew it was eleven minutes because he was holding the Breitling and watching the second hand make its circuit, a habit he'd inherited so thoroughly from William that he sometimes forgot it wasn't his own.

His message was characteristically Andrew: precise, understated, and containing an observation that rearranged everything.

"Twenty-three texts. Twenty-three cohort candidates. You see that, right?"

Blake sat with this. The morning light was working its way across the desk, the rectangle of sun moving from the edge of Eva's photocopy to the spine of the Moleskine. Iceland in September, the light was amber and temporary, the sun low enough that every surface got a shadow, and the shadows were long and dramatic, like lighting in a cathedral where the architect understood that what you don't illuminate is as important as what you do.

Twenty-three texts. Twenty-three candidates. Each candidate assigned one book from the preparation reading list, the way a graduate seminar assigns one monograph per student.

He typed: "That can't be right."

Andrew: "Can't it? The Strahov monks had twenty-three reading stations in the theological hall. I found the inventory six months ago. Twenty-three desks. Twenty-three chains. One book per chain."

"You're saying the reading list was physically encoded in the library architecture."

"I'm saying the library was a preparation chamber. Not for sound. For knowledge. The monks entered the acoustic chamber with their ears. They entered the library with their eyes. Same preparation. Different sense organ."

Blake closed the laptop and looked at the photocopy for a long time. Twenty-three titles. Some of them he'd never heard of. Most of them he'd never read. And according to this 15th-century document, the Strahov monks considered every one of them essential reading before entering the crypt.

The manuscripts he'd spent six years studying were the chamber's instruction manual. But the books on this list were something else, the preparation for the preparation. The prerequisite reading before you could understand what the frequency was actually doing.

He needed to read them all.

[pause]

He wrote Nadia a long email that night. Three paragraphs about Eva's discovery, the reading list, the twenty-three desks in the theological hall. He re-read it. Deleted it. Wrote two paragraphs. Deleted those. Wrote one sentence:

"The preparation protocol is at least 2,200 years old."

Her response arrived twelve minutes later. The speed told him she was at her desk, 10 PM Kansas City, the hour when Nadia Volkov did her best thinking because the Foundation was empty and the silence let her work without the institutional friction of other people's needs.

"I know. Sirach 51. I read it last month."

Blake stared at the screen. She had read the Book of Sirach. One of the apocryphal texts on Eva's list, a book removed from the Protestant Bible four centuries ago. She had found it, read it, and identified its structure before he had even opened the package from Prague.

She hadn't told him because she was waiting for him to arrive at it himself.

The Moleskine. Seventh volume. Final entry:

This is what it is to be married to Nadia Volkov. You are never ahead. You are never lagging. You are walking the same road at different speeds, and the road is patient enough to wait for both of you.

[pause]

The next morning Blake sat at the desk with the finished manuscript pushed to one side and Eva's photocopy weighted with a stone from the lava field. He opened his laptop to sacred-texts.com and began ordering books.

The old work was finished. The new labor was beginning. And between them, on the desk, the Breitling ticked. William's time, his father's time, his time, all of it the same vibration, counting the intervals between what he'd found and what he hadn't yet understood.

The pen trembled on the desk where he'd left it, a faint vibration from the wind outside transmitting through the stone walls, through the desk legs, through the wood, to the pen, which rolled a quarter-inch and stopped.

Or maybe it was his hands, resting on the desk surface, conducting one eleven point two hertz into the furniture.

Blake no longer knew the difference. He was no longer sure there was one.
