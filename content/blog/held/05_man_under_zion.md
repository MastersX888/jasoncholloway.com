# The Man Who Built a City Under Zion

*The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 5 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press*

Every novelist who works from research eventually meets the fact that embarrasses the fiction — the documented thing so improbable that putting it in a novel unaltered would read as laziness. Mine lives twenty minutes from my desk, in Independence, Missouri, a hundred and eighty acres down.

A sourcing note before anything else, because this essay concerns a living person: everything factual below is drawn from public records, published reporting, and his son's public statements. Where the record is silent, this essay is silent. The parallels to my fiction are conceded openly at the end, and the divergences matter more than the resemblances.

## The public record

Roger Billings entered the public record as a prodigy. As a teenager — seventeen, in the standard telling — he converted a car to run on hydrogen, decades before hydrogen energy had a lobby or a market. The work was real engineering, and it made him, in the small world of alternative energy, a genuine pioneer. He went on to found research ventures and an educational institution, the Academy, and to accumulate the kind of résumé — inventor, entrepreneur, institution-builder — that profiles get written about.

In 1980, he relocated to Independence, Missouri. And here the story acquires its second axis, because by his own account the relocation was not about markets or family or weather. It was prophetic. Independence is the ground that Joseph Smith designated, in 1831, as the center place of Zion — the fact pattern the previous essay in this series laid out. Billings came to Independence, founded his own church within the broader Restoration tradition — the Church of Jesus Christ in Zion — and holds within it the office his tradition describes as patriarch and prophet.

## The record beneath the surface

Then there is the land. Billings acquired approximately one hundred eighty acres of underground space — worked limestone, the Bethany Falls formation, the same Pennsylvanian-age stone that hosts [SubTropolis](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/subtropolis/) on the other side of the metro. And he developed it: laboratories, work space, living quarters, an operating campus inside the rock. An engineer-prophet, running research facilities in the ground his own tradition identifies as the New Jerusalem.

Read that sentence again as a fiction editor would. A hydrogen pioneer. Who founded his own church. Who calls himself its patriarch and prophet. Who bought a hundred eighty acres of underground limestone inside the prophesied Zion. And built laboratories in it. If I had submitted that character in a first draft, any competent editor would have flagged him as overwritten — too many axes of strangeness in one man. He is not a draft. He is a matter of county land records and published interviews, and my research file on him (`ROGER_BILLINGS_MASTERS_X_ANALYSIS.md`, for those keeping score against the encyclopedia's bibliography) is one of the thickest in the archive.

## The parallel, conceded

Readers of *The Inheritance of Frequency* will already have made the mapping, so let me concede it rather than be caught at it. William Masters, the trilogy's founding figure, is an engineer of unusual gifts who becomes convinced that something immense sleeps under the Kansas City corridor; who acquires underground limestone; who builds a private research campus in it; who documents everything in notebooks; and who chooses silence over publication for most of his life. The resemblance to the documented figure is not accidental, and the encyclopedia's entry on William Masters says so with a straight face: the strangest thing in the Masters X universe is that its most implausible character has a documented counterpart.

But the divergences are the actual content of the fiction, and they are deliberate. Billings' compound is, on the record, private — fenced, surveilled, access-controlled, as any private research facility would be. His tradition's claims run through his church. William Masters' arc bends the opposite way at every joint the story controls. The Masters Foundation for Acoustic Research is written consent-first: nobody in the trilogy is brought into the chambers unknowing. Its findings are hoarded for two books and then, in the trilogy's ending, given away entirely — the Distribution File, released to the public domain at midnight, 247 pages that cannot be un-released. Where the real pattern is a prophet holding ground privately against a foretold future, the fictional pattern is an engineer's estate finally refusing to hold anything at all.

That inversion is the trilogy's ethical argument, and it only lands because the documented case exists. Fiction can't meaningfully disagree with a straw man. It can disagree with a land record.

## The editorial care note, expanded

A word on method, because writing about a living private-ish figure alongside one's own fiction imposes obligations that writing about Neolithic builders does not.

The rule this essay follows — and the encyclopedia entry follows, and the video episode follows — is attribution over atmosphere. Every factual clause traces to a category of source that can be named in the sentence: land records, published reporting, the subject's own public statements, his son's public statements. Nothing is sourced to rumor, to "locals say," or to the genre's favorite instrument, the unattributed ominous implication. Where the record describes laboratories, this essay says laboratories; where the record is silent — about interiors, about intentions beyond the stated ones — this essay is silent too, and says that it is being silent, which is different from hinting.

The reason is not only fairness, though fairness would suffice. It is that atmosphere is precisely the corruption the trilogy is about. A book built on Eco's warning — that pattern-hunters manufacture the pattern — cannot then manufacture a sinister pattern around a real man because sinister sells. The documented facts are strange enough to carry the essay without a single raised eyebrow, and if they weren't, the essay shouldn't exist.

## Why this is the fifth tradition and not a curiosity

The previous essay counted four traditions that identified the Kansas City corridor as significant ground: the Hopewell builders with their stone-vault chambers, the Osage naming of *Wah-kon-tah*, the Latter Day Saint survey of Zion, and the limestone itself. It would be easy to file Billings as a colorful footnote to the third. I think that undersells what his case demonstrates.

The first four traditions are historical: you can visit their traces, but their choosing happened long ago. Billings is the demonstration that the choosing *continues* — that in 1980, a modern engineer with modern resources, acting on the old designation, looked at the same ground and committed his life and his acreage to it. The corridor's pull, whatever one believes about its source, is still operating on living people with the means to act on it. For a trilogy whose thesis is that this ground keeps recruiting its seekers across millennia, the fifth seeker is not a footnote. He is the proof of continuity — the reason the novel's invented sixth seeker, arriving a generation later, asks no more suspension of disbelief than the newspaper does.

The grade card for this essay, then, is short. The biography, the church, the offices, the acreage, the laboratories: documented — public records, reporting, and family statements, plainly attributed. The interior life, the motives beyond the stated ones, anything underground that the record does not describe: unknown, and this essay does not speculate. William Masters, the Foundation, the consent protocols, the midnight release: invented, and built as a deliberate inversion.
