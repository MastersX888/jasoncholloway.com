Chapter Five. The Revelation.

One hundred nine hertz. Kansas City.


Nadia knocked at 7:15 PM.

Blake had spent the hour trying to clean up, not the apartment, which was beyond help, but himself. Washed the blood from his hair. Changed his shirt. Pressed a cold washcloth against the cut until the swelling went down enough that he didn't look like he'd been in a bar fight.

He still looked like he'd been in a bar fight.

When he opened the door, Nadia stood in the hallway holding two paper bags. One from a Thai restaurant Blake recognized. The other from a pharmacy.

"You haven't eaten," she said. Not a question.

"How did you -"

"Because you never eat when you're processing something." She walked past him, set the bags on the kitchen table. "Pad Thai. Tom Kha. And bandages, because whatever you did to your forehead needs more than a washcloth."

She turned and looked at him properly. In the apartment light, without the coffee shop's theatrical shadows, she was. Blake searched for the word. present. More present than anyone had been in his space since Sarah had packed her things and left a silence behind that Blake had learned to treat as furniture.

"Sit down," she said. "I need to look at that cut."

"You're a nun, not a nurse."

"I'm a woman who grew up in a Ukrainian orphanage." She pulled antiseptic and butterfly bandages from the pharmacy bag. "Sit."

"It's not deep," she said. "But you hit hard. How's your vision?"

"Different."

Her hands paused. "Different how?"

"Everything has structure. Geometry. Relationships I couldn't see before. The patterns from the notebooks, from SubTropolis, they're everywhere now."

Nadia didn't look skeptical. She looked relieved.

"Good," she said quietly, and pressed a butterfly bandage across the cut. "That means it's working."

"What's working?"

She finished the bandage. Stepped back. And for a moment, standing in his kitchen with antiseptic on her fingers and Thai food getting cold on the table, she looked less like an operative and more like a person who'd been carrying something heavy for a long time and had just set part of it down.

"We need to eat first," she said. "Then I need to show you something."



They ate cross-legged on the kitchen floor because the table was too small and the couch was covered in manuscripts. Nadia used chopsticks with unselfconscious ease. Blake used a fork and tried not to stare.

A bottle of wine stood on the counter. Sarah's Grüner Veltliner, Austrian, unopened for six months. It caught the kitchen light and threw a pale green shadow across the wall. Blake noticed himself noticing and tried to stop. Failed. This was his life now. Every surface a text. Every shadow a calculation.

Nadia's presence changed the apartment's resonance. She occupied space the way music occupied a concert hall, altering everything simply by being there.

"So," Blake said. "You've been watching me since I was fifteen."

"Not me personally. The organization." Nadia picked up a piece of tofu. "I've been watching you for about two years. Since your SubTropolis discovery."

"Two years. And you waited until now?"

"You weren't ready until now." She met his eyes. "When the bank called you, when you started moving, that's when we knew the timeline had accelerated."

"We."

"The Keepers." She said it simply, the way you'd say the department. "We've been operating in Kansas City since 1924. Protecting this knowledge. Guiding those who are ready to receive it."

"Since Father Moreau."

Her composure cracked. "Since Father Moreau was murdered. Yes."

Blake thought about his father. About James Masters, dead on a straight road in perfect weather. Nadia saw the thought cross his face.

"The Keepers don't kill," she said. "There are others who do. The Custodians believe humanity isn't ready. The Completion Sect believes the knowledge should be weaponized. And then there's us. We believe people should be allowed to choose."

"Choose what?"

"Whether to see. Whether to pursue the transformation or stay as they are." She set down her chopsticks. "Free will, Blake. Informed consent. That's why your grandfather documented everything."

"Show me what you need to show me," he said.

Nadia pulled the untitled book from Blake's bag. She handled it the way a priest handles a relic. Opened it on the kitchen floor between them, their knees almost touching.

"Do you know what the Ars Notoria is?" she asked.

"A medieval grimoire. Remembrance, enhancement, eloquence. Most scholars consider it superstitious nonsense."

"Most scholars are wrong." Nadia traced her finger along one of the geometric figures. "The Ars Notoria isn't magic. It's technology. Cognitive technology. These figures. The notae. Are interfaces. Tools for accessing parts of presence that are normally dormant."

She looked up. Their faces were close.

"Your brain is capable of far more than you normally access," she said. "The notae are keys that unlock those capabilities. When your brain processes these specific mathematical relationships, it fires in sequences it doesn't normally use. Those sequences activate dormant neural pathways."

"Enhanced perception. Pattern recognition."

"Yes. But here's what matters." Nadia turned pages carefully, stopping at a figure more complex than the others. "The conventional history says this technology was developed by medieval monks building on Greek and Arabic sources. Your grandfather found evidence that contradicts that."

She showed him photographs on her phone. Cave art. Circles within circles. Nested triangles. The same proportions as the figures in the book.

"Picture Cave," she said. "Warren County, Missouri. Osage ancestral ground, sacred to them still. Dated to at least a thousand years ago. Pre-Columbian. Pre-European. Pre-Christian."

"The patterns were here first," Blake said slowly. "Everything in medieval Europe was copied from North American sources."

"That's what your grandfather believed. What thirty years of documentation supports." Nadia's eyes were bright. This wasn't the operative or the nun. This was the scholar. "If we can prove it, we could rewrite the history of human awareness."

"And the Custodians want to stop that."

"The Custodians killed your father to stop that."

The words hung between them. Nadia held his gaze. Her hand on the book had gone still, her ring finger pressing against the silver band.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I know that's -"

"Don't. Don't apologize for telling me what I already know."

Silence. Two people sitting on a kitchen floor with a medieval manuscript between them and a dead man's questions filling the space.

Then Nadia laughed.

Not at him. At the situation. At the two of them cross-legged with pad Thai containers and antiseptic wipes and a book that shouldn't exist.

It was the first time Blake had heard her laugh. It changed her entire face, broke the composure, revealed something younger underneath.

"What?" Blake said.

"Nothing. It's just. I've been preparing for this conversation for two years. I had it planned. Professional. Controlled." She shook her head. "Not on a kitchen floor."

"You wanted a PowerPoint presentation."

"I wanted to seem competent."

"You showed up with Thai food and bandages. That's competent enough."

She smiled. Not the sad, knowing smile from the coffee shop. Something warmer. Something that reached her eyes.

"There's more to show you," she said. "A place. Our operational base. But it can wait until tomorrow."

"Why tomorrow?"

"Because you have a concussion. Because you haven't slept. Because -" She hesitated. "Because I'd rather stay here tonight. If that's. I can sleep on the couch. I just don't think you should be alone right now."

It was a reasonable thing to say. Clinical, even. But Nadia's hand was still on the book between them, and her fingers were two inches from Blake's knee, and the way she'd said I'd rather stay carried a weight apart from concussion protocol.

"The couch is covered in manuscripts," Blake said.

"I'll move them."

"Blake." She looked at him steadily. "I'm offering to stay because you shouldn't be alone. That's all."

"I know."

"Good." She stood. Began clearing the Thai containers. "Show me which manuscripts I'm allowed to move."



Blake didn't sleep. Not really. He lay in the dark, the ceiling patterns moving above him, and listened to Nadia breathing in the next room.

She'd cleared a space on the couch with the efficiency of someone used to making order from chaos. Had arranged manuscripts into careful stacks, handling them with the same reverence she'd given the untitled book.

Now she was asleep, or pretending to be, and Blake lay in the dark and thought about what she'd told him. Keepers. Custodians. Completion Sect. Three organizations fighting over ancient knowledge. His grandfather had found the evidence. His father had died pursuing it.

But that wasn't what kept him awake.

What kept him awake was the laugh. The startled sound of Nadia laughing on his kitchen floor. The way she'd said I wanted to seem competent with a self-awareness that cut through every layer of craft and left something honest standing in the kitchen light.

At 3:47, Blake's eyes opened. He lay still, listening. Nadia's breathing from the couch. The refrigerator hum. The motifs on the ceiling, visible even without light.

He got up. Went to the kitchen.

She was already awake.

"Can't sleep either?" Soft. Not startled. She'd heard him get up, both of them tuned to frequencies most people couldn't hear.

"3:47," Blake said. "Every morning."

"William mentioned that in Notebook 3. A consistent wake time associated with the integration process." A pause. "Two cups?"

Blake made two cups. Brought one to the couch. Nadia sat up, the blanket around her shoulders, her hair loose for the first time. She'd worn it pulled back every time he'd seen her. Loose, it fell past her shoulders, dark in the pre-dawn kitchen.

She took the cup. Their fingers brushed on the ceramic.

"Tomorrow," Nadia said. "I'll take you to Genessee Street. The operational base. There's more to show you. Maps, connections, your grandfather's full research. And we need to talk about what comes next."

"What comes next?"

She turned the cup in her hands. The silver ring caught the early light.

"SubTropolis," she said. "Eventually. When you're ready. But first, you need to learn to control what you're seeing. Your grandfather documented his entire process, exercises, practices. It took him months."

"Months."

"Months." She looked at him. In the gray dawn, her green eyes were dark, almost black. "You're not alone in this, Blake. I know the sequences make everything seem distant. Like you're seeing the world from behind glass. But you're not alone."

She said it the way she'd said it's going to be all right on the phone. As if it were fact and not comfort.

"Thank you," he said.

"For what?"

"For the Thai food."

She laughed again. Quiet this time, almost private.

"You're welcome," she said.

And freshly, in months, maybe years. The 3:47 AM silence in Blake's apartment felt like something other than loneliness.
