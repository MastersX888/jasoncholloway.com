The Frequency That Was Already There

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 1 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

Every page of the Distribution File carries the same footer: f = 111.2 Hz. Two hundred forty-seven pages, one stamp. Readers ask about it more than anything else in the trilogy. Where did the number come from? Is it real?

The number is fiction, but only by one decimal place. The stone underneath it was measured before I started writing.

What was actually measured

Archaeoacoustics is a modest field. Researchers record how sound behaves in ancient built spaces, tracking resonant frequencies, reverberation, and standing waves, then ask whether those properties were designed or incidental. The careful practitioners make careful claims. That is why the field is worth taking seriously.

The site everyone cites is the Ħal-Saflieni Hypogeum in Malta: an underground temple complex carved from limestone around 3600–2500 BCE, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site limited to eighty visitors per day. In the Oracle Chamber, researchers have documented a pronounced resonance around 110–111 Hz. A male voice chanting in that range produces an intense reverberant effect. Cook, Pajot, and Leuchter (2008) reported a shift in prefrontal activity in subjects exposed to that resonance. One study. Small sample. That caveat belongs in every sentence that cites it, and it belongs in mine.

Malta is not alone. Paul Devereux and colleagues measured resonances at Neolithic chambers across Britain and Ireland and found peak frequencies consistently in the 95–120 Hz band. Aaron Watson and David Keating did the same at Camster Round in Scotland. Iegor Reznikoff and Michel Dauvois observed that Palaeolithic paintings at Lascaux and other French caves cluster disproportionately in zones of highest acoustic resonance. That work was published in 1988 and received by mainstream archaeology with interest and caution in roughly equal measure.

I assembled the full record, including sites, studies, citations, and an FAQ, in the 111 Hz Field Note. The short version: a recurring resonance near 110 Hz in ancient stone enclosures is documented. Whether anyone intended it is not.

Why the fiction adds a decimal

Why does the trilogy say 111.2 instead of 110?

If I had written 110 Hz, I would have been borrowing the authority of the measured literature for claims the literature does not make. The archaeoacousticians measured a resonance. They did not measure Blake Masters' bilateral tremor, locked in CANON at exactly 111.2 Hz. They did not measure a preparation protocol or a perception chamber. One decimal past the real number is the fiction signing its own work. Close enough to honor the source. Distinct enough that nobody mistakes my invention for their measurement.

Precision is also a character trait. William Masters is an engineer. A man who leaves seven leather notebooks for his grandson does not write "about 110." He writes 111.2, with the instrumentation to defend the second decimal.

The frequency ladder

The trilogy's frequency system is a ladder. Every rung has a real referent, which is why each one needs a label.

7.83 Hz. The Schumann resonance, the fundamental of the cavity between Earth's surface and the ionosphere. Textbook geophysics. In the world.

110 Hz. The archaeoacoustic band above. In the world, with the caveat that the literature is thinner than a thriller needs it to be.

111.2 Hz. The trilogy's carrier frequency, Blake's tremor, the Distribution File footer. In the novels.

136.6 Hz. Connected in the books to transcendence states, mapped to the Monroe Institute's focus-level framework. The U.S. Army assessed that framework in the Gateway Process document, a real declassified file that gets an essay of its own. The traditions are documented. The claims inside them are not scientific findings. Contested at best.

Chapter headers map this ladder onto real places: the Kansas City limestone under SubTropolis, the Strahov crypt in Prague, Icelandic basalt. Every referent is documented. The claim that they form one coherent system is the novel. The rungs are real. The ladder is fiction.

Sound has shapes

One more piece of real science holds the frequency system up. In 1787, Ernst Chladni drew a violin bow along a sand-covered metal plate and showed that sand migrates to the nodal lines, the places where the plate is still, forming geometric figures that change with frequency. Hans Jenny extended the work in the 1960s: shift from 800 to 865 cycles per second and the pattern does not adjust. It transforms into a categorically different figure.

In a universe where sound produces form, 111.2 is not interchangeable with 111.3. The Cymatics Field Note covers Chladni and Jenny in full, including where popular cymatics claims outrun the research.

Why label any of this

The trilogy is about the danger of unlabeled patterns. Umberto Eco's warning, that pattern-hunters manufacture the pattern they seek, runs through everything Blake Masters does. A book built on that warning cannot hand its readers an unmarked mixture of measurement and invention.

So: the Schumann resonance is in the world. The 110 Hz chamber literature is in the world, thin but real. The 111.2 Hz carrier, the tremor, the protocol, and the chamber are in the novels.

The Grimoire That Was Actually a Study Aid

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 2 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

In The Inheritance of Frequency, Nadia sits on a kitchen floor with a facsimile page between her hands and says the line the trilogy pivots on: "The Ars Notoria isn't magic. It's technology. Cognitive technology."

Readers assume I invented the manuscript. I did not. The Ars Notoria is real, catalogued, requestable at the British Library. What I invented is narrower, and stranger for its narrowness.

The manuscript on the record

The Ars Notoria, or the Notory Art, belongs to the Solomonic tradition: medieval texts attributed, nominally, to King Solomon. The earliest surviving manuscripts date to the mid-thirteenth century. Institutional copies survive across Europe, including British Library MS Sloane 1712 and holdings at the Bibliothèque nationale de France. Robert Turner translated it into English in 1657. Modern scholarly editions exist, and Claire Fanger and Julien Véronèse have spent careers on it.

What the text promises is the rapid acquisition of the seven liberal arts, meaning grammar, rhetoric, logic, arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy, through structured contemplation of geometric figures called notae combined with specific prayers on a prescribed schedule. Not wealth, not love, not power over demons. Knowledge, memory, and eloquence on an accelerated timetable. A medieval manual for learning faster.

The notae are dense, elaborate diagrams, geometric structures carrying prayers in Latin, Hebrew, Greek, and occasionally scripts nobody has identified. They are meant to be gazed at in sequence while the orations are recited. As objects they are unlike anything else in medieval book culture: not illustrations of a text but apparently instruments of it. The full account is in the Ars Notoria Field Note.

Condemned as cheating

The medieval world took this book seriously enough to condemn it, by the Inquisition and by Albertus Magnus. The grounds were not devil-worship. The Ars Notoria promised an illicit shortcut: knowledge acquired without the sanctioned labor of study. Condemned as cheating.

Institutions rarely mobilize against texts they consider trivial. The vehemence suggests the manuscript was circulating widely among exactly the educated class the universities were built to produce.

How a banned book survives

Condemned books in the manuscript era did not disappear. They went expensive. Copies continued to be produced for centuries on good vellum, with the notae executed carefully, patrons paying scribes serious money for a text the authorities had ruled against. By 1657 it had crossed into print in English through Turner's translation. A text survives seven hundred years of official disapproval only if a chain of readers, generation after generation, privately concluded it was worth the risk and the price. The trilogy's Keepers are fiction. The behavior they are modeled on, quiet and well-funded and multi-generational custodianship of a condemned text, is the Ars Notoria's actual transmission history.

What a modern reader notices

Strip away the angelic language and describe the method operationally: extended visual contemplation of complex structured figures, recitation on a fixed schedule, prescribed postures, repetition across weeks and months, a curriculum sequenced from foundational to advanced. A cognitive scientist recognizes elaborative encoding, spaced repetition, and structured retrieval practice, seven centuries before anyone had the vocabulary.

Does that mean medieval practitioners empirically discovered spaced repetition and encoded it as prayer? Maybe. Maybe it is a coincidence of forms. The resemblance is real and striking, and it is the documented foundation the fiction stands on.

Where the fiction begins

In the world: the manuscript, the notae, the promise of accelerated learning, the condemnation, the surviving copies, the scholarly reappraisal.

In the novels: that it works, and how. Nadia's argument, developed across two volumes, is that the notae are interfaces, calibrated visual objects designed to produce specific neurological states in a prepared observer, with fasting rules, postures, and prayer sequences functioning as a protocol for reaching cortical coherence before engaging the figures. No historian makes that claim. No cognitive scientist makes it. It is the trilogy's one experiment the lab has never run.

One further invention points forward: the trilogy's claim that the notae are frozen sound, cymatics patterns copied from standing waves by monks who watched geometry form in water and sand. The figures are real. The mechanism is mine. See Sound Into Form and the Cymatics Field Note.

Why this manuscript

The occult corpus is enormous. The trilogy could have run on the Lesser Key, on Agrippa, on Dee's Enochian tables, all of which sit in my research library, none of which carries the story. The Ars Notoria does, for one reason: it is the only major text in that tradition whose stated purpose is cognition. It doesn't summon anything. It claims to change what a mind can hold and how fast. A trilogy about perception needed an operational manual, and the thirteenth century had already written one and been condemned for it.

Sound Into Form: What Hans Jenny Actually Proved

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 3 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

The oldest special effect in my trilogy requires no computer. A metal plate, a violin bow, a spoonful of fine sand. Draw the bow along the plate's edge and the sand jumps into a symmetrical geometric figure, as if the tone had drawn it. The demonstration is from 1787. You can reproduce it on your kitchen table tonight: level the plate, sift the sand, keep the bow pressure light.

Everything strange in the Masters X universe stands on that plate. What the plate proves is one thing. What the novels build on top of it is another, and the two are worth keeping apart.

Chladni: frequency becomes figure

Ernst Chladni (1756–1827) documented the phenomenon systematically in his 1787 Entdeckungen über die Theorie des Klanges. A bowed plate vibrates in modes. At a resonant frequency, some regions move violently while the nodal lines stay still. Sand bounces off the moving regions and collects on the still ones. The figure you see is a map of the silence, the geometry of where the plate is not moving. Change the frequency and the figure changes.

Measured. Reproducible by anyone. Disputed by no one.

Jenny: one phenomenon, three registers

Hans Jenny (1904–1972), a Swiss physician, took Chladni's parlor demonstration and turned it into a research program. Crystal oscillators replaced violin bows. Liquids, pastes, and powders replaced sand. He published the results in the two-volume Kymatik (1967, 1972), translated as Cymatics, the term he coined.

Two findings matter for the fiction. First, sensitivity. A small frequency change can produce a categorically different pattern, and Jenny documented a shift from 800 to 865 cycles per second that transforms the figure rather than adjusting it. In a world where sound makes form, the decimal places matter. That is why 111.2 Hz cannot be treated as interchangeable with 111 or 112.

Second, conditionality. Turn the tone off and the pattern collapses. Turn it back on and the same pattern returns. Order is not built and then left standing. It is maintained by the frequency. Jenny's summary, "one can hear what one sees," names his triad: the wave you hear, the pattern you see, the periodicity you feel, one phenomenon apprehended three ways.

What Jenny did not prove, and the Cymatics Field Note draws this boundary explicitly, is anything about healing frequencies, water memory, or consciousness. The physics is real. A good deal of what circulates under the word "cymatics" outruns it.

Where the record ends

There is no evidence that viewing a cymatics pattern alters consciousness. The documented phenomenon is sound organizing matter. The trilogy's central conceit is that the resulting geometry, contemplated by a prepared observer, does something to the observer. That claim is entirely in the novels.

The trilogy proposes that medieval practitioners, chanting daily for decades in resonant stone over water basins, saw standing-wave patterns form and copied them. It proposes that the Ars Notoria's notae are cymatics figures, frozen sound, and that a scribe in Bohemia in 1267 fixed seven hundred twelve of them onto vellum without knowing what he was drawing. Every clause of that is mine. No historian makes the claim.

Sound makes form is measured. Form remakes the observer is invented.

Water

Jenny filmed liquids as well as powders, and liquids are where his footage is most arresting, with standing patterns holding on a vibrating water surface. The research note in my files that became a structural principle is one sentence long: water amplifies cymatics effects.

The trilogy keeps returning to water: Brother Aldric's basin in the 1267 prologue, Father Moreau's river, the Ozark lake with its ninety documented feet of dark. In the novels, water is the medium in which the frequency becomes visible enough to copy.

In The Grimoire, Nadia maps the Strahov chamber's resonance into a stable hexagonal figure, then watches footage of Kofi Asante's drumming in Ghana producing a five-sided standing wave in red laterite clay. Both specific patterns, and Nadia's inference that they share a structural origin, are invented. The dependence of pattern on material, geometry, and frequency, the thing that makes her inference feel like science, is real. The Ghana cave acoustics are labeled fiction in the Field Notes.

Yuki Tanaka

The trilogy's researchers do not merely cite cymatics. They practice it. Yuki Tanaka's disc experiments, with controlled frequency, documented media, repeated trials, and one variable at a time, are fictional experiments conducted correctly. The characters' method is real method. Only their results are invented.

Hans Jenny gives the Masters X universe not proof of anything mystical but a demonstration, bench-top and repeatable and gorgeous, that frequency and form are already married in the physical world. The novels propose one further wedding, and they say so out loud.

Why Kansas City? The Ground Itself Is Significant

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 4 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

Somewhere around the second century, people on the Missouri River bluffs built rooms inside their burial mounds. Stone-vault tombs, constructed chambers with doorways, sealed inside the earthworks. Roughly thirty Hopewell-tradition sites are documented in the Kansas City area, the westernmost reach of a mound-building culture that spread along the eastern river systems between about 100 BCE and 700 CE.

I read that in a site report years into the research and set the page down. The impulse the whole trilogy runs on, cutting significant chambers into this specific ground, was not something I invented and not something modern. People were doing it here before Rome fell.

Readers assume Kansas City is hometown convenience. I live here; I wrote what I could see. The convenience ran the other direction. Twenty years of research taught me I live somewhere strange: four traditions have looked at the same thirty miles of river bluff and concluded, in vocabularies that never borrowed from each other, that this ground matters.

The people who were already building underground

The Hopewell record is the oldest layer, and Missouri's prehistory runs deeper still. The state's rock art, documented over decades by Carol Diaz-Granados and James Duncan, is among the richest in the interior of the continent. What the mounds establish for my purposes is narrow and sufficient: chamber-building in this ground has a two-thousand-year precedent, on the public archaeological record, with no help from me.

The trilogy does not stage a scene in a Hopewell mound. It inherits a habit.

The name for the thing

The second tradition gave the trilogy its central abstraction, and the credit belongs upstream. The Osage, whose historic corridor runs through western Missouri, carry the concept of Wah-kon-tah, rendered variously across sources and periods, often described as the sacred mystery that connects everything. It is not a doctrine so much as a relationship: practiced, not notated.

That distinction is the quiet joke underneath eight hundred pages. Masters X is a story about people building instruments, protocols, and chambers to reach something one of this continent's older traditions simply practiced without the machinery. When the novels gesture at what the Foundation is chasing, they are gesturing at something the Osage already had a name for. The name is not mine, and the books say so.

Zion, sixty-three acres of it

The third tradition arrives with a date and a survey plat. In July 1831, Joseph Smith declared Independence, Missouri, twenty minutes from my desk, the "center place" of Zion, recorded in Doctrine and Covenants 57. The theology that grew around the declaration is more specific than most people outside the Latter Day Saint movement realize: the Garden of Eden placed in Jackson County, Adam-ondi-Ahman seventy miles north, a city plat drawn with sites for twenty-four temples. On August 3, 1831, a sixty-three-acre parcel, the Temple Lot, was dedicated for the first of them.

The temple was never built. The lot was never abandoned. It is maintained today, held by denominations of the movement, a mown green rectangle keeping a hundred-ninety-year-old appointment.

Whatever a reader makes of the theology, the fact pattern is extraordinary: an American prairie town formally designated the center of a cosmology, with the designation still honored by its inheritors.

And this is the claim readers most expect me to have exploited, so: there is no Temple Lot scene in the trilogy. We checked during the research audit. The site exerts gravity on the story without ever appearing in it, which is how significance actually behaves.

One more building belongs here. In 1994 the Community of Christ completed its temple in Independence, designed by architect Gyo Obata, a sanctuary that spirals a hundred ninety-five feet upward on a nautilus form. Readers who know the trilogy's fixation on growth ratios assume I invented a Fibonacci spire standing in the prophesied Zion. I did not. The building is there, and it is open to visitors.

The constant is the stone

Under the mounds, the corridor, and the platted Zion runs the fourth tradition, which is not a belief system but a rock formation.

The Bethany Falls limestone was laid down when the middle of North America was a shallow sea. Kansas City's builders quarried it hard through the boom of 1880 to 1920, raising the city above while hollowing the ground beneath. By mid-century someone noticed the worked-out chambers held a constant sixty-five degrees year-round, so the quarries became warehouses, and the warehouses became SubTropolis: roughly fifty-five million square feet of excavated footprint, the largest underground business complex in the world, with about seventeen hundred people commuting into the ground every working day.

The trilogy's bedrock tunnels run 160 feet down. The real facility's depth is in the same conversation. Some settings you do not have to invent.

The stone also handed the trilogy's most quoted line its documentary anchor. In 2011 Westport Presbyterian Church burned, losing roof and interior and everything combustible, except the 1904 limestone walls, which stood. "The stone remembers" was in the manuscript before I found the fire that proved it. That one gets its own essay.

What nobody has measured

Here is the gap. There is no published acoustics study of the Kansas City underground. The chambers of Malta and Britain have been recorded and analyzed. The chambers under my own city have not. The fiction is sited here, not sourced here. Whatever Bethany Falls limestone does to a standing wave at 160 feet, nobody has put it in a journal, and that is a silence a novel may fill, provided it admits it is filling one.

What the novel adds

Four traditions, then. The mound builders who cut rooms into this ground. The Osage, who named the connecting mystery. The Latter Day Saint movement, which surveyed Zion here to the acre. And the limestone, which made all the burrowing possible.

What the novel adds is a seeker who never existed: William Masters, an engineer who becomes convinced that something immense sleeps under this corridor, acquires limestone, builds a private research campus inside it, and documents everything in notebooks nobody reads for forty years. He is invented. The ground he is reacting to is not.

At some point, as Nadia says in the trilogy, coincidence becomes evidence. My job is to keep telling you which side of that line each sentence is standing on. The mounds, the corridor, the plat, and the limestone are in the world. The convergence that explains them is in the novels.

Three Factions, One Declassified Document

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 6 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

The document that gave the Masters X Trilogy its politics is a translation. In 1984, American intelligence took a Chinese government journal on paranormal research, rendered it into English for internal circulation, and filed it. It sits today in the CIA's declassified reading room under the RDP96-00792R designation, alongside a great deal of similar material from the same program years.

Readers assume the trilogy's three factions, the Keepers and the Custodians and the Completion Sect, were engineered for plot. Three-way conflicts are how thrillers manufacture pressure, and the assumption is fair. It is also backwards. I did not invent the three positions. I found them in the file, already arguing.

One government published. The other classified.

Start with what the document is, because its shape is the whole argument.

On one side sits a state research establishment studying claims of extraordinary human capacities in the open: a commission, conferences, funded experiments, a journal you could subscribe to. On the other side sits an intelligence agency that reads the journal, translates it, and stamps the translation.

Two governments, one subject, opposite policies. One published. The other classified the act of reading.

That asymmetry is the political physics of the entire trilogy, and it came from the record rather than from me. Everything the Keepers and the Custodians argue about across eight hundred pages is a costume drama built on one structural fact: secrecy and daylight are policies, chosen, and the choosing is where the story lives.

Three positions in the material

Inside the translated literature, the debate sorts into three recognizable postures. I want to describe them as a reader of the file rather than as a novelist who needed them to be tidy.

The first is suppression. The research drew condemnation as superstition, and "feudal sorcery" is the phrase that recurs in the era's polemics, carrying the full ideological weight of a modernizing state embarrassed by its own countryside. The program these critics wanted was straightforward: stop the work, discredit the workers, protect the institutions.

The second is verification. Other researchers and officials argued that if the phenomena were real, the only defensible response was to publish the methods, replicate the results, and let the claims live or die in daylight.

The third does not argue so much as attend. Military and security interest is present wherever a state studies a capacity that might be applied, and it is present here, most visibly in the fact that the translation exists at all.

Suppress, verify, weaponize. In the novels those become the Custodians, the Keepers, and the Completion Sect, institutionalized and given eight centuries of invented backstory. When Nadia briefs Blake on the factions' logic, she is doing historiography.

One caveat, and it is not a small one: the three-way sort is my reading of the translated material, not a heading inside it. The document is public. A reader who pulls it from the reading room and comes back with a better taxonomy will have earned the correction, and I would rather print that than defend a tidy structure.

Why a translation is stranger than a report

The genre is what made the file novelistically irresistible.

Intelligence agencies produce assessments of paranormal claims, complete with findings and confidence levels and recommendations, and those read exactly as you would expect. This is not that. This is raw open literature from one government, carried across a language barrier and filed by another.

A summary gives you conclusions. A translation gives you people arguing: the skeptics' polemics, the methodologists' appeals, the institutional throat-clearing, all preserved in their own voices because nobody bothered to smooth them out. Factions are made of people arguing. The three in my trilogy have the texture they have because their source was never a finding. It was a transcript of a fight.

The Army's own file

The American counterpart is the Gateway Process assessment, formally the Analysis and Assessment of Gateway Process, prepared for the U.S. Army in 1983. It is an internal evaluation of consciousness-alteration methods that examines the Monroe Institute's audio-driven techniques and attempts a theoretical account of how they might work.

Whatever a reader concludes about its physics, and the physics is where most readers get off the bus, the document's existence is the point. The American security establishment did not only read other governments' consciousness research. It commissioned its own assessment.

This is where the trilogy's 136.6 Hz rung comes from, the frequency the novels map to the Monroe focus-level framework, and the weakest rung on the ladder I laid out in The Frequency That Was Already There. The framework exists. The assessment exists. Neither is a body of scientific findings, and the novels do not get to pretend otherwise.

The fourth answer

Here the fiction stops transcribing and starts arguing.

The record offers three responses to a dangerous capacity: suppress it, verify it, weaponize it. The trilogy proposes a fourth, and it arrives not as a battle but as a file format. Andrew Chen's Distribution File runs two hundred forty-seven pages of preparation protocols, chamber specifications, harmonic derivations, a facilitator's manual, and an acoustic appendix, and it goes onto the open internet at midnight under CC0, the license that reserves nothing.

Chen's design brief is the trilogy's thesis in one move: the opposite of classification is not leaking. A leak preserves the scarcity that gives a secret its power and merely relocates it to whoever leaked. The opposite of classification is making the work boring, meaning reproducible and documented and free, until there is nothing left to guard and nobody left to guard it from.

None of that happened. No such file exists, no such release occurred, and every download figure in the novel's front matter is invented down to the digit. But it is fiction written as a direct reply to two real declassified documents, which is a different thing from fiction written in a vacuum.

What is in the world: the 1984 translation and its debate, the Gateway assessment, the Monroe framework as a documented body of practice. What is in the novels: the Keepers, the Custodians, the Completion Sect, their eight centuries, Nadia's briefing, Andrew Chen, and every page of the Distribution File. The factions' logic is transcribed. Only the names are ours.

The Stone Remembers: A Fire in Westport

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 7 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

"The stone remembers" sat in the Masters X manuscript for most of a decade on probation. It is Father Moreau's line, and it is exactly the kind of sentence a novelist should distrust: too round, too quotable, the metaphor claiming work the world never authorized.

Then a research pass turned up a fire four miles from my desk, and the line came off probation.

The correction that improved the book

The fire comes second. The correction comes first, because it is the less flattering story.

Early drafts described the Cathedral of the Immaculate Conception, the landmark on the bluff above the West Bottoms and the highest ground in downtown Kansas City, as limestone. It seemed safe. This is a limestone city, the trilogy is practically about the limestone, and the cathedral crowns that bluff two hundred feet above the floodplain. Of course it was limestone.

It is red brick. Built 1882 to 1912, brick throughout, with the gold-leaf dome added in 1960. Our church-by-church research pass caught the error before it reached print.

One wrong wall material in a novel might not seem worth the paragraph. It matters because of what this trilogy claims about ground truth. A book arguing that the physical city is stranger than fiction has noticed forfeits the argument the moment it gets the physical city wrong. Every real place in the books is rendered as exactly as I could manage, from the corner table at Miller Nichols Library to the kitchen in the Quality Hill apartment to the Hotel Phillips lobby, and the reader's map of the real Kansas City exists so anyone can check me.

The corrected fact turned out better than the assumed one. A red brick cathedral wearing a gold crown on the city's highest bluff, above a floodplain, is a more precise image of Kansas City than another limestone facade: the city's aspirations built in one material, its foundations in another.

The fire

Westport Presbyterian Church was built in 1904, in the neighborhood that was once the last outfitting stop for wagon trains heading west. Stone walls, timber roof, a century of congregational life inside.

In 2011 it burned. The fire took the roof. It took the interior, meaning sanctuary and woodwork and everything combustible that a hundred and seven years had accumulated. Photographs from the morning after show what fire photographs from stone buildings always show, and what I had somehow never registered until I was looking for it: the walls, standing. The 1904 limestone, smoke-darkened and roofless, holding its full height around a burned-out interior.

The congregation rebuilt inside the standing walls, which is its own sentence about what stone makes possible. The fire could erase the contents but not the fact of the church, because the fact was mineral.

What a church audit looks like

Both stories came out of the same instrument, which deserves a note of its own.

The research behind them is a building-by-building audit of Kansas City's historic religious architecture: construction dates, wall materials, fires, rebuilds, and siting, tracking which congregations built on the bluffs and which in the bottoms, and what the difference cost them across a century of floods and fires. It started as fact-checking for a handful of scenes and turned into something closer to a materials census, because every checked fact raised two more.

St. Francis Xavier's original church, for instance, burned in September 1925 and was answered with an exterior sculpture carved from twenty-one thousand pounds of Bedford limestone: a congregation replying to fire in the one vocabulary fire had respected.

The audit exists so that when the novels say stone, brick, 1904, or bluff, a reader with a library card can catch me if I am wrong. Once, as above, the audit caught me first.

The boom that built and hollowed the city

Both stories sit on the same half-century of geology-as-economics.

Between roughly 1880 and 1920, Kansas City quarried its own foundation. The Bethany Falls limestone came out of the bluffs and went up into the city: churches, warehouses, retaining walls, the bones of every neighborhood old enough to have bones. The boom built the city above and hollowed the ground beneath, and the hollowing never really stopped. It changed vocabulary, going from quarry to warehouse to SubTropolis, where roughly seventeen hundred people now work inside the mined-out seam.

So when the trilogy treats Kansas City as a place whose surface and depths are the same substance in different states, that is municipal history, not mysticism. The 1904 walls that survived the 2011 fire and the chambers 160 feet under the bluffs are the same formation, the same boom, the same stone in two postures.

Not much, in this one. A Document That Cannot Be Un-Released

The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 8 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press

Masters X ends at midnight with a file going onto the open internet. Two hundred forty-seven pages, released under a CC0 dedication, the license that reserves nothing, not even attribution. An eight-hundred-year war loses its object. There is nothing left to fight over. The file cannot be un-released.

Conspiracy trilogies are supposed to end with a chamber stormed and a patriarch unmasked. Mine ends with a license.

The artifact

The Distribution File is written as an artifact rather than a summary. Readers encounter its actual pages, and the omnibus carries it complete. Its five parts have the shape of a working manual: Preparation Protocols; Chamber Specifications; Harmonic Frequency Derivations; a Facilitator Training Manual; and an Acoustic Research Appendix. Every page carries the running footer this whole run of essays started with: f = 111.2 Hz.

Its front matter reports its own reception in the dry voice of a changelog, claiming 1,204,881 downloads in the first week, readers in forty-seven countries, and three independent replications of the core protocol. Andrew Chen, the Foundation researcher who compiles and releases it, appends a design brief.

The brief is invented. The argument inside it is not.

Make the work boring

The intuitive opposite of classification is leaking. Chen rejects the intuition.

A leak preserves everything that gives a secret its power: the scarcity, the drama, the sorting of the world into those who know and those who do not. It relocates that power to whoever did the leaking. The mystery survives its own exposure. Often it grows.

The actual opposite of classification is to make the work boring: documented to the point of tedium, reproducible by strangers, free to the point of worthlessness as property. Nothing left to guard, nobody left to guard it from, no initiation left to sell. Public domain as a one-way door.

Ending a mystery by ending its scarcity is not a compromise between the trilogy's three factions. It dissolves the board they were playing on.

Why a file format can carry an ending

A craft note, since "the trilogy ends with a document" is the sort of choice a writer should be able to defend.

Endings resolve pressure. For two and a half books, the pressure in Masters X is scarcity: one protocol, three factions, each defined entirely by its answer to the question of who may know. A confrontation would have resolved a battle and left the scarcity untouched. Whoever won the chamber becomes the next custodian, and the cycle continues one victor older.

The only ending that actually discharges that pressure destroys the scarcity itself. And scarcity of information is destroyed by exactly one instrument: unrestricted copying. Hence a license. The quietest legal object in the world doing what no gunfight in the genre has managed. The midnight timestamp is drama. The CC0 dedication is the plot.

What it argues with

Chen's brief is fiction. Its interlocutors are not.

Two declassified documents stand behind the trilogy's politics, and I walked both in Three Factions, One Declassified Document: a 1984 American translation of a Chinese government journal on paranormal research, one state publishing openly while another classified its reading of the publication; and the U.S. Army's Gateway Process assessment, a security establishment privately evaluating consciousness-alteration methods. Between them they preserve a real three-way fork: suppress, verify, weaponize.

The Distribution File is the fourth tine, and its real-world referent has nothing to do with the paranormal literature. It is the open-science tradition: reproducibility as arbiter, methods sections detailed enough for strangers to replicate, public archives against paywalled scarcity. CC0 itself is a real instrument from the Creative Commons toolbox, built for precisely the gesture Chen makes with it.

So the choice the file represents, daylight as policy, radical enough to be boring, is documented, with living practitioners. The file, the downloads, the replications: invented, every digit.

A trilogy published by a one-author imprint, arguing that the answer to gatekeeping is to publish the whole thing yourself, is at minimum practicing what it preaches.

Reprinting a fictional document honestly

One editorial problem, because it is the interesting one and I have not seen it handled well elsewhere.

The Distribution File is a fictional document that claims to be free. Reproduce it as an unmarked facsimile and you have manufactured exactly the thing the trilogy's ethics forbid: a fake primary source loose in the world, indistinguishable from a real one, which is how most forgeries begin their careers. Leave it out and you are describing the story's most important object secondhand.

The working rule is the same one everything else here runs on. Present the pages, label them, and shelve the real declassified sources alongside the fictional claims they parented, so a reader holds the fiction in one hand and its documentary parents in the other. A novel about ending secrecy deserves transparent packaging more than most.

The method, once

Every one of these essays ran on one rule, and it is the rule the trilogy exists to argue for: say what was measured, what was documented, and what was invented, and keep the three distinct where a reader can check them.

That is not modesty. A book claiming the world is stranger than fiction has noticed only earns the claim if it gets the world right.

Sentences were restructured rather than repunctuated. An em-dash usually does one of four jobs, and each got a different fix:

  • Parenthetical aside. Recast with commas, or split into its own sentence.
  • Appositive or definition. Replaced with a comma or a colon.
  • Dramatic pause before a final clause. Replaced with a period. This is the most common fix and it shortens the prose.
  • List introduction. Replaced with a colon.

En-dashes survive only in numeric ranges, which is standard typography and not a stylistic tell: 3600–2500 BCE, 110–111 Hz, 95–120 Hz. Everywhere a range appeared in running prose it was spelled out instead, as in "800 to 865 cycles per second."

Two side effects worth knowing. The essays got slightly shorter, since replacing a dramatic dash with a period tends to cut the trailing clause's throat-clearing. And a few sentences now use colons where they previously used dashes, so if colons start to feel like their own tic, that is the next thing to thin.
