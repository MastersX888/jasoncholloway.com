Chapter Twenty-Three. Andrew's Gift.

Three point nine one five hertz. Iceland.


October. The cottage smelled of October, the particular mix of cold air and warm stone that happened when the Icelandic temperature dropped below five degrees and the basalt walls began their winter work of holding heat against the darkness, the walls performing their geological function: absorb, store, release. The cycle of a material that had been doing this since the Pleistocene and would continue doing it long after the cottage returned to the lava field.

Blake was at the desk. The Moleskine. The Breitling. The grimoires pushed to the side, the Gospel of Thomas in the center, the new arrangement, the new altar, the thinnest text in the position of primacy because the thinnest text had said the most.

Andrew came in. He was carrying his laptop, not in the usual way, the way he carried it to the monitoring station or to the kitchen table where he backed up data and ate sandwiches and performed the logistical maintenance that kept the Foundation's research infrastructure running from the inside of a stone cottage in a country that was itself a geological experiment. He was carrying the laptop in both hands, the way a person carries something they have been deciding whether to deliver.

"I need to show you something."

Blake looked up. Andrew's face was doing the thing, the opening, the architectural reveal, the surface giving way to the structure beneath it. The third time Blake had seen this expression. The first had been the four forty-four point eight hertz event. The second had been the Moleskine pages. Now this.

Andrew set the laptop on the desk. Opened it. Turned the screen toward Blake.

`distribution_protocol.md`

247 pages. Blake scrolled. He scrolled slowly, the tremor making the trackpad navigation seismographic, each scroll adding its own annotation, the same way the potholes of the Volta road had added their annotations to his Moleskine notes. He read the first page. The second. The table of contents: seven tiers of institutional distribution. The encryption schemas. The access credentials. The graduated release protocol, Tier 1 institutions first, Tier 2 after Tier 1 demonstrated responsible handling, Tier 3 after Tier 2. The preparation protocol applied to information. The fifty-two weeks applied to knowledge.

He looked up.

"How long?"

"Three years."

"Three years."

"I started after the Strahov session, the one where your EEG first showed the three thirty-three point six hertz resonance, the first whole-number harmonic above the fundamental. I looked at the data and I thought: this is real. And then I thought: if this is real, it needs to survive us."

"Us."

"You and me. Nadia. The Foundation. Iceland. The entire fragile architecture that we've built around seven caves and a tremor and a collection of medieval manuscripts. I looked at all of it, the data, the protocols, the monitoring records, the Moleskines, and I saw what the Strahov monks saw when they sealed the manuscripts in lead: knowledge that was too important to store in only one place."

Blake sat with this. The monitor hummed. The Breitling ticked. Outside, the October wind performed its October work, pushing the cold across the lava field with the persistence of a system that did not stop because stopping was not in its vocabulary.

"You've been building the crypt."

Andrew's face opened further, the full reveal now, the architecture beneath the architecture, the Andrew beneath the monitoring station, the man beneath the engineer. "Yes. I've been building the crypt."

"You didn't tell me."

"No."

"Why?"

Andrew sat down. Not in the monitoring chair, in the other chair, the one beside the desk, the one that was technically Blake's reading chair and that Andrew had never sat in because the chair belonged to Blake's work and Andrew's work belonged to the monitoring station and the division of labor was part of the architecture of their friendship. But now he sat in Blake's chair, and the sitting was itself a message: the architecture is changing. The walls between our work are dissolving. The thing I've been building in my room with the east-facing window is not my work. It is our work.

"Because if I told you, you would have asked why I was building an exit plan. And I would have had to tell you about the Stanford email. And the Stanford email would have become a conversation about whether I was leaving, and I didn't want the conversation about whether I was leaving because the conversation would have distracted us from the question of whether the work survives."

"Stanford."

"An endowed chair. Computational neuroscience. Sarah Chen offered it personally."

Blake looked at Andrew. Looked at the laptop. Looked at the 247 pages that represented three years of silent work, three years during which Andrew had been simultaneously monitoring Blake's frequencies, maintaining the Foundation's infrastructure, making sandwiches, providing blankets, loosening water-bottle caps, and building, in the narrow room with the east-facing window, the institutional architecture that would ensure the knowledge survived regardless of what happened to the two men who discovered it.

"Are you going?"

Andrew closed the laptop. Set his hands on the closed lid, the gesture of a man who has been holding something for three years and is now, finally, setting it down.

"I deleted the email."

"When?"

"Last night. After your session. After the Moleskine pages. After you wrote that you were going again. I sat in my room and I looked at the Stanford email, and I looked at the distribution protocol, and I understood that the two things were contradictory. The distribution protocol was built to ensure the work survives without me. The Stanford email was an invitation to leave. If I leave, the protocol activates. If I stay, the protocol waits." He paused. "I choose to make the protocol wait."

"Andrew -"

"Don't. Don't thank me. Don't make this into a thing. I'm not staying for you. I'm not staying for the work. I'm staying because the protocol needs a guardian, and the guardian needs to be someone who understands both the data and the friendship, and the friendship is not documented in the protocol because the friendship is the thing the protocol cannot contain."

He stood. Picked up the laptop. Started to leave. Stopped at the doorway, the basalt frame that was not a door, just an opening in a wall, the cottage having no internal doors because the cottage was designed by people who understood that walls were sufficient and doors were institutional.

"I built the distribution system to ensure that the knowledge survives you, Blake. But the knowledge includes me. And I'm not ready to be distributed. Not yet."

He left. The room was quiet. The Breitling ticked. The wind pressed.

Blake sat at the desk and looked at the space where Andrew had been sitting. Blake's chair, the reading chair, the chair that now held the impression of Andrew's weight, the cushion compressed by the body of a man who had spent three years building a crypt and had just decided to stay alive instead.

He picked up the Moleskine. Wrote:

Andrew is the seal. He has been the seal the entire time. The preparation protocol requires a guardian, someone who sits at the entrance and monitors and does not enter. The monks had this. The Ashanti drummers had this. Every tradition that found the frequency also found the person who watches. Andrew is the watcher. He chose to keep watching.

He set down the pen. Looked at his hands. The tremor continued, one eleven point two hertz, the fundamental, the frequency that was in his hands and had always been in his hands and would be in his hands until the hands stopped trembling, which would be when the hands stopped living, which would be the only event the distribution protocol was designed to survive.

He looked at the doorway where Andrew had stood.

"Thank you," he said, to the empty room. The basalt heard it. The basalt would remember.
