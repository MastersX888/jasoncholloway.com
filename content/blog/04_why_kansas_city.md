# Why Kansas City? The Ground Itself Is Significant

*The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 4 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press*

When people learn that the Masters X Trilogy is a conspiracy novel, they assume Rome. When they learn it involves medieval manuscripts, they assume Prague — and the story does get to Prague. But the gravity well of the whole trilogy is Kansas City, Missouri, and readers regularly ask whether that was hometown convenience. I live here; I wrote what I could see.

The honest answer is that the convenience ran the other direction. I did not choose Kansas City because I live here. I came to understand, over twenty years of research, that I live somewhere genuinely strange — that five independent traditions across two thousand years looked at the same thirty miles of Missouri river bluff and concluded, in five unrelated vocabularies, that this ground matters. The novel didn't invent that convergence. It just needed a mechanism to explain it.

This essay lays out four of the five. The fifth gets an essay of his own.

## The people who were already building underground

Start two thousand years back. The Hopewell tradition — the mound-building culture that spread along the river systems of eastern North America between roughly 100 BCE and 700 CE — reached its westernmost frontier at the Missouri River bluffs near present-day Kansas City. About thirty sites are documented in the area. And the detail that stopped me when I first read the site reports: some of these mounds contained stone-vault tombs — constructed chambers, entered by doorways, built *inside* the earthworks.

Which means the impulse the trilogy is built on — cutting significant chambers into this specific ground — is not a modern invention and not a fictional one. People were building rooms in this ground before Rome fell. Missouri's prehistoric record runs deeper still: the state's cave art, documented by Carol Diaz-Granados and James Duncan, is among the richest in the interior of the continent. The [Kansas City reader's map](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/kansas-city-locations/) holds the geography.

## The name for the thing

The second tradition supplied the trilogy with its central abstraction, and I want to be careful to credit the source rather than absorb it. The Osage, whose historic corridor runs through western Missouri, carry the concept of *Wah-kon-tah* — often rendered as the sacred mystery that connects everything. It is an attunement tradition: a relationship to the connecting mystery, practiced rather than notated.

The trilogy is, at bottom, a story about people trying to build instruments for something that one of this continent's older traditions simply *practiced*, without needing the machinery. When the novels gesture at what the Foundation is chasing, they are gesturing at something the Osage already had a name for. The name is not mine, and the books say so.

## Zion, sixty-three acres of it

The third tradition arrives with a date and a survey plat. In July 1831, Joseph Smith declared Independence, Missouri — twenty minutes from my desk — to be the "center place" of Zion, recorded in Doctrine and Covenants 57. The theology that grew around the declaration is more specific than most people outside the Latter Day Saint movement realize: the Garden of Eden located in Jackson County; Adam-ondi-Ahman seventy miles north; a city plat drawn with sites for twenty-four temples. On August 3, 1831, a sixty-three-acre parcel — the Temple Lot — was dedicated for the first of them.

The temple was never built. The lot was never abandoned. It is maintained today, kept by three denominations of the movement, a mown green rectangle holding a hundred-ninety-year-old appointment. Whatever a reader makes of the theology, the *fact pattern* is extraordinary: an American prairie town, formally designated the center of a cosmology, with the designation still honored by its inheritors.

And here is the encyclopedia's honesty rule in action, because this is the claim readers most expect me to have exploited: there is no Temple Lot scene in the trilogy. We checked, during the research audit. The site exerts gravity on the story without ever appearing in it — which is, I'd argue, how significance actually behaves.

One more building belongs in this section, because it is the tradition's rhyme with the trilogy's other obsession. In 1994 the Community of Christ completed its temple in Independence — architect Gyo Obata, a sanctuary that spirals a hundred ninety-five feet upward on a nautilus form. The Fibonacci sequence, as literal architectural DNA, standing in the prophesied Zion. Readers assume I invented that one. I did not.

## The constant is the stone

Under all of these — under the mounds, the corridor, the platted Zion — runs the fourth tradition, which is not a belief system but a rock formation. The Bethany Falls limestone was deposited roughly 270 million years ago, when the middle of North America was a shallow sea. Kansas City's builders quarried it through the boom of 1880 to 1920, building the city above while hollowing the ground beneath. By mid-century, someone noticed that the worked-out chambers held a constant 65 degrees year-round, and the quarries became warehouses, and the warehouses became [SubTropolis](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/subtropolis/): fifty-five million square feet of excavated limestone, the largest underground business complex in the world, with roughly seventeen hundred people commuting into the ground every working day. The trilogy's bedrock tunnels run 160 feet down; the real facility's depth is in the same conversation. Some settings you don't have to invent.

The stone also supplied the trilogy's most quoted line with its documentary anchor. In 2011, Westport Presbyterian Church burned — roof, interior, everything — except the 1904 limestone walls, which stood. "The stone remembers" was in the manuscript before I found the fire that proved it. That story gets its own essay later in this series.

And one honest gap, stated plainly because the pattern of this series demands it: there is no published acoustics study of the Kansas City underground. The chambers of Malta and Britain have been measured; the chambers under my own city have not. The fiction is *sited* here, not *sourced* here. Whatever the Bethany Falls limestone does to a standing wave at 160 feet, nobody has put it in a journal — which is exactly the kind of silence a novel is entitled to fill, provided it says it is filling one.

## The fifth seeker

Four traditions, then: the mound builders who cut chambers into this ground; the Osage, who named the connecting mystery; the Latter Day Saint movement, which surveyed Zion here to the acre; and the limestone, which made all the burrowing possible and remembers everything built on it.

The fifth is a living man — an engineer with a prophetic calling and a hundred eighty underground acres of the same limestone, documented in land records and public reporting, whose real biography out-strange-ed anything I had drafted. He is the subject of the next essay, because compressing him into a paragraph would waste him. What the novel adds to all five is a fictional sixth: William Masters, whose Foundation inverts the pattern the real fifth seeker set.

At some point, as Nadia says in the trilogy, coincidence becomes evidence. The apparatus around the novels exists to tell you which side of that line every sentence stands on. The full five-tradition essay, with sources, arrives in the Masters X Universe Encyclopedia; the research layer is live now in the [Field Notes](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/), and the books are at [jasoncholloway.com](https://jasoncholloway.com/books/masters-x/).

The facts are in the files. The fiction is in the books.
