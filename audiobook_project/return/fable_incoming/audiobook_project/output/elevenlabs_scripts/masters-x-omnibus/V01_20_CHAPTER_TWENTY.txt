Chapter Twenty. The Seven Sites.

One hundred nine hertz. Kansas City.


Margaret knew.

Blake brought the evidence to her house. James's manuscript, the Strahov photographs, the timeline. He parked behind Ethan's truck, which hadn't moved in three days.

Margaret's house was the same as always. The red-brick ranch on Fifty-Third Street. Live oaks James had planted as saplings, now thirty feet tall. A basketball hoop over the garage, backboard cracked. Every house becomes a biography of the family that lives in it, and Margaret's was a biography of loss.

Inside: James's engineering awards still hung in the hallway. His father's handwriting on a framed patent. Evidence of a brilliant mind applied to ordinary problems because the extraordinary ones had been too dangerous to pursue.

Blake rehearsed his careful progression. Margaret set tea on the table and let him talk for about three minutes. Then she held up her hand.

"I know, Blake."

"You know what?"

"That your father did not die in an accident. That William was hiding something. That whatever they were investigating got James killed." Fourteen years of practice held her voice steady. "I've known since the week after the funeral."

"You never said anything."

"Silence runs in this family the way your patterns do. Passed down. The women just carry it differently."

She told him. The official-looking people who came after the funeral. James's files, taken. The clear implication about Blake's safety.

"They said accidents happen. And they made sure I understood what kind." Margaret's eyes were dry. "You were twelve. Your father was dead. And the people who killed him made it clear that silence was the price of your survival. So I paid it."

He tried to explain the patterns. Margaret listened. When he finished, she shook her head, not in denial. In recognition.

"You sound exactly like your father. The last year. I could see it was real to him. The most real thing in his life." She paused. "More real than us."

"It's not like that for me."

"It's always like that. The thing you can see that nobody else can. It becomes the most important thing." She looked at James's empty chair. "Your father loved us. And he loved the truth. And in the end, the truth was louder."

"He gave me something," Margaret said. A small lockbox from a cabinet Blake had never seen her open. A USB drive. A photograph of William at Oscar-01, 1963. And a letter.

Blake...

If you're reading this, I'm dead. Your grandfather embedded his knowledge in seven locations across Missouri. Oscar-01 is the key.

But proof isn't enough. Your grandfather had proof and chose silence. I'm choosing exposure. Both paths lead to death.

You need to find a third way.

I believe you can do it. Trust the motifs.

Love, Dad

James had named it before Blake. Before Miroslav. His father had already identified the problem: silence and exposure both led to death.

The USB contained a single folder: SEVEN SITES. William's complete documentation. Scanned photographs, annotated maps. Seven locations where pre-Columbian peoples had carved geometric sequences identical to the European manuscripts.

Oscar-01. SubTropolis. Meramec Caverns. The AT&T bunker at Polo. Atchison underground storage. Holden silo. And a seventh. Coordinates only, labeled "Echo": Flooded but still transmitting through water.

"He made a digital backup," Blake said. "Gave it to you. The Watchers took everything from the apartment. But they never really searched your house."

"They looked. But they were looking for James's work. Not William's. And this was behind my sewing supplies."

"Seven sites," Blake said. He was looking at the map. The locations forming the same proportional relationships as the Strahov ceiling. "He built a distributed preservation system. Together, they form the complete network."

"What does that mean?"

"The knowledge can't be suppressed by destroying one location. Or three. Or five. Grandpa spent forty years building insurance. For us."

"Your father spent five months trying to reach Oscar-01 before they killed him. He never got inside."

"He didn't have the key." Blake pulled the brass key from his pocket. "I do."

Margaret stared at the key. "Promise me you won't go alone."

"I have a team. Dad didn't. Grandpa didn't. But I do."

"And this woman. Nadia. You love her."

"Yes."

"Does she love you?"

"Yes."

"Then be careful with that. Love does not shield people from consequences. It just makes the consequences hurt more."

Blake hugged his mother.

"I'll be careful," he said.

"You'll be reckless. Just like your father." She released him. "But maybe this time, reckless is the right strategy."

Blake drove back to Gladstone with the USB and his father's letter. The pieces were assembling. The Third Way had a shape. Distributed verification, academic publication, public demonstration. So many sources that suppression became impossible.

Redundancy defeats suppression.

Three generations had paid for this moment.
