Chapter Eighteen. The Vance Doctrine.

Five fifty-six point zero hertz. Iceland.

[short pause]

Helena Vance published on a Tuesday.

The paper appeared in Nature Neuroscience, not in the journal's standard publication queue, which required an eight-month peer review cycle, but in its accelerated track, which required institutional sponsorship and an editorial determination that the findings were of sufficient urgency to justify bypassing the ordinary timeline. The accelerated track had been used seven times in the journal's history. Twice for pandemic-related neurology. Once for a breakthrough in consciousness studies that was later retracted. And now once for Vance.

The title: "Compressed Preparation Protocols for Acoustic Frequency Therapy: A Risk-Stratification Framework."

Blake read it at 5 AM Iceland time, sitting at the desk in the stone cottage with the Breitling ticking beside the laptop and the first light of the Icelandic summer beginning its slow pour across the lava field, the light that took three hours to fully arrive in July, as if the sun were being graduated in its own exposure, as if dawn understood preparation.

It was elegant. He could not deny this. Vance wrote with the clinical precision of a woman who had been publishing for twenty-five years and who understood that the most dangerous kind of writing was the kind that was competent enough to be trusted and wrong enough to be lethal. Her argument was simple: the Foundation's fifty-two-week protocol was designed for maximum safety but was not optimized for maximum access. If the goal was to make acoustic frequency therapy available to the greatest number of people, the preparation timeline was the bottleneck. She proposed a risk-stratification model, subjects would be screened using her proprietary neurological assessment tool, and those who scored above a certain threshold would be eligible for compressed protocols.

The triage argument. The argument that every emergency physician made when the waiting room was full and the resources were finite and the question was not what would save everyone but what would save the most. The argument that Blake understood and rejected, not because triage was wrong but because Vance's math was wrong, her denominator included all the people who would benefit from faster access and excluded all the people who would seize.

Andrew read it at 6 AM, having received Blake's email with the subject line: "Read this. Then call me."

Andrew called at 6:47 AM, which meant he had read it twice, once for content, once for methodology. Andrew always read methodology on the second pass because the first pass was for understanding what someone was claiming and the second pass was for understanding whether they were allowed to claim it.

"Her risk-stratification model is built on a sample of eleven."

"From the Maryland pilot."

"From the Maryland pilot. Six weeks. Three seizures. Two cardiac events. But the eight who didn't seize, she's using those eight as evidence that a subpopulation can tolerate compressed timelines." Andrew's voice had the particular flatness it took when he was angry in the way engineers are angry: not hot, not raised, but precise in a way that made the precision itself sound heated. "She's using the survivors to justify the protocol that created the casualties."

"The editorial board approved it."

"The editorial board doesn't know the data Teresa brought us. The editorial board doesn't know that Vance had the safety officer's report showing 34 percent adverse events. The editorial board sees a respected researcher with Georgetown institutional backing and a risk model that sounds rigorous because it uses the right vocabulary."

Blake closed the laptop. Looked at the whiteboard, the two trees, the angel map, the demon map. The same frequencies on both sides. Preparation and destruction.

"She's not wrong about the bottleneck," he said.

"No."

"If the preparation works, if four forty-four point eight hertz is what we think it is, then the fifty-two weeks is a barrier to access. There are people who will never sit in a cave for a year. The question is whether the barrier is a wall or a floor."

"The barrier is a seal," Andrew said. "You wrote it on the whiteboard. The seal is the preparation. Remove the seal and the demon is the angel. Same frequency. Different outcome."

"I know."

"Then don't concede the bottleneck argument. The bottleneck is the point. The bottleneck is the fifty-two weeks that separates the three who seize from the eight who don't. Vance's model identifies the eight. It does not protect the three."

[pause]

Father Emmanuel Crane Jr. contacted Nadia three days after the Vance paper.

He did not email, he called. The Foundation's main line, not Nadia's direct number, which meant he was approaching through the front door, through the institutional channel, with the particular formality of a man who understood that the way you enter an organization communicates as much as the reason you enter it.

David, the receptionist, transferred the call with the note: "Jesuit. Georgetown. Says he wants to discuss the Nature Neuroscience paper."

Nadia picked up the phone the way she picked up everything that arrived at the Foundation through channels she had designed: with the quality of a system receiving a signal it had been calibrated to detect.

"Ms. Volkov. My name is Emmanuel Crane. I'm a theologian at Georgetown. I've been retained by Cognigenics as a consultant, and I want to be transparent about that. I'm calling you because I believe we share a concern that neither Doctor Vance nor your husband has fully articulated."

"What concern is that, Father?"

"The urgency question. Doctor Vance's paper makes a utilitarian argument, faster access to the frequency means more people reached. Your Foundation's protocol makes a safety argument, slower preparation means fewer casualties. Both arguments are correct. Neither argument is sufficient. The question that neither of you has addressed is: what happens when the people who need this most are the people who have the least time?"

Nadia was silent. She recognized the argument, it was the argument that Teresa had warned her about. The argument that a Jesuit with a security clearance could make because a Jesuit with a security clearance understood both the theological urgency and the institutional timeline.

"Father Crane, are you asking me to shorten the protocol?"

"I'm asking you to consider that the protocol's length is itself a form of triage. Fifty-two weeks means you serve the people who can afford to wait a year. The people who cannot afford to wait a year, the terminally ill, the acutely suffering, the people for whom a year is the time they don't have, those people are triaged out. Your protocol is safe. But safe for whom?"

"Safe for everyone who completes it."

"And unavailable to everyone who can't."

The conversation lasted eighteen minutes. Nadia timed it. She timed everything. At the end, she said: "Father Crane, I will think about what you've said. I want you to know that I've thought about it before. Every triage protocol in the history of medicine starts with the question you've asked. And every triage protocol that collapses does so because someone answers the question by deciding that speed justifies sacrifice."

"And if the sacrifice is the suffering of the people you excluded?"

"Then the sacrifice is mine to bear. Not to resolve."

She hung up. Sat at her desk. Looked at the organizational chart on the wall, every name a person, every person a decision she had made about who belonged inside the institution and who did not. The chart was its own form of triage. Every institution was.

She opened her email. Composed a message to the Foundation's full board. Blake, Andrew, Sabrina, the three external advisors, the ethics officer. Subject line: "Crane Jr. Urgency Argument. Full Discussion Required."

She wrote the email in seven minutes. It was detailed, fair, and devastating. It presented Crane's argument without dismissing it. It presented her response without softening it. It ended with the sentence: "The question is not whether the protocol should be shorter. The question is whether we are willing to define the people we cannot help."

She sent it. Closed the laptop. The Foundation hummed around her, the server room, the HVAC, the stairwell light. The frequency of a building that was, for now, still resonating.
