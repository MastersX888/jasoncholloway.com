Chapter Four. The Accident.

One hundred nine hertz. Kansas City.

[short pause]

Two days since Jefferson City.

The notebooks were in his apartment now. Seven of them, locked in the fireproof safe he'd bought at Home Depot the morning after. He hadn't read them yet, not really. He'd managed a few pages of the first before closing it, hands shaking, head pounding, something behind his eyes straining toward a vibration it couldn't quite reach.

Whatever his grandfather had spent thirty years documenting, Blake's brain wasn't ready for it.

But it couldn't leave them alone either. He dreamed about the mathematical figures. Circles and triangles rotating through dimensions that had no names. He'd wake at 3:47, as always, and sit at the kitchen table staring at the safe in the closet, feeling the notebooks like a low hum at the base of his skull.

So here he was, back at Miller Nichols Library, pretending to work on his dissertation while his mind circled the contents of that safe. The notebooks had mentioned something. The Moreau collection. William had visited this library in 1967, 1972, and 1985. Had made notes about manuscripts hidden in plain sight where Blake had spent five years of graduate school and never known to look.

Blake closed his laptop. Walked toward the stacks.

[pause]

The Miller Nichols Library had secrets.

Not official ones, no locked cages, no forbidden sections. But Blake had worked security long enough to recognize things hidden in plain sight. A shelf unit slightly out of alignment. Catalog entries that didn't match the physical collection. A door marked MECHANICAL that was too clean to house machinery.

He started in Medieval History, where he'd spent most of his graduate career. But William's notebooks hadn't mentioned the canon. They'd mentioned something older. Something that shouldn't exist in a university library in Kansas City.

Blake moved deeper. Medieval History gave way to Religious Studies. Religious Studies to Comparative Religion. And Comparative Religion gave way to something the catalog called "Special Collections (Uncataloged)", a single shelf unit against a back wall where the fluorescent lights flickered and the air smelled like dust and forgetting.

And at the top of the shelf, nearly hidden behind a row of bound journals from the 1890s, a book with no title at all.

Dark leather, worn smooth by centuries of hands. No text on the spine. No library markings. No call number. Nothing that indicated this book belonged to any official collection.

Blake reached for the rolling ladder. Oak and brass, solid. He'd climbed it dozens of times. Tested the first rung. Climbed.

Three rungs up, his fingers just brushing the dark leather binding, the rung beneath his foot gave way.

Not cracked. Not broken. Just absent. As if the wood had decided to stop being solid.

Blake grabbed for the shelf. Missed. His shoulder struck wood. His hip caught a corner. And then his head met the tile floor, and the world went white.

[pause]

Not black, white.

Blake was aware of lying on his back. Cold tile beneath him. Pain radiating everywhere. But the pain was distant, his nervous system overwhelmed by input independent of the physical world.

The white light was full of shapes.

Proportional figures. Circles inscribed within circles, triangles nested at precise angles, rotating through dimensions his eyes couldn't track but his brain somehow followed.

He knew these shapes. Had seen them in his grandfather's notebooks. In the carvings beneath SubTropolis. In the flowstone at Meramec Caverns that William had traced with his fingers seventeen years ago.

The shapes pulsed. Once. Twice. In rhythm with something deeper than heartbeat, some fundamental frequency beneath the waking world.

See, something said. Not a voice. Information transmitted directly, bypassing his ears.

See.

The white began to fade. Cognition reassembled itself in fragments. The floor, the fluorescent lights, the smell of old books and his own blood where he'd bitten his tongue.

Everything too sharp, too detailed, edges too crisp, as if someone had adjusted the resolution of reality.

Something heavy pressed against his chest.

The book. The one with no title. It had fallen with him.

Blake opened the cover. No title page. Just pages covered in spatial arrangements. Circles within circles, triangles within triangles. The patterns moved. Or seemed to. Ink on vellum. But for a moment he could have sworn he'd seen them rotate.

He closed the book. Breathed.

[pause]

The cave was cold and the flashlight beam caught dust motes that hung in the air like frozen stars.

Blake was ten years old. His grandfather stood beside him in the darkness.

Meramec Caverns. Tourist attraction outside Sullivan, Missouri. But William had found a side passage, always did, everywhere they went.

"See the formations?" William's voice echoed off walls that had never felt artificial light. "See how they repeat?"

Blake saw rock. Stalactites dripping from the ceiling. Stalagmites rising to meet them. "They're just shapes," he said.

"Nothing in nature is random, Blake." William crouched, eyes level with his grandson's. He traced a finger along the stone curtain, following lines Blake couldn't see.

"It's the same pattern as the water that carved it. The same as the streams on the surface. The same as the rivers." His eyes gleamed. "And that structure is the same as the veins in your body. The same as the branches of a tree. The same as lightning."

"Goes all the way up?"

"All the way down, too." William smiled. "Motifs repeat at every scale. Quantum to cosmic. Everything connects."

He stood, turning the flashlight toward deeper darkness.

"Most people never see it," he said quietly. "They walk through a world made of pattern and beauty, and they see chaos." He looked down at Blake. "But you'll see it. Someday."

"How will I know when I'm ready?"

"You'll see the patterns everywhere. And once you start, you won't be able to stop."

"Then why would I want to see it?"

"Because it's the truth." William's hand tightened on Blake's shoulder. "And once you know the truth, you can only choose whether to run from it or follow it wherever it leads."

[pause]

He was on the floor and the floor was made of. Something. Something that had a name and the name was. Linoleum. The name was linoleum and linoleum was a frequency.

Blake came back to himself on the library floor. The fluorescent lights buzzed at sixty hertz. He could hear the frequency now, separate it from the building's electrical system, from the HVAC, from the almost-inaudible pulse of data moving through network cables in the walls.

He stood. The wallpaper was alive with structure. Hexagons tessellating. Fractals hiding in geometric repetition. The floor tiles. Below, the grain of the shelves. The dust motes in afternoon light. Everything connected. Everything singing.

His hands were trembling. Not from the fall. From the input. He gripped the shelf beside him. The wood grain was a river system matching the bronchial pattern of his lungs, matching the root structure of trees outside, matching the drainage map of Missouri. The same geometry. Everywhere.

A librarian at the end of the aisle. "Sir? Are you all right?"

"I'm fine. The ladder. I fell."

"You're bleeding."

Blake touched his head. Hand came away red. "Just need to clean up."

He looked at the ladder. The rung was intact. Solid oak. No cracks. It should not have broken.

But it had. And something had shattered open.

He tucked the book under his arm and walked toward the exit. The corridor was different. Below, the same corridor he'd walked a thousand times, but now the architectural proportions revealed themselves. The ratio was 1.618. The golden mean. His brain was reading it the way a musician reads sheet music.

October air hit him sharp and clean. Everything was different. The clouds formed fractals. The trees repeated themselves in the Fibonacci sequence. A student crossed the quad ahead of him, headphones on, her walking gait a pendulum oscillation. The crowd moving in patterns that looked random from the surface but from Blake's new elevation revealed themselves as coordinated.

He couldn't turn it off.

Across the street, the gray sedan.

He tried Andrew first.

Andrew picked up on one ring. "Blake. What's up."

"Something happened." Blake heard his own voice. Too fast, too bright. "In the library. I fell, and now I can. The patterns, Andrew. They're everywhere. In the trees, the clouds, the spacing of the streetlights. Everything is connected and I can see the connections."

A pause. The typing stopped.

"Describe what you're seeing," Andrew said. Not alarmed. Analytical. "Be specific."

"Spatial relationships. The ratio between the lamp posts. It's the same as the branching angle on that oak tree, which is the same as the Fibonacci sequence. The same form at every scale."

"Fractal self-similarity. Okay, that's consistent with heightened pattern recognition. I can build a model of that. Map the ratios, run them against known fractal dimensions, see if your perception matches mathematical reality or if it's pareidolia."

Blake closed his eyes. Opened them. The patterns were still there.

"It's not pareidolia," he said.

"You hit your head. Let me quantify this before you decide it's real."

Andrew was right. Methodologically. But the rightness was beside the point. Like describing a symphony by listing the frequencies.

"Build the model. I'll send you the ratios."

"Good. And go to urgent care. You probably have a concussion."

He hung up. Andrew would build something useful. He always did. But what Blake needed was someone who'd say I know what you're seeing and mean it from the inside.

Blake reached for his phone.

Two days he'd been carrying Nadia's number like a talisman.

The habit broke.

He pressed call.

She picked up immediately. Not "hello", just silence, as if she'd been expecting this.

"It's Blake. I fell. In the library. Something happened and I'm, the patterns, I can see, listen, I know how this sounds. I know how this sounds."

A pause. Then: "Tell me."

"I can't. Not on the phone. I can't put it into, it's not words, Nadia. Can you just come here?"

"Where are you?"

"UMKC. Outside the library. I think I have a concussion."

"Go home. Lock the door. Don't open the notebooks until I'm there." Her voice had shifted, urgency underneath the composure. "I'll come to you."

"Nadia -"

"Don't read the notebooks alone, Blake." She said his name differently than she'd said Mr. Masters. Softer. Her accent warming the vowel. "The patterns in those pages. They're not passive. They're designed to do something to the person looking at them. You need someone with you."

"You could have told me that three days ago."

"You wouldn't have believed me three days ago." Another pause. "You've seen them now, haven't you. The motifs. In everything."

Blake looked at the quad. Fractal clouds. Fibonacci trees. "Yes."

"Then you're ready." He heard her stand. The sound of a bag being shouldered. "I'll be there in an hour. Blake -" She stopped. Started again, and what she said wasn't what she'd planned to say. "It's going to be all right."

It was such a human thing to say. So far from the surveillance craft and the manufactured paper trail.

"I'll be here," Blake said.

He hung up. Walked home. Twenty-two blocks through a world singing in a language he was only beginning to understand, his head bleeding and the untitled book pressed against his ribs and Nadia's voice still in his ear, saying his name the way she'd said it.

Softer. Warmer. The accent on the vowel.

Like she'd been waiting to say it for a long time.
