# A Document That Cannot Be Un-Released

*The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 8 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press*

Conspiracy trilogies are supposed to end with a confrontation — the chamber stormed, the patriarch unmasked, the secret detonated or defended. Masters X ends with a file format. At midnight, in the trilogy's final movement, a two-hundred-forty-seven-page document goes onto the open internet under a CC0 dedication — the license that reserves nothing, not even attribution — and the story's eight-hundred-year war over a secret simply loses its object. There is nothing left to fight about. The file cannot be un-released.

This closing essay of the series is about that ending: what's inside the fictional document, the real-world record it argues with, and — as a making-of coda — the genuinely odd editorial problem of publishing a fictional document honestly.

## The artifact

The Distribution File is written as an artifact, not a summary — the trilogy's readers encounter its actual pages, and the omnibus carries it complete. Its five parts are the shape of a working manual: Preparation Protocols; Chamber Specifications; Harmonic Frequency Derivations; a Facilitator Training Manual; and an Acoustic Research Appendix. Every page carries the running footer this series opened with eight essays ago: f = 111.2 Hz.

Its front matter reports its own reception, in the dry voice of a changelog: 1,204,881 downloads in the first week; readers in forty-seven countries; three independent replications of the core protocol. Andrew Chen — the Foundation researcher who compiles and releases it — appends a design brief whose logic is the trilogy's thesis in miniature, and it is worth unpacking, because it is a real argument even though its occasion is invented.

## Chen's brief: make the work boring

The intuitive opposite of classification is leaking. Chen's brief rejects the intuition. A leak, the argument runs, preserves everything that gives a secret its power — the scarcity, the drama, the sorting of the world into those who know and those who don't — and merely relocates that power to whoever did the leaking. The mystery survives its own exposure; often it grows.

The actual opposite of classification is to make the work *boring*: documented to the point of tedium, reproducible by strangers, free to the point of worthlessness as property. Nothing left to guard; no one left to guard it from; no initiation left to sell. The midnight release under CC0 is the mechanism — public domain as a one-way door. Ending the mystery by ending the scarcity is not a compromise between the trilogy's factions. It is the move that dissolves the board they were playing on.

## Why a file format can carry an ending

A craft note, because "the trilogy ends with a document" is the kind of choice a writer should be able to defend. Endings resolve pressure, and for two and a half books the trilogy's pressure is scarcity: one protocol, guarded by three factions, each defined entirely by its answer to the question of who may know. A confrontation ending — chamber stormed, sect defeated — would have resolved a *battle* while leaving the scarcity intact; whoever won would simply become the new custodian, and the eight-hundred-year cycle would be one victor older. The only ending that actually discharges the pressure is one that destroys the scarcity itself, and scarcity of information is destroyed by exactly one instrument: unrestricted copying. Hence a license. The quietest legal object in the world, doing what no gunfight in the genre has ever managed — ending the story by making its secret unownable. The midnight timestamp is drama; the CC0 dedication is the plot.

## The real record it argues with

Chen's brief is fiction, but its interlocutor is not. The sixth essay in this series walked the two declassified documents behind the trilogy's politics: the 1984 CIA translation of a Chinese government journal on paranormal research — one state publishing openly, another classifying its reading of the publication — and the U.S. Army's Gateway Process assessment, a security establishment privately evaluating consciousness-alteration methods. Between them, the documents preserve the real three-way fork: suppress, verify, weaponize.

The Distribution File is the trilogy's fourth tine, and its real-world referent is not the paranormal literature at all — it is the open-science tradition. Reproducibility as the arbiter. Methods sections detailed enough that strangers can replicate. Public archives against paywalled scarcity. The CC0 dedication itself, a real instrument from the Creative Commons toolbox, built for exactly the gesture Chen makes with it. The fiction's argument, graded honestly: the *choice* the file represents — daylight as policy, radical enough to be boring — is a documented tradition with living practitioners. The file, the downloads, the replications: invented, every digit.

There is a small self-referential joke buried in the ending, and I may as well confess it: a trilogy published by a one-author imprint, arguing that the answer to institutional gatekeeping is to publish the whole apparatus yourself, is practicing what its ending preaches. Seventh City Press is not the Masters Foundation, but the two share a theory of daylight.

## Making-of: how do you reprint a fictional artifact honestly?

Which brings me to the coda, and a look inside the encyclopedia project this whole series has been trailing.

The Distribution File poses an editorial problem I have not seen a franchise apparatus handle well: it is a *fictional document that claims to be free*. Print it as an unmarked facsimile and you have manufactured exactly the ungraded artifact the trilogy's ethics forbid — a fake primary source loose in the world. Omit it and the encyclopedia is describing its universe's most important object secondhand.

The working solution runs on the same two-register rule as everything else. The reference edition of the Masters X Universe Encyclopedia excerpts the File as an annotated artifact — its pages present, its seams labeled, the real declassified sources shelved in the margins beside the fictional claims they parented. A collector's treatment — the full facsimile as an object — remains under consideration as exactly that: a labeled artifact of the fiction, not a document pretending to have escaped it. (For readers who follow the project's open decisions: the integration plan is drafted, the collector question is still open, and I'd rather ship it right than ship it soon.)

The encyclopedia's job, here as everywhere, is to keep the seam visible: in-universe fact on the left, research file on the right. A fictional document about ending secrecy deserves, of all things, transparent packaging.

## The series, closed

Eight essays, one method. The frequency that was already there, graded to the decimal. A grimoire condemned as cheating. A physics demonstration from 1787 holding up a medieval hypothesis. Five traditions on one riverbank, and the documented man who out-strange-ed the fiction. Three factions transcribed from a declassified file. A fire that took everything but the walls. And an ending that is a license, not a battle.

Every one of those essays ran on the same rule, and it is the rule the trilogy exists to argue for: label the seam. What was measured, what was documented, what was invented — kept distinct, kept public, kept checkable. The full apparatus arrives in the Masters X Universe Encyclopedia from Seventh City Press. The research layer is live now in the [Field Notes](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/), and the books — including the omnibus, where the Distribution File runs complete — are at [jasoncholloway.com](https://jasoncholloway.com/books/masters-x/).

The facts are in the files. The fiction is in the books.
