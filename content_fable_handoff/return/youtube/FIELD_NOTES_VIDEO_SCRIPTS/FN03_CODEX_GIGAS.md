### HOOK (0:00–0:50)

[VISUAL: Tape measure extended to 92 cm on the desk, standing vertical if possible; then lay a facsimile page beside it.]

> Ninety-two centimeters tall. Fifty wide. Twenty-two thick. Seventy-five kilograms. The Codex Gigas — the Giant Book — is the largest surviving medieval manuscript in the world. Legend says one monk wrote it in a single night, with the Devil's help. The paleography says one scribe really did write it — over decades. Both versions are strange. Only one is documented.

[OST: `92 cm · 75 kg · one scribe`]

---

### BEAT 1 — The record (0:50–2:45)

> Documented: produced at the Benedictine monastery of Podlažice, Bohemia, early twelve hundreds. It contains the complete Latin Vulgate, Josephus, Isidore of Seville's Etymologiae, Cosmas of Prague's chronicle, medical texts — essentially a portable library of everything its era knew.
>
> The legend: a monk called Hermann the Recluse, sentenced to be walled up alive, vowing to produce the book in one night. The analysis: a single hand, consistent across the whole manuscript, working for decades. The legend compresses a lifetime into a night. The truth is arguably harder to believe.

[OST: `Podlažice · early 1200s`]

---

### BEAT 2 — The journey (2:45–4:30)

> Rudolf II acquired it for Prague Castle — the same collection that held the Voynich Manuscript. In 1648, in the last months of the Thirty Years War, the Swedish army took it from Prague as war booty. It has been in Stockholm ever since — the National Library of Sweden — first publicly displayed in 2007, now fully digitized at codexgigas.se.

[OST: `Prague → Stockholm, 1648`]

---

### BEAT 3 — The seam (4:30–6:00)

[VISUAL: To-camera.]

> The label. My trilogy's prologue is set in Bohemia, 1267 — about forty years after the Codex Gigas was finished, close enough that its world is the prologue's world: the parchment costs, the lamp black, the iron gall ink, the physical labor of a manuscript at that scale. Brother Aldric, my prologue's monk, is completing a *different* manuscript — one dispersed across seven cities. Aldric, his manuscript, and the dispersal are fiction.
>
> One placement is deliberate: the trilogy's seven-city scheme includes Uppsala, Sweden — because that is where the real book went. The fiction follows the fact's itinerary.

[OST: `1267 · Aldric — invented · the journey — real`]
