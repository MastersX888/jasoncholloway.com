Chapter Eighteen. Father's Truth.

One eleven point two hertz. Kansas City.


Miroslav gave Blake his father's manuscript on their last night in Prague.

Not at the apartment. At the Strahov Library, after hours, the two of them alone in the Theological Hall while Nadia transcribed Latin in the corridor outside.

The Theological Hall at night was a different space. Without fluorescents, without crowds, the barrel-vaulted ceiling frescoes glowed faintly in Miroslav's lamplight. The plaster ceiling was a continuous geometric field. The trompe l'œil columns encoding proportional relationships that matched the cave carvings exactly.

Miroslav poured two glasses of the Moravian Riesling.

"James sent this to a friend via encrypted email," Miroslav said. "September twenty ten. Two days before he died. The Keepers intercepted it. The friend never received it." He set the folder on the reading desk. "It's been here for fourteen years."

The Motif Watchers. Two hundred forty pages. The last thing James Andrew Masters had written.

Blake reached for the folder instead of the wine.

"Wine can wait," Blake said.

"Wine always waits. That's its purpose."

Blake opened the folder. His father's voice rose from rough prose. An engineer's sentences, direct and functional. Three generations discovering geometric patterns. A grandfather who chose silence. A son who tried to break it.

Blake recognized scenes from his childhood. Transmuted. The birdwatching trips became "nature walks." The Breitling became "a pilot's chronograph." Kansas City became Louisville. His father had been encoding their family history as fiction. Not to teach the patterns. To preserve the STORY. What it felt like to be William, choosing silence. What it cost to be James, choosing to break it.

The final chapter ended mid-sentence:

David stood in the missile facility, his grandfather's key in his hand, and understood what William had been trying to -

September 15, 2010. The day James died.

"He knew the risk," Miroslav said. "We warned him. Offered protection. He refused."

"How did he die?" Blake asked.

Miroslav pulled out a police report. Highway 50 near the Oscar-01 facility. Single vehicle accident. Eleven PM. No witnesses.

"He'd been investigating the Oscar-01 site," Miroslav said. "The Watchers had been following him for months. We don't know if they caused the accident directly or if he simply fell asleep."

"Does it matter? They were watching. They let it happen."

"Yes. That's enough."

Blake went to the corridor. Nadia was sitting on the stone floor, transcription materials around her. She read his face.

"How bad?"

"Come see."

She read the police report first. Then the manuscript. When she finished, she set the pages down carefully.

"He was trying to do what we did at the conference," she said. "But alone. Without anyone to confirm the pattern was real."

"Without a control tower."

"Without anything. He was flying blind. And they shot him down."

"We go home," Blake said. "Take everything. Strahov documentation, conference materials, James's manuscript. And we figure out the demonstration. Not forced. Not overwhelming. Distributed. Gradual. So many sources that no single faction can suppress them all."

"Redundancy defeats suppression," Nadia said.

"We implement it together. With Andrew's infrastructure. With the Keepers' support."

Nadia looked at the manuscript. The unfinished sentence.

"He didn't have someone to read the drafts," she said. "Someone to say 'this is scholarship' and 'this is paranoia.'"

"Together," Blake said.

"Together," she said.

Miroslav watched from his chair. The old man seeing in these two people what William had lacked. What James had lacked.

Partnership. The thing that made the Third Way possible.



They left Prague the next morning.

Miroslav walked them to the bookshop door. "I waited sixty years. William came alone. James came alone. But you brought someone. That's the difference."

Nadia embraced him. When they separated, Miroslav's eyes were wet.

"The Moravian Riesling," Miroslav said. "If you come back, we'll drink another bottle."

"When," Nadia corrected. "Not if."

They walked through Malá Strana in early morning. Prague waking around them.

On the plane, Nadia fell asleep against his shoulder. Blake sat still. Her hair against his jaw. His perception offered data. Her heart rate, her breathing pattern. He declined all of it. Let the warmth be warmth.

He read his father's manuscript while she slept. James's prose was rough where Blake would have been precise. But buried in the rough prose were moments that hit like percussion. A scene where the grandfather takes his grandson to a cave and says: "The world is showing you something. Don't look away."

And the final, unfinished scene. David standing at a threshold, reaching for an understanding he'd never complete.

James had imagined Nadia. Not specifically. The details were wrong. But the architecture was the same. The perceiver needs a scholar. Every generation learns this.

"How long was I out?" Nadia asked, waking.

"Three hours."

She straightened. Reached for her Latin transcription.

He loved her. He was losing her. Both things were true. The gift that connected him to everything was the gift that separated him from the person who mattered most.

For now, they were together. Flying home. With a father's unfinished sentence to complete.

For now was enough.
