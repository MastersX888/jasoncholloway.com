# The Grimoire That Was Actually a Study Aid

*The Facts Behind the Fiction, No. 2 · Jason Carroll Holloway · Seventh City Press*

There is a moment in *The Inheritance of Frequency* when Nadia, sitting on a kitchen floor with a facsimile page between her hands, says the line the whole trilogy pivots on: "The Ars Notoria isn't magic. It's technology. Cognitive technology." Readers tend to assume I invented the manuscript she is holding. I did not. The Ars Notoria is real, it is catalogued, and you can request it at the British Library.

What I invented is narrower than that — and stranger for its narrowness. Let me walk the seam.

## The manuscript on the record

The Ars Notoria — the Notory Art — belongs to the Solomonic tradition, the family of medieval texts attributed, at least nominally, to the biblical King Solomon. The earliest surviving manuscripts date to the mid-thirteenth century. Institutional copies survive across Europe, including British Library MS Sloane 1712 and holdings at the Bibliothèque nationale de France. Robert Turner translated it into English in 1657; modern scholarly editions and translations exist, and serious scholars — Claire Fanger and Julien Véronèse among them — have spent careers on it.

What the text promises is remarkable enough that no fiction writer would dare invent it from scratch: the rapid acquisition of the seven liberal arts — grammar, rhetoric, logic, arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy — through the structured contemplation of geometric figures called *notae*, combined with specific prayers, in a prescribed schedule of practice. Not wealth, not love, not power over demons. Knowledge, memory, and eloquence, on an accelerated timetable. It is, on its face, a medieval manual for learning faster.

The notae themselves are the heart of it. They are dense, elaborate diagrams — geometric structures carrying prayers in Latin, Hebrew, Greek, and occasionally scripts nobody has identified — meant to be gazed at in sequence while the accompanying orations are recited. As objects, they are unlike anything else in medieval book culture: not illustrations of a text but apparently *instruments* of it. The full account, with sources, is in the [Ars Notoria Field Note](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/ars-notoria/).

## Condemned as cheating

Here is the detail that tells you how seriously the medieval world took this book: it was condemned — by the Inquisition, and by no less an authority than Albertus Magnus. And the grounds of the condemnation are the interesting part. The core objection was not devil-worship. It was that the Ars Notoria promised an illicit shortcut — knowledge acquired without the sanctioned labor of study. It was condemned, in effect, as cheating.

Institutions rarely mobilize against texts they consider trivial. Nobody convenes a tribunal over a book that doesn't work and that nobody reads. The vehemence of the response suggests the manuscript was circulating widely and being taken seriously by exactly the people who could afford to commission copies — which is to say, by the educated class the universities were built to produce. Whether the readers were right to take it seriously is a separate question. That they did is documented.

## How a banned book survives

There is a bibliographic subplot here worth a paragraph, because it shapes how the trilogy uses the text. Condemned books in the manuscript era did not disappear; they went expensive. Copies of the Ars Notoria continued to be produced for centuries after the condemnations — on good vellum, with the notae executed carefully, which means patrons kept paying scribes serious money for a text the authorities had ruled against. By 1657 it had crossed into print in English through Robert Turner's translation, and from there into the long tail of early-modern occult publishing that eventually delivered it to modern editors. A text survives seven hundred years of official disapproval only if a chain of individual readers, generation after generation, privately concluded it was worth the risk and the price. The trilogy's Keepers are fiction; the behavior they are modeled on — quiet, well-funded, multi-generational custodianship of a condemned text — is just the Ars Notoria's actual transmission history, read as a plot.

## The pattern a modern reader can't unsee

Now the middle register — not measurement, not invention, but the observation that sits between them.

Strip away the angelic language and describe the Ars Notoria's method operationally: extended visual contemplation of complex structured figures; recitation on a fixed schedule; prescribed postures; repetition across weeks and months; a curriculum that sequences material from foundational to advanced. A cognitive scientist reading that description will recognize elaborative encoding, spaced repetition, and structured retrieval practice — the load-bearing findings of modern memory research — seven centuries before anyone had the vocabulary.

Does that mean medieval practitioners empirically discovered spaced repetition and encoded it as prayer? It might. It might equally be a coincidence of forms — ritual happens to be repetitive, and repetition happens to aid memory. The scholarly literature treats the resemblance with appropriate care, and so do I. But the resemblance is real, it is striking, and it is the documented foundation the fiction stands on. A grimoire that maps onto genuine learning science is a far better inheritance for a novel than a grimoire that maps onto nothing.

## Where the fiction begins: efficacy

So here is the seam, stated as plainly as I can state it.

Real: the manuscript, the notae, the promise of accelerated learning, the condemnation, the surviving copies, the scholarly reappraisal of the text as something more interesting than superstition.

Invented: that it works — and *how* it works. Nadia's argument, developed across two volumes, is that the notae are interfaces: calibrated visual objects designed to produce specific neurological states in a sufficiently prepared observer, with the manuscript's fasting rules, postures, and prayer sequences functioning as a protocol for reaching the required state of cortical coherence before engaging the figures. That reading is the novel's invention. No historian makes it. No cognitive scientist makes it. It is the trilogy's one experiment the lab has never run — and in *The Grimoire*, the lab runs it.

I will flag one further invention, because it connects this essay to the next one: the trilogy's claim that the notae are *frozen sound* — cymatics patterns copied from standing waves by monks who watched geometry form in water and sand. The figures are real. The mechanism is mine. Essay three, on Chladni and Hans Jenny, walks that particular seam in full, and the [Cymatics Field Note](https://jasoncholloway.com/field-notes/cymatics/) holds the physics.

## Why this manuscript, of all manuscripts

The occult corpus is enormous. The trilogy could have run on the Lesser Key, on Agrippa, on Dee's Enochian tables — all of which sit in my research library and none of which carries the story. The Ars Notoria does, for one reason: it is the only major text in that tradition whose stated purpose is *cognition*. It doesn't summon anything. It doesn't bind anything. It claims to change what a mind can hold and how fast. A trilogy about perception — about what a prepared human being might register that an unprepared one cannot — needed an operational manual, and the thirteenth century had already written one and been condemned for it.

The manuscript is real. Nadia's reading of it is the novel.
