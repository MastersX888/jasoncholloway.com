### BEAT 1 — Eco's machine (0:45–2:45)

> Eco's novel follows three editors who, as a game, feed occult fragments into a machine and generate a master conspiracy — the Plan. The mechanism Eco exposes: connect enough dots and the connections start generating themselves. The pattern-hunter doesn't find the pattern. He manufactures it. And then the Plan turns real in the worst way — because other people believe it.
>
> That is the knife every conspiracy novel walks. Most fall on it.

[OST: `The Plan makes itself.`]

---

### BEAT 2 — The occupational hazard (2:45–4:30)

> Here is the honest version, to camera: I spent twenty years assembling real research for these books — archaeoacoustics, medieval manuscripts, declassified files, Kansas City land records. That is exactly the raw material Eco's editors fed their machine. The difference between research and the Plan is not the material. It is the discipline of labeling.
>
> Philip K. Dick modeled the other half of that discipline: a decade on the Exegesis, interrogating one experience, doubting it nightly, starting again from zero. Blake's 3 AM re-checking is borrowed straight from that practice. Enhanced perception, honestly written, looks a lot like torment.

[OST: `Doubt is the method.`]

---

### BEAT 3 — The two-register rule (4:30–6:00)

[VISUAL: Two index cards placed side by side: `IN THE WORLD` / `IN THE NOVELS`.]

> So the trilogy's apparatus runs on one rule with two registers: what is in the world, and what is in the novels — and every claim files under exactly one. The frequency measured in Maltese stone: in the world. The decimal I added: in the novels. The three manuscripts Rudolf II collected: in the world. The system connecting them: in the novels.
>
> Eco's warning, answered as method: you survive writing conspiracy by keeping score in public.

[OST: `in the world / in the novels`]
