Chapter Twelve. The Monthly Call.

One hundred nine hertz. Kansas City.


She scheduled the call for Saturday morning because Saturday was the day the Foundation didn't own her.

9 AM Kansas City, 4 PM Prague. She used the Foundation's calendar system to send the invitation, the only shared calendar they still maintained, and typed the subject line Monthly Personal Check-In before realizing what the words meant. She'd turned their relationship into an agenda item. She'd given their love a subject line. She sent it anyway, because what else could she do? Call unannounced and interrupt whatever perceptual state the Prague archives had produced this week?

She had spent thirty-two years being organized. Organization was how she'd survived the convent. Organization was how she'd built the Foundation. Organization was how she loved Blake Masters, by creating structures that could hold the weight of his absence.

The subject line was devastating. She knew it and sent it anyway.



Saturday morning in Kansas City. February light through the east-facing window of their apartment, the apartment that was technically still theirs, though Blake's side of the closet had thinned to the few things he hadn't packed and the bathroom shelf held his razor and his toothbrush and the archaeological evidence of a man who existed here in theory.

She showered. Dressed. Made coffee in the kitchen where Blake had told her about Europe, at the table where she'd poured a glass of wine and listened to the man she loved explain why he had to leave. The coffee mug was the blue one from the Foundation's opening event. MASTERS FOUNDATION est. 2024 printed in the clean sans-serif she'd chosen because an institution's typography was the first thing serious people noticed.

At 8:45 she sat at the desk in their second bedroom, the one she'd converted to a home office because the Foundation office felt too institutional for Saturday calls and the kitchen table felt too loaded with the memory of the last conversation they'd had at it.

She opened her laptop. Checked her reflection in the dark screen before the video app loaded. Hair down. She'd thought about this, the calculation of appearance that women performed instinctively and men rarely understood. Hair up was Foundation Nadia: competent, structured, holding. Hair down was the Nadia he'd known before the Foundation, before the gift accelerated, before the subject lines replaced the spontaneous calls. She wore it down because she wanted him to see her, not the director.

The call connected.

His face appeared, thinner than last month. He was always thinner. Each call subtracted something from the man she'd memorized: cheekbones more prominent, the dark circles deepened past shadow into permanence, the slight tilt of his head that meant he was listening to something beneath the ambient noise. He was beautiful and diminishing, like a coastline that eroded more elegantly each season.

"Hey," she said.

"Hey."

"You look thin."

"European portions."

"Blake."

He stopped. She watched the automatic reassurances queue behind his eyes, the phrases he'd prepared, the I'm-fine, the Everything's-good, the verbal equivalent of the compressed video signal that showed her his face without the dimension that made it his. She watched him let them pass.

"I'm not fine," he said. "I'm finding extraordinary things and I can't explain them to you and it's making me -" He searched for a word. She waited. She was good at waiting. "Untranslatable."

The word landed in her chest like something small and precise and surgical.

"Untranslatable," she repeated, because she needed to hold it in her mouth to understand its weight.

"What I'm seeing, in the cathedrals, in the archives, in the stone, it doesn't compress into language. Not English, not Czech, not the academic vocabulary I was trained in. The only medium that captures it is the geometry itself. And I can't share geometry through a video call."

"You share it with Andrew."

She said it without anger. Without accusation. The clean incision of observed fact. She'd been thinking about this for weeks, how Blake sent Andrew screenshots and coordinates and correlation coefficients at 3 AM, how their shorthand had developed into a private language that required no translation, how the relationship between Blake and Andrew was becoming the primary creative partnership while the relationship between Blake and Nadia was becoming a monthly calendar event with a subject line.

"Andrew processes the data," Blake said carefully. "He runs the algorithms. He sees the correlations. But he doesn't -"

"Doesn't what?"

"Doesn't feel it."

"Then who does?"

The silence on the call was not lag. It was the silence of a man looking at a question he didn't want to answer.

"Nobody," he said. "Nobody feels it."

Nadia absorbed this. She absorbed it the way she absorbed regulatory filings and board objections, with the composed attention of a woman who processed institutional threats and personal grief through the same architecture.

Nobody feels it. He was alone inside the perception. Andrew processed it. She administered it. But nobody shared it.

She was not enough. The Foundation was not enough. The calls and the emails and the institutional framework she'd built to hold his work were not enough because the work had exceeded the architecture, the way water exceeded a dam when the river rose past the engineer's calculations.

"Tell me about Prague," she said. Because she was a professional and a partner and a woman who had learned in a convent orphanage that when someone you loved was drowning in something you couldn't see, you asked them to describe the water.



He told her about Prague. Not everything, she'd learned to hear the editing, the careful selection of what he shared and what he withheld. He told her dates and documents and institutional contacts: the Klementinum, Eva Černá, Rudolf's inventory. The parts that translated. The parts that fit through the video call's bandwidth.

The parts that didn't translate, the felt experience of the cathedrals, the vertigo of standing where Rudolf had stood, the weight of Miroslav's absence in a city Miroslav had never left, she heard in the silences between his sentences. In the half-second pauses where he searched for a word and settled for a lesser one. In the way his eyes shifted slightly left when he omitted something, the micro-expression she'd cataloged in their second month together and that he didn't know she could read.

She knew him well enough to see the redactions. She loved him well enough to let them pass.

When he finished, she told him about the Foundation. The professional register, the language they shared most fluently, the only channel that still operated at full bandwidth.

"Yuki Tanaka's assessment scores broke the testing scale." She watched his face change, not dramatically, but with the subtle reorganization that occurred when Blake's perception encountered significant information. "The spatial processing metrics Kozlov designed topped out at the ninety-ninth percentile. Tanaka was beyond the scale. Kozlov had to redesign the upper range."

"Beyond the scale?"

"She sees geometry the way you describe seeing it. Not your level, but she described a temple in Kyoto the way you describe Chartres. The same vocabulary. The same emotional response."

She paused. The next item on her mental agenda was harder. Not institutionally, medically.

"Sabrina sent the longitudinal telemetry analysis." Nadia kept her voice level, the administrative register, the one that could carry data without bending under its weight. "Your cardiac and neurological metrics since the first chamber session. Nine months of sustained exposure."

"And?"

"Your resting heart rate has been below fifty BPM for four consecutive months. The bilateral tremor is progressive, it's measurable now in both hands. Your weight is down eleven pounds from baseline." She read these facts as she would read a Foundation compliance report: accurately, without editorial. But her hands were pressed flat on the desk, the anchoring posture. "Sabrina's assessment is that the pattern is consistent with acoustic-induced cardiac remodeling. The sustained sub-threshold vibration exposure is restructuring the cardiac muscle."

"Remodeling."

"The term is specific. It means the heart wall is thickening in response to the vibrational load, the way a weightlifter's heart thickens in response to heavy training. But the vibration isn't periodic, like exercise. It's sustained. And Sabrina's models suggest that if the exposure continues at current intensity, the remodeling becomes irreversible at approximately eighteen months."

The number fell between them like a stone into deep water. Eighteen months. He was at nine. Nine months remaining before the cost of perception was written permanently into the architecture of his heart.

"Irreversible means -"

"It means the cardiac structural changes become self-sustaining. The thickened wall doesn't thin. The resting heart rate doesn't rise. The tremor doesn't resolve." She paused. "It doesn't mean death, Blake. It means permanence. It means a heart that beats differently for the rest of your life."

She watched his face on the screen, the beautiful, diminishing face, and saw him absorb the number the way he absorbed everything: by mapping it, converting the medical data into the architectural framework that was his native processing language. Nine months gone. Nine remaining.

"I'd like to talk to her," he said. "When I get back."

"When you get back," Nadia repeated. She heard the conditional. "Blake, when are you coming back?"

"The Heritage Ministry application -"

"I'm not asking about the Heritage Ministry."

"I know." He rubbed his eyes. The wifi stuttered. "The application will take months. I'll need to stay in Prague to maintain the institutional contacts."

"How many months?"

"I don't know."

"Guess."

"Three. Maybe four."

Nadia absorbed the number. Three to four months. Not eight weeks. Three to four months on top of the weeks already elapsed, which meant half a year at minimum, because three became four and four became six and Blake's sense of time had become geologic, measured in centuries of manuscript transmission rather than weeks of human relationship.

"That's not eight weeks," she said.

"I know."

"You said eight weeks. You said maximum."

"Things have changed."

"Things always change with you, Blake. That's the whole problem." She heard herself and held herself and continued because the words had been accumulating for weeks. "Things change and change and change and each change is significant and each change adds weeks and each time you explain why and each time the explanation is real and I can't argue with it because you're right, you're always right about the research, but -"

She stopped. Put her hand against her mouth. Held. The convent had taught her this: that composure was the architecture around emotion, the wall that held the water, the dam that kept the river inside its banks. She held.

"But you're not here," she finished.

"I know," he said. For the third time.

The knowing and the being-here were different things, and the distance between them was the distance between two people who loved each other across a gap that geography made visible but perception had caused.



"Blake, there's something else." She dropped the professional register. What was underneath was not vulnerability. It was certainty, the hard, structural certainty of a woman who had been handling a crisis alone for four weeks because the man she loved was in a Prague pension reading medieval manuscripts.

"Someone filed a regulatory complaint against the Foundation."

His face changed. "What kind of complaint?"

"State Board of Higher Education. Anonymous filing. Claims our cognitive assessments constitute unlicensed psychological testing." She paused. "It's been four weeks. I've been handling it."

"Four weeks?" His voice sharpened. "You've been dealing with a regulatory threat for a month and you didn't tell me?"

"You were in a conservation lab examining thread stubs." She heard the blade in her own voice and did not withdraw it. "What would you have done from Uppsala? Called the Missouri Board of Education and explained that our cognitive assessments are based on thirteenth-century manuscripts?"

"I would have -"

"You would have worried. You would have analyzed the institutional threat patterns. You would have mapped the regulatory complaint against the faction architecture and concluded that either the Custodians or the Completion Sect were using bureaucratic channels to obstruct the Foundation. And you would have been right." She let the sentence breathe. "But being right doesn't file the compliance documentation or attend the board review hearing."

Silence.

"Is the Foundation at risk?" he asked.

"I handled it. Kozlov's credentials satisfied the board's concerns. But Blake, whoever filed this knew our assessment schedule. They knew the cohort was arriving. The timing was precision-targeted."

"The Custodians monitor institutional channels. They've done it for centuries."

"I don't need a historical analysis. I need you to understand that while you're chasing the knowledge, someone is trying to dismantle the infrastructure that will deliver it."

She watched his face on the screen and continued, because this was the thing she'd been organizing in her mind for four weeks, the thing that had no subject line and no calendar slot.

"The manuscripts are extraordinary. But if the Foundation doesn't survive its first year, the manuscripts are beautiful objects in a Swedish library and a Prague archive, and nothing else."

The silence between them was an ocean. Not the Atlantic, something deeper. The silence that occurs between two people who are both right and whose rightness is incompatible.

"I'll come home," Blake said.

"No." The word was immediate and absolute. "You're too close to the synthesis. The eight pages, the mirror chambers, the resonance architecture, if you stop now, you'll lose the thread. And the Foundation needs what you're finding more than it needs you sitting in the office disagreeing with board administrators."

"Then what do you need?"

"I need you to acknowledge that the work I'm doing is as dangerous as the work you're doing. Different danger. Institutional rather than physical. But the Custodians are fighting us on both fronts, and only one of us is paying attention to their bureaucratic weapons."

"I acknowledge it," he said.

She heard the sincerity. She also heard its limits. Acknowledgment was not the same as presence. A man who said I see your work from six thousand kilometers away was a man who saw a compressed, pixelated version of it.

She acknowledged his acknowledgment. She let it be enough because it had to be enough.

"Find your pages," she said.

And ended the call.



The apartment was very quiet.

Nadia closed the laptop. Sat with her hands flat on the desk, the posture she used when her body needed an anchor. Through the window, Kansas City's February light fell on the buildings of Troost Avenue with the indifferent clarity that her city produced in winter, sharp, honest light that showed surfaces as they were without the kindness of cloud-filtered diffusion.

Three to four months. The number sat in her like a stone.

She stood up. Went to the kitchen. Poured a glass of water, not wine, not today, because wine was a form of marking time and she didn't want to give it the ceremonial weight of fermentation and glass. Water was sufficient. Water was what survived.

She stood at the kitchen window and thought about the convent. About Sister Maria Theresa, who had explained to a seven-year-old orphan that loving someone who leaves is a discipline. That the women who hold things hold them because they understand that the alternative, letting go, would mean the weight falls on the ground and breaks.

She held.

She drank the water. Rinsed the glass. Set it upside down in the rack.

Then she opened her laptop and began drafting the Foundation's quarterly report to the NSF, because the institutional architecture she'd built was still standing and still needed her and would need her tomorrow and next week and for the three to four months that the man she loved would spend in Prague, finding pages she couldn't read in a language she couldn't hear.

The light moved across the desk. Blake's empty chair across the room collected shadow. Nadia wrote the report.
