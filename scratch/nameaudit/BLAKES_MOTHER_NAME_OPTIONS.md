## 0. Headline findings

1. **There is a third Margaret.** The audit lists two. Book 2 also contains
   **Senator Margaret Holt**, Chair of the Commerce Subcommittee on Science,
   Oceans, Fisheries, and Weather — seventy-one, Methodist, former prosecutor.
   She is a *third* woman named Margaret, and she is in the **same volume** as
   Blake's mother. Renaming Blake's mother clears that too, which is a bonus.

2. **The two principal Margarets never share a volume**, so there is no
   genuinely ambiguous mention. Blake's mother is Books 1–2; Margaret Chen is
   Book 3 only. The clash is a *reader-memory* clash across volumes, not a
   scene-level one.

3. **Her name is spoken aloud only three times, and never by Blake.** All three
   are in Book 1, all in Prague, all by people who have never met her —
   Miroslav twice, Nadia once. Blake calls her "Mom." This matters for name
   selection: her given name functions almost entirely as a *reported* name, a
   name in William's letters, said with reverence by strangers.

4. **She has no `CANON.md` entry at all.** "Margaret" does not appear in
   `CANON.md`. Whatever name is chosen, she should probably be added.

## 1. Character portrait

### Hard facts

| Fact | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Age | 63 | B1 1920, "Margaret Masters, sixty-three" |
| Implied birth year | **~1960–61** | See dating note below |
| Home | Red-brick ranch, **Fifty-Third Street, Kansas City, Missouri** | B1 3945–3946 |
| Husband | **James Masters**, d. September 2010, single-car accident, Oscar-01 | `CANON.md` L64; B1 |
| Father-in-law | **William Masters**, d. 2003 | `CANON.md` L63 |
| Children | Blake, plus at least one deceased child (Ethan, 31, is her **grandson** and Blake's nephew) | B1 1939–1941, 2030 |
| Ethnicity | **Not stated.** Only signal: Blake has "your mother's **coloring**," distinct enough that a Czech stranger reads it off his face | B1 3004–3005 |
| Widowed | ~14 years at story present | B1 3966 |
| Book 1 endgame | Working with the Foundation's Kansas City operation, alongside Ethan | B1 4337–4339 |

**Dating note.** She says "**Fourteen years** of practice held her voice steady"
and "I've known since the week after the funeral." James died September 2010, so
Book 1's present is **~2024**, making her birth year **~1960–61** — a year or two
earlier than the 1962–63 in the brief. Either way: a girl named in **1960 or 1961**,
in Missouri. That is the naming target.

### What she has endured

She is not a grieving bystander. She is a woman who made a **transaction** and
honoured it for fourteen years:

> "They said accidents happen. And they made sure I understood what kind."
> Margaret's eyes were dry. "You were twelve. Your father was dead. And the
> people who killed him made it clear that silence was the price of your
> survival. **So I paid it.**" — B1 3973–3976

She has buried a father-in-law (2003), a husband (2010, murdered), and a child
(Ethan's father — "I'm tired of going to **Masters funerals**," B1 2025). Ethan is
described as the one who "answered the phone calls and identified the bodies and
**held their grandmother together**" (B1 2028–2030).

And she frames the silence as **inherited and gendered**:

> "Silence runs in this family the way your patterns do. Passed down. **The
> women just carry it differently.**" — B1 3969–3970

### She is an intellect, not just a keeper

The Foundation's twelve-year assessment programme still runs on a term **she
coined**:

> "The profile that **Margaret Masters** had described, in her private
> correspondence with William, as **'the perceptual fingerprint.'**" — B2 3561–3562

She corresponded privately with her father-in-law about the family's perceptual
gift and named its diagnostic signature. That naming survives her into Book 2's
laboratory protocols. She is an unacknowledged theorist of the thing the whole
trilogy is about.

Miroslav, quoting William's letters:

> "The woman who **kept him human** when the patterns tried to take him
> somewhere else." — B1 3007–3009

### Voice and manner

- **Diagnostic, not consoling.** Blake rehearses a careful three-minute
  progression; she raises a hand and cuts him off: *"I know, Blake."* (B1 3960–3962)
- **Short declaratives. No sentiment.** *"He knew something was wrong."* /
  *"So I paid it."* / *"You'll be reckless. Just like your father."*
- **One aphorism, and it is bleak:** *"Love does not shield people from
  consequences. It just makes the consequences hurt more."* (B1 4039–4040)
- **Observational to the point of interrogation.** Her opening line in the book
  is a diagnosis: *"You're not fine. You're wearing the watch."* (B1 1926)
- **Physically understated, actually strong:** "Her grip was stronger than it
  looked, **sixty-three years of holding things together**." (B1 2034–2035)
  "Her eyes were **fierce**." (B1 2039)

### Sensory signature (the texture a name has to sit inside)

Apron dusted with flour. A house that smells of **bread and lavender** — "the
smell that meant safety." Serves **tea, not coffee**, when she wants a real
conversation, and does it twice, in both of her scenes, as a tell. Three
weathered **beehives** at the property line — the hives where James taught Blake
that hexagons are a language, the origin scene of the trilogy's whole premise.
Live oaks James planted as saplings. A cracked basketball backboard. And she hid
William's complete documentation — the USB with SEVEN SITES on it — **behind her
sewing supplies**, where the Watchers never looked because they were looking for
James's work, not William's.

### One-paragraph portrait

A Missouri-born woman of about sixty-three, matriarch of a family that has buried
three of its own, who learned the week after her husband's funeral that he was
murdered and then said nothing for fourteen years because the people who killed
him made her son's survival the price of her silence — and who describes that not
as trauma but as an invoice she paid. She is no bystander to the family gift: she
corresponded privately with her father-in-law about it and gave its diagnostic
signature the name the Foundation still uses. She keeps bees, bakes bread, serves
tea instead of coffee when she intends to be told the truth, hid the most
dangerous documents in the trilogy behind her sewing supplies, and speaks in short
flat declaratives that diagnose rather than comfort. William's letters called her
"the woman who kept him human." Her grip is stronger than it looks.

### Ambiguity check

**No genuinely ambiguous mention exists between Blake's mother and Margaret Chen.**
They never appear in the same volume. Every Book 3 bare "Margaret" (4 of the 10)
sits inside a board-meeting scene where "Margaret Chen" has already been named on
the page.

The one *same-volume* collision is **Book 2: Margaret Masters (line 3561) and
Senator Margaret Holt (line 4894)**, ~1,300 lines apart. Both are always
surnamed, so neither is ambiguous in isolation — but two Margarets in one volume
is exactly the kind of low-grade friction that produced this audit. Renaming
Blake's mother resolves it.

## 3. Candidate names — screened

Every candidate below was run through `scratch/nameaudit/screen_mother.py`, which
checks four things:

1. **Collision** against a 114-name roster of every personal name in the trilogy.
2. **Near-miss** — any roster name within 0.68 `SequenceMatcher` similarity.
3. **Prose presence** — case-sensitive whole-word count across all three books
   (must be 0).
4. **Euphony against `Masters`** — M-alliteration, initial-echo with Blake /
   William / James, sibilant tail running into the S of Masters.

The roster was **extended beyond the original `screen.py`** with the ten
*incoming* names from the 2026-08-29 ruling — Ferrand, Ashworth, Lindgren,
Rosalind, Whitaker, Okada, Zhao, Halloran, Nolan, Eriksen — because a candidate
that collides with a name about to enter the book is just as broken as one that
collides with a name already in it. (This is why **Rosalind** is excluded: it is
now the Book 2 Stanford neuroscientist.)

### Group A — Classic-dignified
*Formal register. Reads well on a Foundation letterhead and in Miroslav's mouth.*

| Name | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Vivian** | Latin *vivus*, "living." William's letters called her "the woman who **kept him human** when the patterns tried to take him somewhere else" — a name meaning *alive* on the woman who kept a man alive to himself. Peaked in the 1920s–30s and stayed current enough to be unremarkable on a 1961 birth certificate. "Vivian Masters" is the best-sounding full name on this list: open V, clean landing on Masters, no shared consonants. |
| **Frances** | The plainest kind of mid-century Midwestern competence — a name that promises nothing and delivers. Suits a woman whose defining act was fourteen years of doing nothing visible. *Caution:* the -es tail runs into the M-s of Masters; "Frances Masters" has a slight hiss. Also carries a Frances/Francis gender ambiguity in print. |
| **Constance** | Literally *constancy, steadfastness*. The text already uses the word: Ethan has "his **grandmother's steadiness**." This is the most exact one-word description of her in the trilogy, and it happens to be a name. *Caution:* three S-sounds in "Constance Masters." Say it aloud before committing. |
| **Genevieve** | Germanic, roughly "tribe-woman" — the woman who *is* the family. Elegant and unmistakably dignified. Slightly ornate for a 1961 Missouri birth, and four syllables plus Masters is long. Held in reserve. |

### Group B — Warm-earthy
*The kitchen, the beehives, the bread and lavender.*

| Name | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Rosemary** | An herb name for a woman whose house is a sensory signature — bread and lavender, tea instead of coffee — and, by the Ophelia association, the name means **remembrance**. She is the family's memory vault; she hid the trilogy's most dangerous documents behind her sewing supplies. Kitchen name and memory name at once. Fully 1961 Missouri. Long but it scans. |
| **Lorraine** | 1940s–60s Midwestern, warm, with a faint built-in melancholy — a name that already sounds like someone who has lost people. "Lorraine Masters" flows cleanly. The least "meaningful" name on this list and possibly the most naturalistic. |
| **Ruth** | Biblical Ruth is the archetype of loyalty that survives bereavement. A monosyllable against two-syllable Masters gives "Ruth Masters" a hard, plain, unsentimental rhythm that matches her short declaratives exactly. Risk: possibly *too* plain — it may not carry the gravitas the brief asks for. |
| **Georgia** | *Earth-worker.* The trilogy's own line is "Someone has to hold the ground while you chase the sky" — and she is the one who held the ground for fourteen years. Warm, unfussy, and Missouri is border-South enough that it doesn't read as misplaced. |
| **Hazel** | Warm, grandmotherly, and phonetically clean against Masters. *Flag:* Hazel is currently a top-30 US baby name, so a modern reader may hear it as a toddler's name rather than a sixty-three-year-old's. This is the one candidate that breaks the "not contemporary-trendy" screen on the *reader's ear* even though it is period-accurate for 1961. Include only if that doesn't bother you. |

### Group C — Quietly formidable
*Cool-steel. Dignity with an edge.*

| Name | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Regina** | Latin for **queen**. Your own words were "a **queen bee** that has been through hell" — and she keeps **three beehives** in the backyard, the hives where James taught Blake that hexagons are a language. The literal meaning lands on the literal object, in the scene that starts the whole trilogy. Solidly 1961 (peaked mid-century, strong in the Catholic Midwest), and it reads as dignified rather than cute because nobody consciously translates it. |
| **Vera** | *True / truth.* She held the truth for fourteen years and refused to say it: "silence was the price of your survival. So I paid it." Her own line about James is "in the end, **the truth was louder**." A woman named Truth who chose silence is the strongest ironic charge available here. Crisp two syllables into Masters. *Flag:* Vera reads faintly Slavic, and with Nadia Volkov and Lviv already in the book it may blur an ethnic signal — though it was a common enough American name mid-century that this is a soft risk. |
| **Naomi** | In the Book of Ruth, Naomi is the widow who buries her husband and both her sons — an almost exact match for a woman who has buried a husband and a child and attends "Masters funerals." *Flag:* grieving Naomi renames herself **Mara** — and **Mara is already a character in the trilogy**. The resonance and the collision are the same fact. Screens clean (Naomi/Mara are not near-misses), but a reader who knows the text will notice. |
| **Claudia** | Roman gravitas, unfussy, cool-toned; "Claudia Masters" is a strong full name. Held in reserve — the etymology ("lame") offers nothing, so this is a sound-and-register pick only. |
| **Roberta** | *Bright fame*, and a genuinely period-correct 1961 Missouri name. Solid and matronly. Held in reserve: slightly dowdy, and the -a/Ma- junction in "Roberta Masters" is a touch soft. |

## 4. Top three

### 1. **Regina Masters**
It is the only candidate that answers your brief in its own words. You said
"queen bee"; *regina* is queen, and the bees are not a metaphor in this book —
they are three weathered white boxes at the property line, and the hive is where
James delivered the line that governs the whole trilogy ("Pattern as language").
The name puts a crown on the woman who kept the hive together while the men flew
off after the patterns and died. It is period-accurate, it is not precious, and
it sounds authoritative in Miroslav's mouth: *"Regina, yes?"* The only risk is
that it is on-the-nose — but it is on-the-nose in a way no reader will articulate,
which is the right kind.

### 2. **Vera Masters**
The sharpest irony available. Her defining act is the withholding of the truth,
and her own summary of her husband is "in the end, the truth was louder." Naming
her Truth turns every one of her flat declaratives into a small indictment.
Mechanically it is also the cleanest: two syllables, hard V, no consonant
collision with Masters, and it survives being said aloud in accented English —
which is the only way her name is ever spoken. Take the Slavic-adjacency flag
seriously but do not overweight it.

### 3. **Vivian Masters**
The most beautiful of the three as a full name, and the one that honours her
rather than characterising her. *Living* — "the woman who kept him human." Where
Regina crowns her and Vera indicts her, Vivian simply says what she was for
William and then for James and then for Blake: the thing that kept them
attached to the world. If you want warmth and dignity without irony, this is it.

**Runners-up worth a second look:** **Constance**, if you want the book's own
word for her ("steadiness") made literal — check the sibilance aloud first. And
**Rosemary**, if you want the name to live in the bread-and-lavender-and-sewing-
supplies texture rather than in the theme.
