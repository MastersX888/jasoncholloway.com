Chapter Eight. The Key.

One hundred nine hertz. Kansas City.

[short pause]

Blake's mother answered the door in an apron dusted with flour.

Margaret Masters, sixty-three. Her face did the thing it always did. Joy at seeing her son compressed by the worry that accompanied every unexpected visit. Blake hadn't called ahead. He should have called ahead.

"Blake." Her eyes cataloged the bruise on his forehead, the butterfly bandages. "What happened?"

"I fell in the library. I'm fine."

"You're not fine. You're wearing the watch."

Blake looked at his wrist. The Breitling. He'd been wearing it since Jefferson City without thinking about it.

"Come in," Margaret said. "Ethan's here."

The house smelled like bread and lavender. His mother's house always smelled like bread and lavender. The same olfactory signature Blake had carried in his memory since childhood, the smell that meant safety.

Ethan was in the kitchen. Blake's nephew, thirty-one, practical, the kind of person who fixed things with his hands while Blake fixed them with his mind. Ethan had his father's build and his grandmother's steadiness.

"Hey." Ethan looked up from the Sports section. Took in Blake's face. "Rough week?"

"You could say that."

Margaret set tea on the table. Not coffee. Tea. Blake noticed the choice. His mother had always served tea when she wanted a real conversation.

"Mom," Blake said. "I need to ask you about Dad."

Margaret's hands went still on the teapot. Ethan set down the newspaper.

"What about him?" Margaret's voice was careful.

"The accident. The things he was asking about before he died. Oscar-01."

The kitchen went quiet. Outside, a cardinal landed on the feeder. Red against October gray. Blake tracked the bird's angular trajectory automatically.

Through the kitchen window he could see the backyard. Smaller than he remembered, but the beehives were still there. Three white boxes at the edge of the property line, weathered by fifteen years of Missouri seasons.

The remembrance came without warning. Nine years old, standing next to the hives in a white suit too big for him. James beside him, lifting the top cover.

"Watch how they build, Blake." Thousands of bees across the comb. "Perfect hexagons. Every single time."

"Why hexagons?"

"Math. Hexagons use less wax than squares. Hold more honey. Stronger structure. The bees figured this out millions of years before humans invented geometry."

James pointed at the cells. Tilted slightly upward. "Thirteen-degree angle. Same in every hive, everywhere in the world. They don't measure it. They just know."

"They're talking with math?"

"They're talking with geometry. Pattern as language." James knelt down. Lifted the veil so they were face to face. "If you see patterns that scare you. Don't try to figure it out alone. Working alone is how people get lost. Remember that."

Blake surfaced from the memory. The bees were gone. The hives empty now, paint peeling. James was gone. The mathematics remained.

"Your father was a good man," Margaret said. "But he couldn't leave things alone."

"Neither can I."

Margaret studied him. Then she stood, walked to the back bedroom, and returned with a wooden box Blake had never seen. Carved walnut, simple, the kind of work his father had done in the garage on weekends.

"James left this. In his desk at work. They sent it home with his things."

Inside: a key. Different from William's. Smaller, modern, a Post Office box key. And a note in James's handwriting:

Don't go alone.

Three words. The advice of a man who'd gone alone and paid for it.

"He knew the accident was coming," Blake said.

"He knew something was wrong." Margaret's eyes were wet. "He called me the night before. Said if anything happened, to hide this. Give it to you when you were ready."

"When I was ready."

"He said you'd know when."

Blake looked at the key. At his mother. At Ethan, who was watching with an expression that mixed confusion and the beginning of fear.

"Blake," Ethan said. "Whatever Dad was into. Whatever you're into. You need to be careful."

"I am being careful."

"You fell in a library and you're wearing Grandpa's watch and you look like you haven't slept in three days." Ethan's pragmatism, slicing through ambiguity. "That's not careful."

"I'm not working alone this time. I have people."

"People." Ethan's voice was tight. "I'm tired of going to Masters funerals, Blake."

The words sat between them. Blake heard what his nephew meant, not anger, not even fear. Exhaustion. The exhaustion of the family member who stayed behind and answered the phone calls and identified the bodies and held their grandmother together.

"So am I," Blake said. "That's why I'm doing this differently."

Ethan studied him for a long moment. Then, softer: "Be careful. Please."

Margaret put her hand over Blake's. Her grip was stronger than it looked, sixty-three years of holding things together.

"Your father loved you," she said. "William loved you. Whatever they were trying to protect you from, they were trying to protect you."

"I know, Mom."

"Then don't do what they did." Her eyes were fierce. "Don't disappear into it. Don't become so obsessed that you forget to call your mother. Don't -" Her voice broke. "Don't die on a straight road in perfect weather."

Blake placed his arm around her. Held her the way she'd held him when he was small and afraid and the world was too vast and too bright.

"I won't," he said.

And meant it. And hoped it was true.

[pause]

On the drive home, Blake thought about his father's key.

Don't go alone.

He pulled over. Reached for his phone. Called Nadia.

She answered on two rings. "Blake."

"My mother had a key. From my father."

A pause. Then: "What kind of key?"

"Post Office box. And a note. Three words: Don't go alone."

Silence. Then Nadia's voice, quiet and sure: "Then don't."

Blake looked at the road ahead. The same road James had driven a thousand times. Below, the same October light. The same Missouri field.

"I'm heading back now," he said. "Meet me at Genessee Street?"

"I'll be there."

He hung up. Drove home through a world that was singing in frequencies his father had heard and his grandfather had documented and Blake was only beginning to understand.

But not alone.

Not anymore.
