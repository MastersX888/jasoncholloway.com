Chapter Four. Peter's Question.

Three point nine one five hertz. Kansas City.


Andrew Chen entered the Foundation lobby and stopped walking.

He was thinner than the last time Nadia had seen him, thinner in the way that people who live in Iceland for seven years become thin, the body pared down to function by a landscape that does not reward excess. His eyes had the particular distance of a man who has spent those years listening to seven point eight three hertz through headphones and monitoring screens and the thin basalt walls of a stone cottage, the distance not of detachment but of calibration, the look of someone whose instruments are set to frequencies most people don't perceive.

He stood in the lobby and looked at the walls. The signage was different. The monitoring suite on the second floor had been converted to a teacher training room, he could tell from the directory board by the elevator, which now listed "Instruction" where it used to list "Research Wing B." The reception desk had a stack of printed curricula where the visitor logs used to be.

"You changed the building," he said.

"I changed the mission," Nadia said, from behind him. She was standing in the doorway to her office, coffee in hand, wearing the expression she wore when institutional architecture was being questioned, which was the expression she wore most of the time, because institutional architecture was always being questioned, usually by people who had never built anything they had to defend.

"Same thing," Andrew said. And it was.



He observed Blake's classroom session the next morning. Room 204, Harris Hall. The thirteen chairs. The three-minute silence that had become fifteen minutes, the twelve students who breathed and perceived and described what they perceived with the tentative precision of people who have been given vocabulary for experiences that used to be private.

After the session, the students filed out. Andrew stood by the whiteboard. Blake's cathedral-torso diagram still drawn in dry-erase marker, the proportions annotated with measurements. He studied it the way he studied data: carefully, without judgment, with the willingness to be convinced that precedes the demand for proof.

"The students aren't producing measurable results above baseline unless you're running EEG during the sessions," Andrew said. "Marcus's quarterly data is suggestive, but it's quarterly. We need continuous monitoring. We need controlled conditions."

Blake was stacking the chairs. He stacked chairs the way he did everything now, slowly, with intention, each chair placed with the care of a man whose trembling hands require him to plan every movement or risk dropping things. The tremor at one eleven point two hertz making each stack a small negotiation between will and vibration.

"The data is their experience, Andrew."

"Experience without measurement isn't science."

"And measurement without experience isn't knowledge."

They stood on opposite sides of the room. The fluorescent lights hummed. The HVAC cycled. The building produced its composite frequency, the fifty-two hertz background that Blake could identify by ear and that Andrew could identify by spectrometer and that neither of them had ever asked Nadia whether she could identify at all.



Nadia interrupted. Not from the doorway, she walked into the center of the room, between them, into the space where the thirteen chairs had been arranged. She did not position herself as mediator. Mediators stand between parties. Nadia stood in front of Andrew and spoke to him directly, the way she spoke to board members and senators and anyone else who needed to hear what she had to say without the softening that conversation typically applies to difficult truths.

"Andrew. When you monitored Blake's heart rate in the eighth chamber and it dropped to forty-four, who decided that was safe enough to continue?"

"I did."

"Based on what data?"

The silence was architectural. It had load-bearing walls. Andrew stared at her, and in the stare was the recognition, slow, structural, the recognition of a man whose entire professional identity was built on measurement, that his most important decision in seven years had not been data-driven. It had been relational. Seven years of knowing Blake. Seven years of learning the difference between dangerous-quiet and safe-quiet. Seven years of sitting three meters from a man's body and developing the ability to know, without instruments, whether the man inside the body was coming back.

He didn't answer. The answer was visible in the silence.

"That's the data, Andrew. The relationship IS the data. Seven years of learning to trust what you couldn't measure. You made the most important clinical decision in the Foundation's history based on something you could not quantify, and it was the right decision, and you cannot replicate it in a controlled study because the variable was love."

Andrew opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"I didn't..."

"You did. And there's a text for it." Her voice was steady, the voice she had used before the Senate, before the board, the voice that was not cold but precise, the distinction that people who knew Nadia understood and people who didn't know her misread as severity.

"Mary told Peter she had seen the risen Lord. Peter's response was: 'Did he prefer her to us?' Not 'What did you see?' Not 'Describe what happened.' His question was about authority. About hierarchy. About who had the right to perceive."

She looked at Andrew directly. I am not flinching, her posture said. You should not flinch either.

"You've monitored Blake's heart rate for seven years. His EEG. His tremor. His cortical coherence. His cardiac rhythm. Every frequency his body has produced. You have measured the man to the fourth decimal place." She paused. "You have never measured me. In seven years, you have never once attached an electrode to my scalp. You have never asked what I perceive."

Andrew's face, the face that opened slowly, tectonically, the face that revealed its architecture over minutes rather than seconds, opened.

"Not because you're unkind. Not because you don't respect me. Because it never occurred to you that the person who organizes the spreadsheet might also be hearing the frequency. Peter's question wasn't about Mary's evidence. It was about his own assumption that the teaching came through the person who stood in front of the group, not the person who held the group together."

The room was still. Blake was standing in the doorway, he had been stacking chairs, but he had stopped, and now he stood in the doorframe the way Andrew had once stood in the basalt doorframe in Iceland, a man on the threshold of a conversation he would not enter but could not leave. Andrew reached for his laptop. It was on the desk by the window, the desk that was Marcus's, technically, but that Andrew had commandeered the way Andrew commandeered flat surfaces, by placing equipment on them and looking at you as though the equipment had been there all along.

He opened a new file. Typed the filename with the deliberate keystrokes of a man who was performing an act of correction:

`nadiavolkovsession_001.eeg`

"Tell me what you hear."

Nadia sat in one of the thirteen chairs. The chair that had been Blake's, the thirteenth, the teacher's chair, the one at the head of the circle that was not at the head because circles don't have heads but that was positioned, by habit, near the whiteboard.

Andrew attached the electrodes. His hands, the hands that had attached these sensors to Blake's scalp hundreds of times, that knew the placement grid by muscle memory, that could find F3 and F4 and Cz in the dark, worked with the same precision they always had. But something in the gesture was different. The difference was in the intention: he was not monitoring a subject. He was listening to a colleague.

Blake stayed in the doorway. His hands in his pockets, hiding the tremor. His eyes on Nadia. On the electrodes in her hair. On the screen where her brainwaves were beginning to appear, the slow, steady oscillation of a mind at rest, a mind that was always at rest even when it was at work, because Nadia's rest and Nadia's work were the same frequency.

"I hear the building," Nadia said.

"Describe it."

"The Foundation resonates at approximately sixty hertz baseline, the electrical system. But over that, there's a composite. It shifts depending on who's in the building and what they're doing. When Marcus teaches, the composite rises to around ninety. When the board meets and the meeting is productive, it climbs to a hundred and five. When the meeting is contentious, it drops to seventy. I can feel the difference the way Blake feels the difference between harmonics. I have felt it for ten years."

Andrew looked at the EEG. His face did the full thing now, the complete architectural reveal, the surface dissolving to show the structure beneath.

Nadia's cortical coherence, during this description, was 0.87. The number sat on the screen the way all significant numbers sit on screens, innocuously, in digital typeface, without the trumpet fanfares or thunderclaps that important data deserves but never receives.

0.87. Higher than any cohort candidate except Yuki Tanaka. Higher than Blake's resting baseline. Higher than anyone Andrew had measured in seven years of continuous monitoring across hundreds of sessions in four countries.

He saved the file. Looked at Nadia.

"You've been running the Foundation at 0.87 coherence for ten years."

"I've been running the Foundation. The coherence was not the point."

"It was always the point." His voice was quiet, the quiet of a man who has just discovered that the most important data in his seven-year career was sitting at the desk next to him the entire time, running the organization, signing the grants, making the coffee, and never once asking to be measured because she believed the measuring was someone else's job. "We just didn't have the electrode on the right person."

Nadia looked at the screen. Her own brainwaves, a plateau, not the dramatic peaks and valleys of Blake's visionary perception. A continuous, organized, architecturally stable frequency. The neurological signature of a building that had been standing for a long time and intended to keep standing.

She closed her eyes. Opened them.

"Then put the electrode on the right person," she said. "And ask me what I perceive."
